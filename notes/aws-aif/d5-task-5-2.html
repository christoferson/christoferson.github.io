Overview

Task 5.2

Recognize governance and compliance regulations for AI systems.

Governance is how an organization directs and controls its use of AI: who decides, what rules apply, how risk is assessed and how use is monitored. Compliance is meeting the external obligations (laws, regulations, industry standards, contracts) and internal policies that apply, and being able to prove it. AI raises new obligations, such as AI-specific laws, model documentation and transparency requirements, on top of existing data protection and industry rules. The exam expects you to recognize which AWS service supports which governance need, and the main data governance and process practices. The running example is an insurance company rolling out several generative AI applications across its business.

AWS services for governance and compliance

Skill 5.2.1Identify AWS services and features to assist with governance and regulation compliance (for example, AWS Config, Amazon Inspector, AWS Artifact, AWS CloudTrail, AWS Trusted Advisor)

Each service answers a different governance question. Remembering the question is the quickest way to pick the right one.

AWS Config: "Are our resources configured the way our rules require, and how have they changed?"

Continuously records the configuration of AWS resources and its history, and evaluates it against Config rules, flagging non-compliant resources. AWS provides managed rules, and you can write custom ones; conformance packs bundle rules for a standard or policy. For AI workloads, rules can check that S3 buckets holding training data are encrypted and not public, that SageMaker AI notebook instances have no direct internet access, that SageMaker endpoint configurations use KMS encryption, and that resources carry required ownership tags. Config can trigger automatic remediation, and its history shows what a resource looked like on any date, which auditors need.

Amazon Inspector: "Do our workloads have known software vulnerabilities?"

Automatically and continuously scans Amazon EC2 instances, container images in Amazon ECR and AWS Lambda functions for known software vulnerabilities (CVEs) and unintended network exposure, and prioritizes findings by risk. For AI, it covers the servers, containers and functions that host models, run inference code, process data or power agents' tools. Regular vulnerability scanning is a requirement in many compliance standards.

AWS Artifact: "Where are AWS's compliance reports and agreements?"

A self-service portal for AWS's security and compliance documents: third-party audit reports and certifications (SOC 1, 2 and 3 reports, ISO certificates including ISO/IEC 42001 for AI management systems, PCI DSS attestations and others), and agreements you can review and accept, such as a Business Associate Addendum (BAA) needed for health data under HIPAA. Customers give these to their auditors and regulators as evidence that the AWS part of the shared responsibility model is controlled. Artifact proves AWS's compliance, not yours.

AWS CloudTrail: "Who did what, when, from where?"

Records API activity in AWS accounts: every call to create, change, use or delete resources, including invoking Bedrock models, changing guardrails, creating knowledge bases, starting SageMaker AI training jobs and updating agents, with the identity, time, source and parameters. It provides the audit trail for investigations and compliance, can be centralized across accounts, and protected against tampering with log file integrity validation. For the content of prompts and responses, Bedrock model invocation logging complements it.

AWS Trusted Advisor: "Are we following AWS best practices?"

Inspects your AWS environment and gives recommendations across cost optimization, performance, security, fault tolerance, service limits (quotas) and operational excellence: for example, S3 buckets with open access, IAM users without MFA, security groups allowing unrestricted access, idle resources wasting money, and services approaching their quotas. It is a quick, ongoing health check of whether the environment that hosts AI follows good practice.

Related services Extra
  • AWS Well-Architected Tool: reviews workloads against the Well-Architected Framework, including the Machine Learning, Generative AI and Responsible AI lenses.
  • Amazon CloudWatch: monitoring, logs and alarms for AI applications, including Bedrock and AgentCore metrics.
  • AWS Organizations service control policies: organization-wide limits, such as allowing only approved models or Regions.

"Continuously check resources against compliance rules" is AWS Config. "Scan for software vulnerabilities" is Amazon Inspector. "Download AWS's SOC or ISO reports, or sign a BAA" is AWS Artifact. "Audit who invoked a model or changed a setting" is AWS CloudTrail. "Best-practice recommendations on cost, security and limits" is AWS Trusted Advisor.

Data governance strategies

Skill 5.2.2Describe data governance strategies (for example, data lifecycles, logging, residency, monitoring, observation, retention)

Data governance decides how data is managed from creation to deletion: who owns it, who may use it for what, where it is kept, for how long, and how its use is watched. AI multiplies the places data goes (training sets, embeddings, prompts, outputs, logs, agent memory), and each needs a governance decision.

Data lifecycles

Managing data through each stage: creation or collection, storage, use (training, retrieval, inference), sharing, archiving and deletion. For each AI data type, define what happens at each stage. Training data sets are versioned and archived with the models trained on them; knowledge base documents are refreshed when sources change and removed when withdrawn; conversation logs move to cheaper archive storage after 90 days and are deleted after their retention period. Amazon S3 Lifecycle rules automate moving data to archive storage classes such as S3 Glacier and deleting it on schedule.

Lifecycle planning must include data that's hard to delete: if a customer exercises a right to erasure, where does their data live? In source systems, training sets, vector stores, logs and possibly in a fine-tuned model's weights, which is one reason to keep personal data out of training.

Logging

Recording activity and data use: API calls (AWS CloudTrail), model prompts and responses (Bedrock model invocation logging to S3 or CloudWatch Logs), data access (S3 server access logs, CloudTrail data events), guardrail interventions and agent traces. Governance decides what must be logged for compliance and investigation, what must not be logged (or must be masked) because it is sensitive, who can read logs, and how they are protected from tampering.

Residency

Requirements that data is stored and processed in specific countries or regions, from laws (such as GDPR's rules on transfers outside the EU, or national data localization laws), sector rules or contracts. On AWS, you choose the Regions where data is stored and processed, and data doesn't leave a Region unless you move it. For generative AI, check that the model is available in the required Region, and that any cross-Region inference uses a geographic profile that keeps processing within the required area (for example, an EU profile) rather than a global one. Service control policies can block use of other Regions.

Monitoring

Continuously checking data and its use against expectations: data quality metrics and drift in inputs, unusual access patterns, sensitive data appearing where it shouldn't (Amazon Macie on S3), configuration compliance (AWS Config), model quality and guardrail metrics (CloudWatch). Monitoring triggers alerts and action when something breaks policy.

Observation

Observability: being able to see inside an AI system's behavior in enough detail to understand what happened and why: end-to-end traces of each request through retrieval, model calls, guardrails and tool use (AgentCore Observability, OpenTelemetry traces), dashboards, and the ability to inspect individual interactions. Where monitoring tells you something is wrong, observation lets you find out why, which governance needs for incident investigation and for explaining outcomes to regulators.

Retention

How long each type of data is kept, and that it is deleted when the period ends. Retention balances legal requirements to keep records (insurance claim records for a set number of years; audit logs for regulators) against privacy principles that say data shouldn't be kept longer than needed. Set periods per data type (training data, prompts and responses, logs, agent memory), enforce them with S3 Lifecycle rules, CloudWatch Logs retention settings and memory expiry, and use S3 Object Lock where records must be kept unchanged for a period.

Other data governance practices Extra
  • Ownership and stewardship: every data set has a named owner who decides permitted uses.
  • Classification: label data by sensitivity (public, internal, confidential, restricted) to drive controls.
  • Cataloging and lineage: know what data exists and where it flows (AWS Glue Data Catalog, Lake Formation).
  • Consent and purpose limitation: use data only for the purposes it was collected for.

Governance processes

Skill 5.2.3Describe processes to follow governance protocols (for example, policies, review cadence, review strategies, governance frameworks such as the Generative AI Security Scoping Matrix, transparency standards, team training requirements)

Policies

Written rules for how the organization uses AI: an AI use policy (approved and prohibited uses, which tools are allowed, what data may be used with them), a risk classification that sets the level of review each use needs, requirements for human oversight, documentation and testing, data handling rules, and incident response. Policies assign accountability: who owns each AI system and who approves it. For the insurer: "Customer data may only be used with approved AI services in our AWS accounts; public AI chat tools may not receive customer or claim information; automated decisions on claims require human review."

Review cadence

How often AI systems and governance are reviewed. Reviews happen at milestones (approval before development, before launch, before major changes such as a new model or data source) and on a schedule after launch, with frequency set by risk: a high-risk claims-triage model reviewed quarterly, a low-risk internal writing assistant yearly. Triggers such as incidents, regulatory changes, drift alerts or provider model updates prompt an immediate review.

Review strategies

How reviews are done: risk-based (more scrutiny for higher-risk uses); cross-functional (business owners, data science, security, legal, compliance and risk together, often as an AI review board); evidence-based (evaluation results, bias tests, model cards, Config compliance, logs); independent (internal audit or external auditors for high-risk systems); and including red-teaming and sample reviews of real outputs. Outcomes are documented: approved, approved with conditions, or rejected, with actions and owners.

Generative AI Security Scoping Matrix

What it is

An AWS framework that classifies generative AI use cases into five scopes according to how much of the model and application the organization owns and controls. The scope determines which security and governance responsibilities fall on you, so you can apply the right controls instead of one standard for every use.

  • Scope 1, consumer app: using a public, consumer generative AI service (a free public chatbot). You control nothing about it beyond what you type in.
  • Scope 2, enterprise app: using a third-party business application with generative AI features, under an enterprise agreement (an AI feature in a SaaS product, or Amazon Quick).
  • Scope 3, pre-trained models: building your own application on an existing foundation model through an API, without changing the model (an app on Amazon Bedrock, including with RAG).
  • Scope 4, fine-tuned models: customizing an existing foundation model with your own data (fine-tuning on Bedrock or SageMaker AI).
  • Scope 5, self-trained models: training a model from scratch on your own data.

Moving from Scope 1 to Scope 5, you own more of the stack, gain control, and take on more responsibility. Across all scopes the matrix considers security disciplines: governance and compliance, legal and privacy, risk management, controls, and resilience.

Using it: the insurer's applications

  • Staff using a public chatbot to draft emails (Scope 1): main controls are usage policy and training: never enter customer or confidential data; review terms of service.
  • An AI feature in the company's CRM (Scope 2): vendor due diligence, contract terms on data use and retention, access settings.
  • A claims assistant built on Bedrock with a knowledge base (Scope 3): the company owns the application, so it owns prompt design, RAG data access, guardrails, IAM, logging, testing and output review.
  • A model fine-tuned on claims notes (Scope 4): all of Scope 3, plus training data governance (rights, personal data, lineage), protecting the custom model and evaluating it.
  • Scope 5 isn't planned: the company has no need to pre-train its own model.

Transparency standards

What they require

Commitments, often required by law or regulators, to be open about AI use: telling people when they are interacting with AI or when content is AI-generated; explaining automated decisions that affect them and how to contest them; documenting models (model cards with intended use, data, evaluation and limitations); publishing information about AI systems and their risks; and labeling or watermarking generated media. Organizations also align with recognized frameworks and standards, such as ISO/IEC 42001 for AI management systems and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and with AI regulation such as the EU AI Act, which sets transparency obligations by risk level.

On AWSSageMaker Model Cards for documenting your models; AWS AI Service Cards documenting AWS's own AI services; invisible watermarks on images generated by Amazon Nova Canvas.

Team training requirements

Who needs what

Governance only works if people know the rules and the risks. Training is tailored by role:

  • All staff: AI literacy, the AI use policy, approved tools, what data may never be entered, recognizing and reporting problems.
  • Builders (developers, data scientists): secure and responsible AI development, prompt injection and other AI threats, bias testing, documentation requirements.
  • Reviewers and approvers: how to assess AI risk and evidence.
  • Users of AI outputs (claims handlers, advisers): limitations, when to override, how to check outputs, avoiding over-reliance.

Training is repeated as tools, rules and regulations change, completion is recorded as evidence, and some regulations now require AI literacy for staff who operate AI systems.

"Classify a generative AI use by how much of the model the organization controls" points to the Generative AI Security Scoping Matrix. Using a public chatbot is Scope 1; a SaaS tool with AI features is Scope 2; building on a foundation model API such as Bedrock is Scope 3; fine-tuning is Scope 4; training from scratch is Scope 5.

Key takeaways

  • AWS Config checks resource configurations against rules and records their history; Amazon Inspector scans workloads for vulnerabilities; AWS Artifact provides AWS's audit reports, certifications (including ISO/IEC 42001) and agreements such as a BAA; AWS CloudTrail records who did what via the API; AWS Trusted Advisor recommends best practices for cost, security, fault tolerance, performance and quotas.
  • Data governance: manage each AI data type across its lifecycle (S3 Lifecycle), log activity and model interactions, keep data in required Regions (geographic inference profiles), monitor data and use, observe system behavior with traces, and enforce retention periods.
  • Governance processes: written policies and risk classification, reviews at milestones and on a risk-based schedule, cross-functional evidence-based review strategies, transparency standards (disclosure, documentation, frameworks such as ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIST AI RMF), and role-based training.
  • The Generative AI Security Scoping Matrix: Scope 1 consumer app, 2 enterprise app, 3 pre-trained models, 4 fine-tuned models, 5 self-trained models; responsibility and control grow with scope.
