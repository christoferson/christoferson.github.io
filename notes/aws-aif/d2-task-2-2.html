Overview

Task 2.2

Understand the capabilities and limitations of GenAI for solving business problems.

Questions here are about judgment: given a business problem, is generative AI a good fit, what could go wrong, which model would you pick and why, and how would you measure success. The strengths and weaknesses come from the same source. A model that generates fluent, flexible language from patterns, rather than looking up facts or following rules, is adaptable and conversational, and for the same reason can be confidently wrong and give different answers each time.

Advantages of GenAI

Skill 2.2.1Describe the advantages of GenAI (for example, adaptability, responsiveness, conversational capabilities, ability to generate content)

Traditional ML builds one model per task from labeled data. Generative AI starts from a foundation model that already understands language and content broadly, which changes how fast and how widely AI can be applied.

Adaptability

One foundation model can perform many different tasks, and switch between them, guided only by instructions: summarize a contract, then classify a complaint, then draft a reply, then translate it. New tasks often need just a new prompt and a few examples rather than a new training project with thousands of labeled records. It also adapts to new situations: a well-built assistant can handle questions nobody anticipated, where a rule-based bot would fail.

Example: an insurer uses the same model for summarizing claim files, extracting details from repair invoices, drafting customer letters and answering staff policy questions. With traditional ML, each would have been a separate model and project.

Why it mattersFaster time to value, lower cost per new use case, and less dependence on scarce labeled data and ML specialists.

Responsiveness

Generative AI produces answers and content on demand, in seconds, at any hour and at any volume. A product team can get a first draft of a launch announcement immediately; a support assistant can answer a customer at 3 a.m.; a sales team can get a briefing on a prospect before the call. It also responds to context in real time: the answer reflects the specific question, the customer's history and the documents retrieved for it.

Why it mattersShorter waiting times for customers and employees, 24/7 coverage, and the ability to handle spikes in demand without adding staff.

Conversational capabilities

People can interact in ordinary language rather than menus, forms or query languages, and can follow up, clarify and refine: "Show me last quarter's sales by region. Now only Europe. Why did Germany drop?" The model keeps track of the conversation, understands imprecise phrasing and typos, and can ask clarifying questions. This makes systems usable by people without technical skills, and makes customer interactions feel natural.

Why it mattersWider adoption (anyone can use it), better customer experience, and access to data and tools that previously needed specialists.

Ability to generate content

Generative AI creates new material: text, code, images, audio and video, in any style, length or language, and personalized for each audience. Product descriptions for 40,000 items, a marketing email variant for each customer segment, test cases for a new feature, images of a product in a hundred settings, a training course narrated in five languages.

Why it mattersContent production that scales far beyond human capacity at low marginal cost, and personalization that wasn't economically possible before.

Further advantages Extra
  • Simplicity: a capable application can be built with a prompt and an API call, with no model training or ML infrastructure.
  • Handles unstructured data: reads emails, documents and transcripts directly, the data most organizations have the most of and use the least.
  • Works with little or no labeled data (zero-shot and few-shot), so projects can start without a data-labeling effort.

Disadvantages of GenAI

Skill 2.2.2Identify disadvantages of GenAI solutions (for example, hallucinations, interpretability, inaccuracy, nondeterminism)

These limitations come from how foundation models work: they predict likely next tokens based on patterns in their training data. They don't look facts up in a verified database, don't follow explicit logic, and don't "know" when they don't know. Every disadvantage below can be reduced but not eliminated, which is why GenAI suits tasks where errors can be caught or tolerated.

Hallucinations

A hallucination is output that sounds fluent and confident but is false or unsupported: an invented statistic, a citation to a paper that doesn't exist, a product feature the company never offered, a made-up refund policy. It happens because the model generates what is plausible given its training, and plausible isn't the same as true. Hallucinations are most likely when the model lacks the relevant information (recent events, your internal data, niche topics) or is asked for precise details such as numbers, names and dates.

Real cases have had serious consequences, such as a chatbot promising a discount the company had to honor and lawyers citing nonexistent court cases generated by AI.

How to reduce itGround answers in your own documents with RAG and require citations; instruct the model to say "I don't know" when the context lacks the answer; use Amazon Bedrock Guardrails contextual grounding checks to block answers not supported by the source; use Automated Reasoning checks to validate answers against policy rules; keep a human reviewer for high-stakes outputs. Task 5.1 covers detection and grounding in depth.

Interpretability

Interpretability is the ability to understand why a model produced a given output. Foundation models have billions of parameters interacting in ways nobody can trace, so you can't point to the reason for a specific answer the way you can follow the branches of a decision tree. A model can be asked to explain itself, but that explanation is itself generated text, plausible but not guaranteed to reflect the actual computation.

This is a problem where decisions must be justified: lending, insurance, hiring, medical and legal decisions, and anywhere regulators or customers have a right to an explanation. It also makes debugging harder: when the model fails, it is hard to know what to fix.

How to reduce itUse GenAI to assist rather than decide in regulated decisions; show the sources used (citations from RAG); log prompts, context and outputs for audit; use interpretable traditional ML for decisions that must be explained; document models with model cards.

Inaccuracy

Beyond outright hallucination, outputs can be subtly wrong: outdated (the model's knowledge stops at its training cutoff), incomplete, biased toward patterns in its training data, wrong on arithmetic or multi-step logic, or right in general but wrong for your company's specific products, policies and terms. Accuracy also varies by task, domain and language: a model strong at general writing may be weak at your industry's terminology.

How to reduce itProvide current, authoritative information through RAG; use tools for precise work (a calculator or database query instead of model arithmetic); evaluate the model on your own test cases before launch; fine-tune for specialized domains; validate outputs (check that extracted totals add up, that generated SQL runs); monitor accuracy after launch.

Nondeterminism

The same prompt can produce different outputs on different runs. Models choose each next token by sampling from a probability distribution, and settings such as temperature control how much randomness that sampling uses. This is useful for creative work (varied marketing ideas) but a problem where consistency matters: two customers asking the same question get different answers, a test that passed yesterday fails today, an audit can't reproduce a past output.

Lowering temperature toward 0 makes outputs much more consistent, but doesn't guarantee identical results every time, and provider model updates can change behavior too.

How to reduce itUse low temperature for factual and structured tasks; specify the exact output format (for example, a JSON structure) and validate it; use templates and fixed examples; pin a specific model version; log every output; evaluate on many runs, not one.

Other limitations Extra
  • Knowledge cutoff: the model knows nothing after its training data ends, unless given current information.
  • Bias and toxicity: models can reproduce stereotypes or produce harmful content from their training data (Domain 4).
  • Security risks: prompt injection, jailbreaking and leakage of sensitive data (Task 3.2 and Domain 5).
  • Cost and latency at scale: per-token costs and generation time grow with usage and length.
  • Intellectual property: uncertainty over training data rights and ownership of generated content (Task 4.1).

Is GenAI a good fit? Worked judgments

Drafting first replies to customer emails, reviewed by an agent before sending

Good fit: adaptability and speed are valuable, and the human review catches hallucinations.

Calculating each customer's final invoice amount

Poor fit: an exact, deterministic result is required; use a rule or formula.

Automatically approving or declining loan applications with no human involvement

Poor fit: interpretability, consistency and fairness requirements favor an explainable traditional model with human oversight.

An internal assistant answering staff questions from the HR handbook, with citations

Good fit: RAG grounds the answers, citations let staff verify, and an occasional error is low risk.

When a question asks how to reduce hallucinations about company-specific facts, the best answer is usually RAG (grounding in your data), not fine-tuning, and not a larger model. When it asks how to make outputs more consistent, the answer is usually lower temperature.

Selecting a GenAI model

Skill 2.2.3Identify factors to consider when selecting GenAI models (for example, model types, performance requirements, capabilities, constraints, compliance, cost, latency, model complexity)

There is no best model, only the best model for a use case. Amazon Bedrock offers models from many providers precisely so you can pick and switch. A practical approach: list the requirements, shortlist models that meet the hard constraints, test the shortlist on your own examples (Bedrock Evaluations helps), and choose the smallest, cheapest model that meets the quality bar.

Model types

Match the type of model to the task's inputs and outputs:

  • Text generation (LLMs): writing, summarizing, answering, classifying, code.
  • Multimodal understanding: questions about images, documents, charts or video.
  • Image and video generation: diffusion-based models such as Amazon Nova Canvas or Stability AI; Amazon Nova Reel for video.
  • Embedding models: for search, RAG and similarity, not for generating text.
  • Speech models: real-time voice conversations, such as Amazon Nova Sonic.
  • General versus specialized: general-purpose models, or ones tuned for code, a language or a domain; and reasoning models that "think" step by step for complex problems, at higher cost and latency.

Performance requirements

How good does the output have to be, measured how? A marketing brainstorm tolerates variable quality; extracting amounts from invoices needs near-perfect accuracy. Define the quality bar and the metrics (accuracy on a test set, human ratings, faithfulness to sources) before comparing models, and test with your own data, because public benchmark scores don't always reflect performance on your task.

Capabilities

What the model can technically do: the size of its context window (can it take a 300-page contract?), maximum output length, languages supported, modalities, tool use (function calling, needed for agents), structured output, reasoning ability, and whether it can be customized (fine-tuning or distillation on Bedrock is available only for some models).

Constraints

Practical limits that rule models in or out: availability in the AWS Regions you must use, throughput quotas (tokens per minute), context limits, hardware needs if self-hosted, integration with your existing systems, and timelines. A model that is perfect but not offered in the Region where your data must stay isn't an option.

Compliance

Legal and regulatory requirements: where data is processed (data residency), whether prompts and outputs are stored or used for training (on Amazon Bedrock they are not used to train the base models and aren't shared with model providers), the model's license terms (especially for open-weight models), the provider's certifications, IP indemnity, and industry rules such as healthcare or financial regulations. Responsible AI properties, such as documented bias testing, also count.

Cost

Price per input and output token (which varies by more than an order of magnitude between models), times expected volume, plus any customization and hosting costs (fine-tuning, Provisioned Throughput). Estimate cost per interaction and per month at full scale, not just for the pilot. Discount options such as batch inference, the Flex tier and prompt caching change the comparison.

Latency

How fast the response must arrive: time to the first token and total generation time. A voice assistant needs near-instant responses; an overnight report generator doesn't care. Smaller models are faster; streaming improves perceived speed; latency-optimized inference options and the Priority tier help for time-critical uses.

Model complexity

Larger models (more parameters) are generally more capable at complex reasoning, nuanced writing and rare knowledge, but are slower, cost more per token and need more infrastructure to host. Smaller models are fast and cheap, and often good enough for focused tasks such as classification, extraction and routing. Many systems use several: a small model to route or classify each request, a large one only for the hard cases. Model families such as Amazon Nova offer tiers for this purpose, from Nova Micro (text only, fastest and cheapest) to larger, more capable multimodal models.

Choosing for a scenario

A call-center assistant classifying 2 million short messages a day into 12 categories

Volume and cost dominate; the task is simple. A small, fast model, possibly fine-tuned or distilled, with batch processing where results can wait.

A legal team analyzing 400-page contracts for unusual clauses

Capability dominates: a large context window, strong reasoning and high accuracy; latency matters little.

A European bank's customer assistant

Compliance dominates: a model available in EU Regions (with EU cross-Region inference keeping data in the EU), with Guardrails, logging and documented testing.

"Use the largest, most capable model" is rarely the right exam answer. The right answer meets the requirements at the lowest cost and latency.

Business value and metrics

Skill 2.2.4Determine business value and metrics for GenAI applications (for example, cross-domain performance, ROI, efficiency, conversion rate, average revenue per user, accuracy, customer lifetime value)

A GenAI application is worth building only if it moves a business outcome. Metrics should be chosen before launch, measured against a baseline (performance without the AI, or a control group), and tied to how the application creates value: saving cost, growing revenue or improving the customer experience.

The running example: an online outdoor-equipment retailer launches a shopping assistant that answers product questions and recommends gear, and uses GenAI to write product descriptions and personalized emails.

Cross-domain performance

How well one model or application performs across different tasks, subject areas or departments. Because foundation models are general-purpose, a single investment (a platform, a model, an assistant) can serve many teams: the same model writes product copy for marketing, answers questions for customer service and summarizes reviews for product managers. Measured by testing quality on each domain and counting the use cases served.

Why it mattersThe more domains one model serves well, the more its cost is shared, and the higher the overall return. Weak performance in a domain (for example, technical climbing gear) signals a need for retrieval or customization there.

Return on investment (ROI)

(Benefit − cost) ÷ cost. Benefits: revenue gained plus costs avoided. Costs: development, integration, model usage (tokens), infrastructure, monitoring, governance and staff time. The retailer spends $250,000 in the first year and gains $600,000 in extra margin and saved copywriting: ROI = ($600,000 − $250,000) ÷ $250,000 = 140%.

Efficiency

How much faster or cheaper work gets done: time per task (writing a product description falls from 30 minutes to 5 including review), tasks handled per person, share of requests resolved without a human (containment or automation rate), average handling time in a contact center, cost per interaction. Efficiency gains are often the first and most measurable value of GenAI.

Conversion rate

The share of visitors or prospects who take a desired action, usually buying: conversions ÷ visitors. If 2.0% of visitors buy without the assistant and 2.4% of those who use it buy, the assistant lifts conversion by 20% (relative). Measured with A/B tests: some visitors see the assistant and some don't, so the difference can be attributed to it.

Average revenue per user (ARPU)

Total revenue ÷ number of users (or customers) in a period. Personalized recommendations and better answers can raise basket size and add-on purchases: if ARPU among assistant users rises from $84 to $91 a quarter, the assistant is adding about $7 per customer per quarter.

Accuracy

The share of outputs that are correct: answers that are factually right, recommendations that match what was asked, extracted fields that match the source. It is both a quality metric and a business one: inaccurate answers cause returns, complaints and lost trust. Measured on test sets before launch and by sampling and reviewing real outputs after.

Customer lifetime value (CLV)

The total value a customer brings over the whole relationship. A simple estimate: average order value × orders per year × years as a customer. A customer spending $90 per order, 4 times a year, for 3 years has a CLV of $1,080. Better service and personalization raise CLV by increasing retention and repeat purchases; it is a long-term metric, so leading indicators such as repeat purchase rate and satisfaction are tracked meanwhile.

Other common GenAI metrics Extra
  • Customer satisfaction (CSAT, Net Promoter Score) and thumbs-up rates on answers.
  • Adoption and engagement: active users, sessions, repeat use.
  • Task completion rate: share of conversations where the user's goal was achieved.
  • Cost per interaction and token cost per user.
  • Escalation rate: share of conversations handed to a person.

Matching the metric to the goal

"Show that the shopping assistant increases sales"

Conversion rate and ARPU, measured with an A/B test.

"Show that the support assistant reduces costs"

Efficiency: containment rate, average handling time, cost per interaction.

"Show long-term customer value from personalization"

Customer lifetime value, with retention and repeat purchase rate as leading indicators.

"Justify the investment to the finance team"

ROI, including all costs.

"Decide whether to extend the platform to more departments"

Cross-domain performance.

Technical metrics alone (BLEU, ROUGE, latency) don't show business value. A question about demonstrating value to the business expects business metrics such as ROI, conversion or efficiency.

Key takeaways

  • GenAI's advantages: adaptability (one model, many tasks, little labeled data), responsiveness (instant, 24/7, scalable), conversation (natural language for anyone) and content generation at scale.
  • Its disadvantages come from predicting plausible tokens: hallucinations (confident falsehoods), poor interpretability, inaccuracy (outdated, domain gaps, arithmetic), and nondeterminism (varied outputs).
  • Reduce them with RAG and citations, contextual grounding and Automated Reasoning checks, tools for precise work, low temperature and fixed formats, evaluation on your own data, and human review for high stakes.
  • Select models on type, performance requirements, capabilities (context window, tools, customization), constraints (Region, quotas), compliance, cost, latency and complexity. Prefer the smallest model that meets the bar.
  • Prove value with business metrics against a baseline: cross-domain performance, ROI, efficiency, conversion rate, ARPU, accuracy and customer lifetime value.
