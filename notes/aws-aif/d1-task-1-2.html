Overview

Task 1.2

Identify practical use cases for AI.

Exam questions in this task describe a business situation and ask what fits: is AI worth it here, which technique solves it, which AWS service does it out of the box, and does it need a foundation model or a smaller, explainable ML model. The skill is reading the scenario for clues: the shape of the answer (a number, a category, a group), the data available, and the constraints (regulation, explanation, latency, cost).

Where AI/ML adds value

Skill 1.2.1Recognize applications where AI/ML can provide value (for example, assist human decision making, solution scalability, automation)

AI is worth considering when a task involves patterns in data that are too complex, too numerous or too fast for people or hand-written rules to handle well. The exam guide names three kinds of value. Most real projects deliver more than one.

Assisting human decision making

The model doesn't decide; it gives a person better information to decide with. It ranks, scores, flags or summarizes so the expert spends their time where it matters. A doctor sees an AI flag on the three scans most likely to show a problem; a loan officer sees a risk score and the top factors behind it; a maintenance planner sees which machines are most likely to fail this month.

This is the right pattern when decisions are high-stakes, regulated or need judgment the model can't supply, and it is the easiest to get approved because a person remains accountable. Its value comes from consistency (the model never gets tired), coverage (it reviews every case, not a sample) and speed.

Exam cue"Help analysts prioritize", "provide recommendations to staff", "human makes the final decision" all point here, and to keeping a human in the loop.

Solution scalability

Once trained, a model handles ten cases or ten million at the same quality and nearly the same effort. People don't scale that way. A product team can read a few hundred reviews a week; Amazon Comprehend can analyze the sentiment of millions. A contact center can't translate every chat into thirty languages with staff; Amazon Translate can do it in real time.

Scalability matters when volume is growing faster than headcount, when demand spikes (a retailer on a holiday weekend), or when the business wants to reach more customers, languages or markets without proportional cost.

Exam cue"Millions of documents", "growing volume", "global customers", "24/7" point to scalability.

Automation

The model performs a repetitive task end to end, with people handling only exceptions. Reading invoices and entering their totals (Amazon Textract), transcribing calls (Amazon Transcribe), routing support emails to the right queue (a classification model), blocking obviously fraudulent payments, answering routine customer questions (a chatbot built with Amazon Lex or a foundation model).

Automation saves time and cost and reduces human error on dull work, but it needs a confidence threshold: high-confidence cases go straight through, low-confidence cases go to a person. Agentic AI extends automation from single steps to multi-step tasks.

Exam cue"Reduce manual effort", "repetitive", "process documents without human review" point to automation.

Other signs AI fits Extra
  • Rules would be too complex or change too often: spam and fraud patterns change weekly; a model retrained on new data keeps up, while rules need constant rewriting.
  • The input is unstructured: images, speech and free text can't be handled by rules at all.
  • Personalization at scale: a different recommendation or offer for each of millions of customers.
  • Enough relevant data exists, or a pre-trained model or service already covers the task.

When AI/ML isn't appropriate

Skill 1.2.2Determine when AI/ML solutions are not appropriate (for example, cost-benefit analyses, situations when a specific outcome is needed instead of a prediction)

ML produces predictions: likely, usually right, sometimes wrong. That makes it the wrong tool in some situations, however impressive it is. The exam expects you to recognize these.

When a specific, exact outcome is needed

If the answer must be exactly right every time and the rule for producing it is known, write the rule. Calculating sales tax, computing an invoice total, applying a published price list, checking whether a password matches, enforcing "no withdrawals over the daily limit", or calculating a pension under a fixed formula: all are deterministic. A model would only approximate the formula, could get some cases wrong, and would be harder to audit than the formula itself.

The same applies where law or contract requires a definite, traceable rule: an eligibility rule written in regulation should be implemented as that rule.

Exam cue"Must always produce the same result", "deterministic", "exact calculation", "100% accuracy required", "rules are well defined" mean AI is not appropriate.

When costs outweigh benefits

A cost-benefit analysis compares the full cost of an AI solution with the value it creates. Costs include collecting and labeling data, building or customizing the model, inference (paid on every use, and per token for foundation models), monitoring and retraining, specialist staff, and the cost of the model's mistakes. Benefits include time saved, errors avoided, revenue gained and risk reduced.

AI often loses this comparison when volume is low (an AI system to process 50 forms a month will rarely pay back; a clerk will), when a simple rule already gets 95% of cases right, when the cost of a wrong prediction is very high, or when a cheaper non-AI option (a better form, a process change) removes the problem.

Other warning signs Extra

  • No suitable data: too little, poor quality, unrepresentative, or not legally usable. A model can't learn patterns that aren't in the data.
  • Full explainability required, and the model can't provide it: some decisions must be justified to a regulator or customer step by step.
  • The world is changing faster than the model can learn: a model trained on pre-pandemic travel data failed when travel stopped.
  • Errors are unacceptable and can't be caught: fully automated decisions with no review where one wrong answer causes serious harm.

When an answer option suggests a simple rule, a lookup or a formula, and the scenario describes known rules with an exact required result, that option is usually correct over any ML option.

Choosing the ML technique

Skill 1.2.3Select the appropriate AI/ML techniques for specific use cases (for example, regression, classification, clustering)

The quickest way to choose: look at the shape of the answer the business wants, and whether labeled past examples exist.

  1. Is the answer a number on a scale?Price, quantity, minutes, temperature, revenue. Use regression.
  2. Is the answer one of a fixed set of categories, with labeled examples?Yes or no, fraud or genuine, which of five topics. Use classification.
  3. Are there no labels, and you want to discover natural groups?Customer segments, similar documents. Use clustering.

Regression

Predicts a continuous numeric value from input features. The model learns how the inputs relate to the number: how floor area, location and age affect a house's price. Linear regression fits a straight-line relationship; tree-based methods such as XGBoost capture more complex ones.

Examples: predicting a home's sale price; estimating delivery time in minutes; forecasting next week's sales units (time-series forecasting is a specialized form of regression); estimating a customer's lifetime value; predicting energy demand in megawatts; estimating an insurance claim's cost.

How it's judgedBy how far predictions are from actual values, for example mean absolute error ("off by $12,000 on average") or root mean squared error, which punishes big misses more.

Classification

Predicts which category a case belongs to, learned from labeled examples of each category. Binary classification has two classes (spam or not; will churn or won't); multi-class has more (which of ten product categories; which department an email belongs to). The model usually outputs a probability per class, and a threshold turns it into a decision.

Examples: fraud detection on card payments; email spam filtering; predicting customer churn; diagnosing a disease from symptoms; sentiment (positive, negative, neutral); recognizing which product is in an image; approving or declining a loan.

How it's judgedBy accuracy, precision, recall and F1 score (covered in Task 1.3).

Clustering

Groups similar items together without labels: unsupervised learning. The algorithm (for example, k-means) measures how similar records are and forms groups; people then examine each group and decide what it means. You don't know the groups in advance; finding them is the point.

Examples: customer segmentation for marketing; grouping similar support tickets to find common problems; grouping news articles by topic; finding patterns in sensor data; organizing a product catalog by similarity.

How it's judgedBy how tight and well separated the groups are, and above all whether the groups are useful to the business.

Worked choices

"Predict how many bikes will be rented at each station tomorrow"

A number: regression (on time-series data).

"Decide whether an insurance claim should be investigated, using past claims marked fraudulent or not"

Two labeled categories: binary classification.

"Discover groups of shoppers with similar habits to target campaigns"

No labels, discover groups: clustering.

"Assign each incoming support ticket to billing, technical or account teams"

Predefined categories with past labeled tickets: multi-class classification.

"Estimate the probability that a customer will cancel"

Still classification. The output is a probability, but it is the probability of a category (cancel or stay).

A probability output doesn't make a problem regression. "Probability of default" is binary classification; "amount of the expected loss in dollars" is regression.

Other techniques you may see Extra
  • Anomaly detection: finding unusual records, often unsupervised; for fraud with few labeled examples, equipment faults, security threats. SageMaker AI's Random Cut Forest algorithm does this.
  • Recommendation: predicting what a user will like from past behavior of similar users and items (Amazon Personalize).
  • Forecasting: regression on time-series data, predicting future values from past ones.
  • Dimensionality reduction: compressing many features into fewer (for example, PCA).

Real-world AI applications

Skill 1.2.4Identify examples of real-world AI applications (for example, computer vision, NLP, speech recognition, recommendation systems, fraud detection, forecasting, knowledge bases, agentic AI)

For each application, know what it does, typical examples across industries, and the AWS service that most directly supports it.

Computer vision

Interpreting images and video. Manufacturing: spotting defects on a production line. Retail: checking shelves for gaps. Media: moderating uploaded images for unsafe content. Security: verifying a person's identity with face comparison and liveness checks. Insurance: assessing vehicle damage from photos. Finance: reading text from scanned documents and IDs.

On AWSAmazon Rekognition (labels, objects, faces, text in images, unsafe content, Custom Labels for your own objects, Face Liveness); Amazon Textract (text, forms, tables, invoices, receipts, IDs from documents).

Natural language processing

Understanding text. Analyzing the sentiment of reviews and social media; extracting names, dates and amounts from contracts; detecting and redacting personal data; classifying emails; translating product listings into other languages.

On AWSAmazon Comprehend for text analysis; Amazon Translate for translation; foundation models on Amazon Bedrock for summarizing, drafting and open-ended language tasks.

Speech recognition

Converting spoken language to text (automatic speech recognition, ASR), and the reverse, text to speech. Transcribing and analyzing contact-center calls; captioning videos and meetings; clinical note dictation; voice-controlled apps; reading content aloud for accessibility.

On AWSAmazon Transcribe (speech to text); Amazon Polly (text to speech); Amazon Lex (understanding spoken or typed requests in a conversation).

Recommendation systems

Suggesting items a user is likely to want, based on their behavior and that of similar users: "customers who bought this also bought", personalized home pages on streaming services, next-best-offer in banking, personalized marketing emails, ranking search results per user.

On AWSAmazon Personalize, a managed service that trains recommendation models on your interaction data (who viewed, clicked or bought what).

Fraud detection

Spotting fraudulent transactions, claims, accounts or logins. Usually classification trained on past cases marked fraud or genuine, often combined with anomaly detection for new fraud patterns. Examples: card payment fraud, fake account sign-ups, insurance claim fraud, promotion abuse.

On AWSModels built and hosted on Amazon SageMaker AI (for example, XGBoost for classification, Random Cut Forest for anomalies). Amazon Fraud Detector has been closed to new customers since November 7, 2025; AWS points new users to SageMaker AI and AutoGluon instead.

Forecasting

Predicting future values from past time-series data: retail demand by store and product, staffing levels for a contact center, energy load, cash flow, web traffic and server capacity.

On AWSAmazon SageMaker AI, including no-code forecasting in SageMaker Canvas. Amazon Forecast, the earlier dedicated service, is no longer available to new customers.

Knowledge bases

Letting people ask questions in natural language and get answers drawn from an organization's own documents: policies, manuals, product specs, past tickets. The system searches the documents for the most relevant passages and gives them to a foundation model, which writes an answer and cites its sources. This approach is called retrieval augmented generation (RAG), covered in Task 3.1. Examples: an HR assistant answering policy questions; a support assistant that knows every product manual; a legal team searching thousands of contracts.

On AWSAmazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases handles the whole flow: ingesting documents, splitting and embedding them, storing them in a vector store, retrieving relevant passages and generating cited answers. Amazon Quick provides ready-made knowledge assistants for business users. Amazon Kendra, an earlier enterprise search service, has been in maintenance mode since June 30, 2026 and closed to new customers since July 30, 2026.

Agentic AI

Agents that complete multi-step tasks by reasoning and calling tools. Customer service agents that look up orders, process returns and update records; IT agents that diagnose an alert and restart a service; coding agents that write, test and fix code (Kiro); research agents that search sources and write a report; finance agents that reconcile invoices against purchase orders and flag mismatches; agents that modernize legacy code (AWS Transform).

On AWSAmazon Bedrock AgentCore to deploy and operate agents; Strands Agents to build them; Kiro for software development; Amazon Quick for business-user agents and automations.

AWS managed AI/ML services

Skill 1.2.5Explain the capabilities of AWS managed AI/ML services (for example, Amazon SageMaker AI, Amazon Transcribe, Amazon Translate, Amazon Comprehend, Amazon Lex, Amazon Polly)

AWS offers AI at three levels. AI services (Transcribe, Translate, Comprehend, Lex, Polly, Rekognition, Textract, Personalize) are pre-trained models behind an API: you send data and get results, no ML expertise needed. Generative AI services (Amazon Bedrock) give access to foundation models. ML platforms (SageMaker AI) let you build, train and deploy your own models. The rule of thumb: if a pre-trained AI service does the job, it is the fastest and cheapest choice; build with SageMaker AI only when your problem is specific to your data.

Amazon SageMaker AI

A fully managed service to build, train and deploy ML models at any scale. It covers the whole lifecycle: notebooks and Studio for data scientists, data preparation, built-in algorithms and support for common frameworks (PyTorch, TensorFlow, scikit-learn, XGBoost), managed training jobs, automatic hyperparameter tuning, a model registry, pipelines for MLOps, and hosting with real-time, serverless, asynchronous and batch inference. SageMaker JumpStart offers pre-trained and open-source models you can deploy or fine-tune, and SageMaker Canvas lets business analysts build models without code.

Use it when you need a custom model trained on your own data (churn, fraud, demand), want full control over the model, or need to host an open-source model yourself.

NameAmazon SageMaker was renamed Amazon SageMaker AI on December 3, 2024. "Amazon SageMaker" is now the name of the wider unified data, analytics and AI platform.

Amazon Transcribe

Automatic speech recognition: converts speech in audio or video into text, in batch (recorded files) or streaming (live audio). It adds timestamps, punctuation and speaker identification (who said what), supports custom vocabularies for product names and jargon, can redact personal data from transcripts, and filters unwanted words. Call Analytics adds call-center insight such as sentiment and call summaries; Transcribe Medical handles clinical speech.

Typical usesTranscribing customer calls for analysis, captioning videos, meeting notes, searchable media archives.

Amazon Translate

Neural machine translation between dozens of languages, in real time or in batch for documents. Custom terminology keeps brand and product names translated consistently; it can detect the source language automatically and translate formatted documents.

Typical usesTranslating product listings, support chats, user reviews and websites for international customers.

Amazon Comprehend

NLP service that finds insights in text: sentiment (positive, negative, neutral, mixed), entities (people, places, organizations, dates, quantities), key phrases, the language of the text, and personally identifiable information (PII) to detect or redact. Custom classification and custom entity recognition can be trained on your own labeled examples, for example to route tickets into your own categories.

Typical usesAnalyzing customer feedback, sorting documents, redacting PII before storing or sharing text.

StatusComprehend's topic modeling, event detection and prompt safety classification features are no longer available to new customers; the core features above continue.

Amazon Lex

Builds conversational interfaces (chatbots and voice bots) that understand typed or spoken requests. You define intents (what the user wants, such as "book appointment"), sample phrases and slots (the details to collect, such as date and time); Lex handles speech recognition and language understanding, asks follow-up questions to fill missing slots, and calls AWS Lambda to complete the action. It integrates with contact centers such as Amazon Connect, and can use generative AI to help understand requests and answer from documents.

Typical usesCustomer service bots, phone menus that understand natural speech, booking and order-status bots. Lex is the same technology as Alexa.

Amazon Polly

Text to speech: turns text into lifelike speech in many languages and voices, including neural and generative voices. Speech Synthesis Markup Language (SSML) controls pronunciation, pauses, emphasis and speed; lexicons define how specific words are pronounced.

Typical usesVoice responses in contact centers, reading articles aloud, e-learning narration, accessibility features.

Other in-scope AI services Extra

Amazon Rekognition, Amazon Textract, Amazon Personalize

Rekognition analyzes images and video (objects, faces, text, unsafe content, custom labels). Textract extracts printed and handwritten text, forms, tables and specific fields from documents, going beyond plain OCR because it keeps the structure. Personalize builds recommendation models from user interaction data.

Matching a requirement to a service

"Convert recorded customer calls to text"

Amazon Transcribe.

"Find out whether product reviews are positive or negative"

Amazon Comprehend (sentiment analysis).

"Offer the website in Spanish, French and Japanese"

Amazon Translate.

"Build a chatbot that books appointments by voice or text"

Amazon Lex.

"Read order confirmations aloud to callers"

Amazon Polly.

"Train a custom model to predict which customers will cancel"

Amazon SageMaker AI.

"Translate a Spanish voice call into English speech"

A chain: Transcribe (speech to Spanish text), Translate (Spanish to English text), Polly (English text to speech).

Transcribe and Polly are opposites: Transcribe turns speech into text, Polly turns text into speech. Lex understands what the speaker wants and runs a conversation; it is not just transcription.

Traditional ML or foundation models

Skill 1.2.6Identify when traditional ML models or foundation models (FMs) are appropriate for a specific use case (for example, based on regulatory concerns, explainability requirements, operational constraints)

A foundation model can attempt almost any task, which makes it tempting to use one for everything. But a small model trained for one job is often more accurate on that job, cheaper, faster, more predictable and easier to explain. The decision turns on the task and on three kinds of constraint.

Start with the task

Traditional ML fits narrow prediction on structured data

Predicting a number or category from tabular records (credit risk, churn, demand, fraud, prices) is what classic ML does best. A gradient-boosted tree trained on your own history will usually beat a general foundation model at it, at a tiny fraction of the cost per prediction.

Foundation models fit open-ended language and content tasks

Summarizing, drafting, answering questions from documents, conversational assistants, translating with nuance, generating images or code, extracting information from varied documents, and tasks with little or no labeled data. A foundation model can be ready in days through prompting, where a custom model would need months of data collection.

Then check the constraints

Regulatory concerns

In regulated decisions such as credit, insurance pricing, hiring and healthcare, organizations must show what data the model used, test it for bias, document it, and often keep it unchanged between approvals. A traditional model trained on known, governed data, with fixed behavior, is far easier to validate and audit. A foundation model was trained by a provider on data you can't fully inspect, its outputs vary, and the provider may update it. Regulations about personal data and data residency also matter: where the model runs and what data it sees.

Leans towardTraditional ML for regulated automated decisions about people. A foundation model can still help in these businesses for lower-risk tasks, such as drafting letters a person checks.

Explainability requirements

If you must explain each decision ("your loan was declined mainly because of your debt-to-income ratio"), you need a model whose reasoning can be traced. Linear models and decision trees are interpretable by design; other traditional models can be explained with feature-importance tools such as SHAP. A foundation model's billions of parameters make its reasoning opaque; it can write an explanation, but that explanation is generated text, not a faithful account of how the answer was produced.

Leans towardTraditional ML, especially interpretable models, when each decision must be justified.

Operational constraints

The practical realities of running the model:

  • Latency: scoring a card payment in a few milliseconds suits a small model; a large FM takes much longer to respond.
  • Cost at volume: millions of predictions a day on a small model cost very little; FM inference is priced per token and adds up quickly.
  • Consistency: a traditional model gives the same output for the same input; FMs are nondeterministic unless carefully configured.
  • Deployment environment: a small model can run on a device, at the edge or offline; large FMs need substantial GPU infrastructure or a managed API.
  • Data availability and speed to deliver: no labeled data and a deadline next month favors a foundation model with good prompts.

Worked choices

A bank scoring credit applications, which must explain declines to applicants and regulators

Traditional ML: structured data, regulated decision, explanation required.

A payment processor screening 5,000 transactions a second

Traditional ML: millisecond latency and huge volume make FM inference too slow and costly.

A law firm wanting to summarize and question thousands of contracts

Foundation model with RAG: unstructured text, open-ended questions, no labeled data.

A retailer writing product descriptions for 40,000 new items

Foundation model: content generation.

An insurer that wants to triage claims and also draft customer letters

Both: a traditional model scores claims for triage; a foundation model drafts letters that a handler reviews. Combining them is common.

"Explain each decision", "regulator", "audit", "structured data", "millisecond latency" or "consistent output" point to traditional ML. "Generate", "summarize", "conversational", "unstructured documents", "little labeled data" or "many different tasks" point to a foundation model.

Key takeaways

  • AI/ML adds value by assisting human decisions (a person stays accountable), scaling to volumes people can't handle, and automating repetitive tasks with exceptions sent to people.
  • AI isn't appropriate when an exact, deterministic outcome is required and the rule is known, or when a cost-benefit analysis shows data, inference, monitoring and error costs exceed the value.
  • Regression predicts a number, classification a predefined category (even when it outputs a probability), clustering discovers unlabeled groups.
  • Know the application-to-service map: computer vision to Rekognition and Textract; NLP to Comprehend and Translate; speech to Transcribe, Polly and Lex; recommendations to Personalize; fraud and forecasting to SageMaker AI; knowledge bases to Bedrock Knowledge Bases; agents to AgentCore, Strands Agents, Kiro and Quick.
  • Pre-trained AI services need no ML expertise; SageMaker AI is for custom models; Bedrock is for foundation models.
  • Fraud Detector, Forecast and Kendra are closed to new customers; their use cases now map to SageMaker AI and Bedrock Knowledge Bases.
  • Choose traditional ML for structured prediction, regulated or explained decisions, low latency, high volume and consistent output; choose foundation models for open-ended language and content tasks with little labeled data. Many solutions combine both.
