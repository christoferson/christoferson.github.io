Basic AI terms

Skill 1.1.1Define basic AI terms (for example, AI, ML, deep learning, neural networks, computer vision, natural language processing [NLP], model, algorithm, training and inferencing, bias, fairness, fit, large language model [LLM], generative AI [GenAI], agentic AI)

The exam guide lists fifteen terms. They fall into three groups: the fields of AI (what kind of AI something is), the building blocks (how any model is made and used), and the qualities a model can have (whether it is fair and whether it generalizes). Learning them in groups makes the relationships easier to remember.

The fields of AI

Artificial intelligence (AI)

AI is the broad field of building computer systems that perform tasks normally needing human intelligence: recognizing speech, understanding language, spotting patterns, making decisions, solving problems. It is an umbrella term for a goal, not a specific technique. Anything that makes a computer behave intelligently counts, whether it learned that behavior from data or a person wrote it as rules.

Early AI was mostly hand-written rules ("if the temperature is above 30 and humidity above 80%, warn the operator"), called rule-based or expert systems. A chess program that searches possible moves, a route planner that finds the shortest path, and a fraud rule that blocks payments over $5,000 from new accounts are all AI, but none of them learns.

Exam cue"AI" is the widest term. If an answer says a rule-based system "isn't AI", it is wrong: rules are AI, just not machine learning.

Machine learning (ML)

Machine learning is the branch of AI in which a system learns patterns from data instead of following rules a person wrote. You give it many examples, and it works out the relationship between the inputs and the outcome by itself. The result can then be applied to new cases it has never seen.

Machine learning is the right tool when the rules are too many, too subtle or change too often for anyone to write down. Nobody could write rules that reliably separate fraudulent card payments from genuine ones across millions of customers, but a model trained on millions of past payments, each marked fraud or genuine, can learn the signs. Typical ML tasks: predicting whether a customer will cancel, forecasting next week's demand, estimating a house price, grouping customers by behavior.

On AWSAmazon SageMaker AI is the service for building, training and deploying your own ML models.

Deep learning

Deep learning is a branch of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers (hence "deep"). Each layer learns slightly more abstract patterns than the one before. In an image model, early layers detect edges, middle layers combine edges into shapes such as wheels or eyes, and later layers recognize whole objects such as a car or a face.

Its big advantage is that it learns useful features from raw data by itself. Classic ML usually needs people to prepare the inputs ("number of purchases in the last 30 days"); deep learning can take raw pixels, audio or text. That is why it dominates image recognition, speech and language. The cost is that it needs much more data and computing power (usually GPUs), and its reasoning is hard to explain.

Exam cueUnstructured data such as images, audio and free text, plus a need for high accuracy, points to deep learning. Small tabular data and a need to explain decisions points to classic ML.

Neural network

A neural network is the structure deep learning uses, loosely inspired by the brain. It is made of layers of connected nodes, called neurons. The input layer receives the data (for example, one node per pixel), one or more hidden layers transform it, and the output layer gives the answer (for example, one node per possible label, each with a probability).

Each connection has a weight, a number saying how strongly one node influences the next. Training adjusts the millions or billions of weights until the network's outputs match the right answers. A network with one hidden layer is "shallow"; one with many is "deep", which is where deep learning gets its name. Different shapes suit different data: convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for images, recurrent networks for sequences, and transformers for language and most modern generative AI.

Exam cueNeurons, layers and weights are the vocabulary. You won't be asked to calculate anything.

Computer vision

Computer vision is the field of AI that lets computers interpret images and video. Its common tasks are:

  • Image classification: what is this image? ("a damaged car").
  • Object detection: what objects are in it and where? (boxes around each person in a store camera feed).
  • Segmentation: which exact pixels belong to each object (outlining a tumor on a scan).
  • Facial analysis, text in images and video analysis: detecting faces, reading a license plate, tracking people across frames.

Examples: a manufacturer spotting scratches on parts on the production line; a retailer counting shelf gaps; a radiology tool flagging scans for review; a phone unlocking with a face.

On AWSAmazon Rekognition for image and video analysis; Amazon Textract for reading text, forms and tables from scanned documents.

Natural language processing (NLP)

NLP is the field of AI that lets computers understand and produce human language. Tasks include sentiment analysis (is this review positive?), entity recognition (finding names, places, dates and account numbers in text), classification (routing an email to the right team), translation, summarization, question answering and speech handling (turning speech into text and back).

Before large language models, each NLP task usually had its own model. Today LLMs handle many NLP tasks with one model, but purpose-built services are still cheaper and more predictable for a single, well-defined task at high volume.

On AWSAmazon Comprehend (sentiment, entities, key phrases, PII detection, custom classification), Amazon Translate, Amazon Transcribe (speech to text), Amazon Polly (text to speech), Amazon Lex (conversational interfaces).

Large language model (LLM)

An LLM is a very large deep learning model, usually a transformer, trained on huge amounts of text to understand and generate language. Its basic skill is predicting the next piece of text (a token, roughly three-quarters of a word) given everything before it. Repeating that prediction one token at a time produces sentences, answers, summaries and code.

Because they learned from so much text, LLMs can do many tasks without task-specific training: answer questions, summarize a contract, draft an email, classify a ticket, translate, write and explain code. "Large" refers to the number of parameters (from billions to hundreds of billions) and the amount of training data. An LLM is one kind of foundation model: a large model trained on broad data that can be adapted to many tasks.

On AWSAmazon Bedrock gives API access to LLMs from Amazon (Amazon Nova) and other providers such as Anthropic, Meta and Mistral AI.

Generative AI (GenAI)

Generative AI is AI that creates new content (text, images, audio, video, code) rather than only classifying or predicting. It is built on foundation models. A traditional ML model answers "is this email spam?"; a generative model writes the reply.

Its strength is flexibility: one model, instructed in plain language through a prompt, can perform many tasks. Its weaknesses are the reverse side of that: it can produce fluent but false statements (hallucinations), the same prompt can give different answers (it is nondeterministic), and it is hard to explain why it produced a given output. Examples: drafting product descriptions, summarizing support calls, generating marketing images, answering employee questions from company documents, writing code.

On AWSAmazon Bedrock to build GenAI applications on foundation models; Amazon Quick and Kiro as ready-made GenAI applications for business users and developers.

Agentic AI

Agentic AI is AI that pursues a goal on its own: it plans steps, takes actions by calling tools, observes the results and adjusts until the goal is met. An AI agent combines a foundation model (to reason and decide) with tools (APIs, databases, search, code it can run), memory (what happened earlier in the task and in past sessions) and a loop that keeps going until it finishes or hands over to a person.

The difference from a chatbot is action. Asked "my order hasn't arrived", a GenAI assistant explains the returns policy. An agent looks up the order, checks the carrier's tracking, sees the parcel was lost, issues a replacement within its approved limit and tells the customer. Because agents act, they need firm limits: which tools they may use, what they may spend or change, and when a person must approve.

On AWSStrands Agents, an open-source toolkit, to build agents; Amazon Bedrock AgentCore to run, connect, secure and monitor them at scale. Amazon Bedrock Agents, the earlier console-configured agent feature, is now called Amazon Bedrock Agents Classic and is no longer open to new customers from July 30, 2026; AWS recommends AgentCore instead.

The building blocks

These terms describe how every ML model, from a small fraud model to an LLM, is made and used. In the order they happen: an algorithm is applied to data during training, which produces a model; using the model on new data is inferencing.

Algorithm

An algorithm is the step-by-step method used to learn from data. It is general: the same algorithm can be applied to house prices, customer churn or loan defaults. Examples are linear regression (fits a straight-line relationship), decision trees (learn a series of yes/no questions), k-means (groups similar records) and neural networks. Think of the algorithm as a recipe: it says how to cook, not what the finished dish tastes like.

On AWSSageMaker AI provides built-in algorithms, such as XGBoost for tabular prediction, k-means for clustering and Random Cut Forest for anomaly detection.

Model

A model is what you get when an algorithm is trained on data: the learned patterns, stored as numbers called parameters (weights). If the algorithm is the recipe, the model is the dish. Two companies using the same algorithm on different data get different models, because each model reflects the data it learned from. A model is a file that can be stored, versioned, deployed and replaced.

Exam cue"Algorithm" is the method; "model" is the trained result. Answers that swap them are wrong.

Training

Training is the process of showing an algorithm many examples so it can adjust the model's parameters. In each round, the model makes predictions on the examples, compares them with the right answers, measures how wrong it was (the loss) and nudges its parameters to be less wrong. This repeats over the data many times (each full pass is an epoch) until the error stops improving.

Training is expensive: it needs prepared data, computing power (for large models, many GPUs for weeks) and expert time. It happens before the model is used and again whenever the model needs refreshing.

Inferencing

Inferencing (or inference) is using a trained model to make predictions on new data. Training looks back at the past; inference applies what was learned to the present. A fraud model is trained once on historical payments, then performs inference on every new payment. An LLM was trained by its provider; every prompt you send it is an inference request.

Inference is the running cost: you pay for it every time the model is used. For a popular application, lifetime inference cost usually far exceeds training cost. How you run inference (in real time, in batches, asynchronously or serverlessly) is covered in Types of inferencing below.

Qualities of a model

Bias

In AI, bias has two meanings, and the exam uses both:

  • Unfair bias: a model systematically produces worse outcomes for some groups of people. It usually comes from the data: if past hiring favored men, a model trained on past hiring learns to favor men. It can also come from data that under-represents a group (a face model trained mostly on light-skinned faces works worse on darker skin), from labels that reflect human prejudice, or from features that stand in for a protected attribute (postal code acting as a proxy for ethnicity).
  • Statistical bias: error caused by a model being too simple to capture the real pattern. A straight line fitted to a curved relationship is wrong in a consistent way. High statistical bias causes underfitting (see Fit below).

Exam cueA question about demographic groups, discrimination or fairness means unfair bias. A question about "bias and variance" or model complexity means statistical bias.

Fairness

Fairness is the goal that a model's outcomes don't unjustly disadvantage individuals or groups, particularly by attributes such as age, gender, ethnicity or disability. It is checked by comparing results across groups: does the loan model approve similar applicants at similar rates whatever their gender? Does the speech model make as few errors for older speakers as younger ones?

There is no single definition of fair. "Same approval rate for every group" and "same error rate for every group" can conflict, so the right measure depends on the use and should be agreed before the model is built. Fairness is one of the dimensions of responsible AI covered in Domain 4.

On AWSAmazon SageMaker Clarify measured bias in data and models, but is no longer open to new customers (see the note below). For generative AI, Amazon Bedrock Evaluations can test models for bias and toxicity, and Amazon Bedrock Guardrails filters harmful content.

Fit

Fit describes how well a model has learned the real pattern in the data, judged by how it performs on new data it didn't see in training. There are three cases:

  • Underfitting: the model is too simple, or trained too little, to capture the pattern. It does badly on the training data and on new data. Example: predicting house prices from floor area alone, ignoring location. This is high bias.
  • Overfitting: the model has memorized the training data, including its noise and coincidences, instead of learning the general pattern. It does very well on training data but badly on new data. Example: a model that "learns" that every house on one street sold high because of one unusual sale. This is high variance: small changes in training data change the model a lot.
  • Good fit: the model performs well, and similarly, on training data and new data.

To detect fit, data is split: most is used for training, and a held-back portion (validation and test sets) checks performance on unseen examples. A big gap between training and test performance means overfitting. Overfitting is reduced with more and more varied data, a simpler model, regularization (penalizing complexity) or stopping training earlier; underfitting is reduced with a more complex model, better features or longer training.

Exam cue"High training accuracy, low test accuracy" means overfitting. "Low accuracy on both" means underfitting.

SageMaker Clarify is no longer open to new customers. Existing customers can keep using it, but AWS doesn't plan new features. For new work, AWS points to calculating bias metrics directly with open-source libraries (pandas, scikit-learn), SHAP for explanations, and Amazon Bedrock Evaluations for foundation models. Exam questions written before the change may still name Clarify as the bias and explainability tool, so know what it did.

Supporting terms Extra

The exam guide doesn't list these, but the definitions above depend on them and they appear in answer options throughout the exam.

Features and labels

Features are the input facts the model learns from: for a loan, the applicant's income, debts and employment length. A label is the known answer for a past example: "repaid" or "defaulted". In a table of training data, features are the input columns and the label is the column the model learns to predict.

Parameters and hyperparameters

Parameters are the values the model learns during training (a neural network's weights). Hyperparameters are settings people choose before training that control how it learns: the learning rate (how big each adjustment is), the number of epochs, the number of layers. Choosing hyperparameters well is called hyperparameter tuning; SageMaker AI can automate it.

Foundation model (FM)

A very large model pre-trained on broad data (text, images or both) so that it can be adapted to many downstream tasks. LLMs are text foundation models; there are also image, multimodal and embedding foundation models. Task 2.1 covers them in depth.

AI, ML, deep learning, GenAI and agentic AI

Skill 1.1.2Describe the similarities and differences between AI, ML, GenAI, deep learning, and agentic AI

The five terms are best understood as nested circles, each a subset of the one outside it, with agentic AI as a way of using generative AI rather than a new kind of model.

  1. Artificial intelligenceAny technique that makes computers behave intelligently, including hand-written rules.
  2. Machine learningThe part of AI that learns patterns from data instead of following written rules.
  3. Deep learningThe part of ML that uses many-layered neural networks, and learns features from raw data.
  4. Generative AIThe part of deep learning that creates new content, using foundation models.
  5. Agentic AIGenerative AI given goals, tools, memory and a loop, so it can plan and act, not just respond.

What each level adds

From AI to machine learning: learning from data

Rule-based AI does exactly what its authors wrote. ML replaces hand-written rules with patterns learned from examples. The benefit is handling problems too complex for rules; the cost is that you need good data, and the model's behavior is only as good as that data.

From ML to deep learning: learning the features too

Classic ML (such as decision trees or linear regression) works best on structured, tabular data where people have prepared the input columns. Deep learning learns its own features from raw images, audio and text. The benefit is accuracy on complex, unstructured data; the costs are more data, more compute and less explainability.

From deep learning to generative AI: creating instead of predicting

Most deep learning models are trained for one task and output a label or number ("defect" or "no defect"). Generative models are trained on vast, broad data, output new content, and can be steered to many tasks with a prompt. The benefit is flexibility and speed to build; the costs are hallucination, nondeterminism and per-token running costs.

From generative AI to agentic AI: acting toward a goal

A GenAI application responds: one prompt in, one answer out. An agent is given a goal and decides its own steps: it reasons with a foundation model, calls tools, checks results and repeats. The benefit is automating multi-step work; the costs are less predictable behavior, more model calls (so more cost and time) and new risks, because an agent that can act can also act wrongly.

What they have in common

  • All of them are AI. Each inner term is still AI; generative AI is also ML and deep learning.
  • All ML-based types learn from data, so all inherit data problems: missing, outdated or biased data gives poor or unfair results.
  • All produce probabilistic outputs. A prediction is a likelihood, generated text is the likeliest continuation, and an agent's plan is the model's best guess. None is guaranteed correct, so all need evaluation and monitoring.
  • All follow the same basic lifecycle: data, training (by you or by a model provider), evaluation, deployment, inference and monitoring.

One problem, five approaches

A parcel delivery company wants to handle customer messages about late deliveries. Each level of AI solves a different part of it:

Rule-based AI

If a message contains "late" or "where is", send the tracking link. Cheap, predictable, fully explainable, but misses "my parcel never came" and can't handle anything unexpected.

Machine learning

A classifier trained on 200,000 past messages, each labeled by the team that handled it, routes each new message to "delivery", "billing" or "damage" far more accurately than keywords.

Deep learning

A speech recognition model transcribes customers' voicemails, so they can be routed too. (In practice this is Amazon Transcribe, a deep learning service.)

Generative AI

A foundation model reads the message and the tracking history and drafts a personal, accurate reply for an agent to approve.

Agentic AI

An agent checks the tracking system, sees the parcel has been stuck at a depot for four days, opens a carrier investigation, books a redelivery, issues a $10 credit (within its limit) and replies to the customer, escalating to a person only if the parcel is high value.

Match the clue to the level. "Fixed rules", "if-then" or "deterministic logic" means rule-based AI. "Learns from labeled historical data" means ML. "Images, speech, unstructured data, neural network" means deep learning. "Creates new content, prompts, foundation model" means generative AI. "Autonomously plans, uses tools, takes actions, multi-step" means agentic AI.

Agentic AI isn't a separate kind of model. Agents use the same foundation models as generative AI applications; what makes them agentic is the goal, tools, memory and loop around the model.

Types of inferencing

Skill 1.1.3Describe various types of inferencing (for example, batch, real-time, asynchronous, serverless)

Once a model is trained, it has to be hosted somewhere so applications can send it data and get predictions back. The four types differ in how quickly the answer is needed, how big each request is, how steady the traffic is and whether you pay for capacity when nothing is happening. Amazon SageMaker AI offers all four as deployment options for models you host; Amazon Bedrock offers real-time (on-demand) and batch inference for foundation models.

Real-time inference

The model runs on an always-on endpoint. An application sends one request, waits, and gets the prediction back within milliseconds to seconds. This is the pattern whenever someone or something is waiting for the answer: approving a card payment at checkout, recommending products while a page loads, answering a chatbot message.

Because the endpoint is always running, you pay for the instances every hour, whether or not requests arrive. It can scale out automatically as traffic grows. On SageMaker AI, a real-time request can carry up to 6 MB and must be answered within 60 seconds; responses can also be streamed back piece by piece, which is how chat applications show text as it is generated.

Best forLow latency, steady or predictable traffic, interactive applications.

Serverless inference

You deploy the model without choosing or managing instances. AWS starts capacity when requests arrive, scales it with traffic and scales to zero when idle, and you pay only for the compute used while processing requests. The trade-off is the cold start: after an idle period, the first request waits while capacity starts up, so occasional responses are slower.

On SageMaker AI, serverless endpoints accept payloads up to 4 MB, must respond within 60 seconds, use 1 to 6 GB of memory and don't support GPUs, so they suit smaller models. Provisioned concurrency can keep some capacity warm to avoid cold starts. Example: an internal tool that classifies expense receipts, used heavily at month end and barely at all otherwise.

Best forIntermittent or unpredictable traffic with idle periods, where some cold-start delay is acceptable and you want no infrastructure to manage.

Asynchronous inference

Requests are placed in a queue instead of being answered immediately. The application uploads the input to Amazon S3 and sends a request; the endpoint processes queued requests in order and writes each result to S3, optionally sending a notification (through Amazon SNS) when it is done. The caller doesn't wait on an open connection.

It is built for large inputs and long processing: on SageMaker AI, payloads up to 1 GB and up to one hour of processing per request. The endpoint can scale to zero when the queue is empty, saving cost. Examples: transcribing and analyzing a one-hour video, running a large computer vision model on high-resolution satellite images, processing a long document when a user uploads it.

Best forLarge payloads, long processing times, near-real-time needs where waiting minutes is fine.

Batch inference

A whole dataset that already exists is processed in one job, with no endpoint at all. You point the job at the data in Amazon S3; AWS starts compute, runs predictions on every record, writes the results to S3 and shuts down. You pay only while the job runs. On SageMaker AI this is called batch transform.

Examples: scoring every customer each night for churn risk; generating next week's demand forecast for every product and store; tagging an archive of a million product images; on Amazon Bedrock, summarizing 100,000 support tickets with a foundation model. Amazon Bedrock batch inference takes a file of prompts in S3 and costs about 50% less than on-demand prices for supported models, because results aren't needed immediately.

Best forLarge volumes, no need for an immediate answer, scheduled or one-off jobs, lowest cost.

Inference for foundation models on Amazon Bedrock

Bedrock is serverless from your point of view: you call a model through an API and pay per token, with no endpoints to manage. On-demand requests are real-time; batch inference handles large offline jobs. Bedrock also offers service tiers for on-demand calls (Priority for the fastest responses at a premium, Standard as the default, Flex at a discount for work that can wait longer) and Provisioned Throughput, where you buy dedicated capacity by the hour. Task 2.3 covers these cost choices.

Read the requirement words. "Immediately", "while the user waits", "low latency", "milliseconds" means real-time. "Large payloads", "long processing time", "minutes", "queue" means asynchronous. "Intermittent", "unpredictable traffic", "idle periods", "no infrastructure to manage" and "tolerates cold starts" means serverless. "Entire dataset", "nightly", "no need for a persistent endpoint", "offline" means batch.

Batch and asynchronous inference are both "not real time", but they are different. Batch processes a whole dataset you already have, as one job, with no endpoint. Asynchronous handles individual requests as they arrive, queues them and notifies you when each result is ready.

Types of data

Skill 1.1.4Describe the different types of data in AI models (for example, labeled and unlabeled, tabular, time-series, image, text, structured and unstructured)

The type of data decides which kind of learning is possible, which kind of model fits, and how much work is needed before training. The exam's terms describe data in three different ways, which overlap: whether it has labels, how organized it is, and what form it takes.

Whether it has answers: labeled and unlabeled

Labeled data

Each example comes with the correct answer the model should learn to predict. An email marked "spam" or "not spam"; a photo of a part tagged "cracked"; a past loan with its outcome, "repaid" or "defaulted". Labeled data is what supervised learning needs.

Labels are often the most expensive part of a project. Some come for free from what happened (whether a customer cancelled), but many need people to look at each example: radiologists marking scans, reviewers tagging documents. Labels must also be consistent: if two reviewers disagree about what counts as "damaged", the model learns that confusion.

Unlabeled data

Raw data with no answers attached: millions of customer transactions, website clickstreams, a library of documents, hours of call recordings. It is plentiful and cheap. It can be used directly by unsupervised learning (to find groups or anomalies), and it is what foundation models are pre-trained on: an LLM learns from unlabeled text by predicting the next word, so the text itself supplies the answers (self-supervised learning).

SageMaker Ground Truth is no longer open to new customers. It was AWS's managed data labeling service, combining human labelers (your own staff, vendors or a public workforce) with automatic labeling. Existing customers can keep using it, with no new features planned. Older questions about "creating labeled training data with human labelers" expect Ground Truth.

How organized it is: structured and unstructured

Structured data

Data organized in a fixed format of rows and columns with defined types, as in a database table or spreadsheet: each row a customer, each column a fact such as age, plan or monthly spend. It is easy to search, filter and feed to classic ML algorithms. Most business records (orders, payments, inventory, sensor readings) are structured.

Unstructured data

Data with no predefined format: emails, documents, PDFs, images, audio, video, social media posts. Most of an organization's data is unstructured. Traditionally it was hard to use; deep learning and foundation models changed that, and a common use of AI is to turn unstructured data into structured data (for example, Amazon Textract reading invoice totals into a table, or Amazon Comprehend pulling names and dates from emails).

Semi-structured data Extra

Data with some organization but no fixed table shape, such as JSON, XML or log files: each record has labeled fields, but records can have different fields. Common in application logs and APIs.

What form it takes

Tabular data

Structured data in a table. Each row is one example and each column a feature, with one column often the label. It is the most common data in business ML: churn prediction, credit scoring, price estimation, fraud detection. Tree-based algorithms such as XGBoost typically work best on it.

Time-series data

Measurements recorded in time order, where the order matters: hourly electricity use, daily sales per store, a machine's temperature every second, a stock price every minute. Models learn trends (growing over time), seasonality (higher every December, lower every Sunday) and how past values predict future ones. Uses: demand forecasting, capacity planning, predictive maintenance, anomaly detection on sensors.

The key rule: never shuffle time-series data or test on the past. A forecasting model must be trained on earlier periods and tested on later ones, otherwise it "sees the future" in training and looks better than it is.

Image data

Photos, scans, video frames and medical images, stored as grids of pixel values. Uses: defect detection, product recognition, medical imaging, document scanning, content moderation. Image models are almost always deep learning (especially convolutional networks), and labeled image data often needs boxes or outlines drawn around objects, not just a single tag.

Text data

Free-form language: emails, reviews, chat logs, contracts, support tickets, web pages. Text has to be converted into numbers before a model can use it: split into tokens and turned into vectors (embeddings). Uses: sentiment analysis, classification, search, summarization, question answering and all LLM applications.

Audio and video Extra

Sound recordings and moving images, both unstructured and large. Audio is often transcribed to text first (Amazon Transcribe); video is analyzed frame by frame or with video models (Amazon Rekognition Video).

The categories overlap. A table of customer records is tabular, structured and, if it has a churn column, labeled. A folder of call recordings is audio, unstructured and unlabeled. A question can describe the same data set in more than one way.

Types of learning

Skill 1.1.5Describe different types of AI/ML learning (for example, supervised learning, unsupervised learning, reinforcement learning methods)

The three types differ in what the model learns from: correct answers, the structure of the data itself, or rewards from trial and error. The question to ask of any scenario is "what does the data look like, and is there a known right answer?"

Supervised learning

Learning from labeled examples

The model is trained on examples paired with the correct answer, and learns to map inputs to outputs. It is "supervised" because the labels act like a teacher marking each guess. After training, it predicts the answer for new, unlabeled cases. Most business ML is supervised.

It has two main forms:

  • Classification: predicting a category. Spam or not spam; fraud or genuine; which of five product types an image shows; will this customer churn (yes or no). With two categories it is binary classification; with more, multi-class.
  • Regression: predicting a number. A house's sale price; next month's sales; how many minutes a delivery will take; a customer's lifetime value.

NeedsLabeled data, often a lot of it, and labels that are accurate and consistent.

Unsupervised learning

Finding structure without labels

The model is given unlabeled data and finds patterns by itself. There is no right answer to learn; the value is in discovering structure people didn't define in advance. Its main forms:

  • Clustering: grouping similar items. A retailer groups customers by buying behavior into segments such as "bargain hunters" and "weekly families", then designs offers for each. The algorithm finds the groups; people then interpret and name them.
  • Anomaly detection: finding items unlike the rest. Unusual network traffic, a sensor reading that doesn't fit its normal pattern, an expense claim unlike any other. Useful when the bad cases are rare or new, so there aren't enough labeled examples of them.
  • Dimensionality reduction: compressing many features into a few that keep most of the information, to simplify data or visualize it.
  • Association: finding items that occur together, such as products often bought in the same basket.

NeedsOnly data, which makes it cheap to start, but the results need human interpretation and are harder to evaluate because there is no answer key.

Reinforcement learning

Learning from rewards by trial and error

An agent takes actions in an environment, receives a reward (or penalty) for each outcome, and learns a strategy (a policy) that maximizes total reward over time. Nobody provides the right answer for each situation; the agent discovers what works by trying things.

It suits problems made of sequences of decisions, where the effect of an action appears later: a robot learning to walk, a game-playing program, a warehouse system deciding which orders to pick first, dynamic pricing, or traffic-light timing. Training usually happens in a simulation, because trial and error in the real world is slow, expensive or dangerous. AWS DeepRacer, a small self-driving race car trained in simulation, was AWS's teaching tool for it; the console service closed on December 15, 2025, and it now continues as an open-source solution you deploy in your own account.

Link to generative AIReinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) uses this idea to make LLMs more helpful and safe: people rank model answers, and the model is rewarded for producing the kind of answers people prefer. Task 3.3 covers it.

Other learning approaches Extra

Self-supervised learning

The data creates its own labels: hide part of the input and train the model to predict it. LLMs are pre-trained this way, predicting the next word of billions of sentences. It is how foundation models learn from huge unlabeled data sets.

Semi-supervised learning

A small amount of labeled data combined with a large amount of unlabeled data, useful when labels are expensive: label 1,000 scans by hand, then use them to help learn from 100,000 unlabeled ones.

Choosing the type from a scenario

A bank with five years of loans, each marked repaid or defaulted

Labeled, so supervised; the answer is a category, so classification.

An energy company predicting tomorrow's demand in megawatts

A number learned from past demand, so supervised regression on time-series data.

A streaming service wanting to discover viewer groups it doesn't know about yet

No labels and no predefined groups, so unsupervised clustering.

A factory spotting unusual vibration in machines that have never failed before

No examples of failures to label, so unsupervised anomaly detection.

A logistics company teaching a robot arm to stack boxes

A sequence of actions judged by results (did the stack stand?), so reinforcement learning, trained in simulation.

"Grouping customers into segments" is clustering (unsupervised), even though the output looks like categories. Classification needs predefined categories and labeled examples of each; clustering discovers the groups.

Key takeaways

  • AI is the umbrella (including rules); ML learns from data; deep learning uses many-layered neural networks and learns its own features; generative AI creates content with foundation models; agentic AI wraps a foundation model in goals, tools, memory and a loop so it can act.
  • An algorithm is the method, training adjusts parameters on data, the model is the trained result, and inferencing is using it on new data. Inference is the running cost.
  • Computer vision interprets images and video (Rekognition, Textract); NLP understands and produces language (Comprehend, Translate, Transcribe, Polly, Lex); LLMs are transformer foundation models that predict the next token.
  • Bias means unfair outcomes for groups, or statistical error from an over-simple model. Fairness compares outcomes across groups. Overfitting is good on training data and poor on new data; underfitting is poor on both.
  • Real-time inference answers in milliseconds from an always-on endpoint; serverless scales to zero for intermittent traffic but has cold starts; asynchronous queues large or slow requests; batch processes a whole dataset as a job with no endpoint.
  • Labeled data has answers (supervised learning); unlabeled data doesn't (unsupervised and pre-training). Structured data has fixed rows and columns; unstructured data (text, images, audio) needs deep learning. Time-series data must be split by time, never shuffled.
  • Supervised learning predicts categories (classification) or numbers (regression) from labeled examples; unsupervised learning finds clusters, anomalies and associations; reinforcement learning learns a policy from rewards, and RLHF applies it to LLMs.
