Overview

Task 4.2

Recognize the importance of transparent and explainable models.

People affected by an AI decision increasingly expect, and in many areas have a legal right, to know that AI was involved and why it decided as it did. Regulators expect organizations to document how their models were built and tested. And teams can't fix or trust a model whose behavior they can't understand. This task covers what makes a model transparent and explainable, the AWS and open-source tools that help, the costs of transparency, and how to design for it. The running example is a lender that uses AI to assess loan applications and a generative assistant to explain decisions to applicants.

Transparent and explainable vs not

Skill 4.2.1Describe the differences between models that are transparent and explainable and models that are not transparent and explainable

The two words are related but distinct. Transparency is about what you can know about the system: how it was built, from what data, for what purpose, with what limitations and test results. Explainability is about understanding its outputs: why it produced this particular result. A third term, interpretability, describes how far a person can understand a model's inner workings directly, which is a property of the model type.

Transparent and explainable models

The model's design, data and behavior are documented and can be inspected, and its decisions can be traced to the factors that drove them. Interpretable by design ("glass-box") models are the clearest case:

  • Linear and logistic regression: each feature has a weight showing how much it pushes the result up or down ("each additional $10,000 of existing debt lowers the approval score by 4 points").
  • Decision trees: the decision is a readable series of rules ("debt-to-income above 45% and fewer than 2 years at current employer: refer to underwriter").
  • Rule-based systems: fully specified logic.

For any decision, you can say exactly why: "declined mainly because of a debt-to-income ratio of 52% and two missed payments in the last year". This supports regulatory explanations, appeals, debugging and bias checks.

Models that aren't transparent or explainable ("black boxes")

Complex models whose internal reasoning can't be followed: deep neural networks, large ensembles, and foundation models with billions of parameters. Their outputs arise from millions of interacting weights, so nobody can point to "the reason" for a result. For proprietary foundation models, transparency is also limited from outside: the training data, architecture details and weights aren't published.

Black-box models are often more accurate on complex tasks, especially with images, text and audio. Their explainability can be improved after the fact with post-hoc explanation techniques (below), but these approximate the model's behavior rather than reveal its true logic. A foundation model can also be asked to explain its answer, but that explanation is generated text and may not reflect how the answer was actually produced.

Why the difference matters

  • Regulation and rights: lending, insurance, hiring and public services often require reasons for decisions about individuals.
  • Trust and adoption: staff and customers accept decisions they understand.
  • Debugging and improvement: knowing why a model fails shows what to fix.
  • Fairness: seeing which factors drive decisions reveals proxy discrimination.
  • Accountability: documentation shows who built what, how, and with what testing.
Post-hoc explanation techniques Extra

Explaining black-box models

  • SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations): assigns each feature a contribution to a specific prediction, showing which factors pushed it up or down and by how much. Used for both individual decisions and overall feature importance.
  • LIME: fits a simple, interpretable model around one prediction to approximate why it was made.
  • Feature importance and partial dependence plots: show which features matter most overall and how the prediction changes as one feature changes.
  • For generative AI: citations to retrieved sources (RAG), showing the steps of an agent's reasoning and tool calls (traces), and Automated Reasoning checks that explain why an answer conflicts with a policy.

"Must explain each decision to customers or regulators" points to an interpretable model (linear model, decision tree) or post-hoc explanations such as SHAP. "Document how a model was built, its intended use and risks" points to transparency, using model cards.

Tools for transparency and explainability

Skill 4.2.2Describe tools to identify transparent and explainable models (for example, Amazon SageMaker Model Cards, Amazon Bedrock Model Evaluations, open source models, data, licensing)

Amazon SageMaker Model Cards

A SageMaker AI feature for documenting a model in a standard, structured format, a single source of truth about the model across its life. A model card records:

  • Model overview: what the model is, its version, owner and algorithm.
  • Intended uses: what it is for and not for ("assess personal loan applications up to $50,000; not for mortgages or business loans"), and who uses it.
  • Risk rating: unknown, low, medium or high, with the reasoning.
  • Training details: training data, objective and method.
  • Evaluation results: metrics, including results per group.
  • Additional information: ethical considerations, caveats, recommendations.

Model cards are versioned (each edit creates a new version, preserving history), can be exported as PDF to share with auditors and risk teams, and are linked with SageMaker Model Registry so documentation travels with each registered model version. They support governance reviews and regulatory evidence.

Amazon Bedrock Evaluations

Transparency about how well a model actually performs: Bedrock Evaluations produces evaluation reports comparing models on accuracy, robustness and toxicity, LLM-as-a-judge scores for qualities such as correctness, faithfulness, harmfulness and stereotyping (each with the judge's explanation), and human reviewers' ratings. These results document a model's strengths and weaknesses on your use case and can be recorded in model cards and governance records.

Open source models

Models whose weights (and sometimes code and training details) are published allow inspection that closed models don't: researchers and your own team can examine the architecture, test behavior in depth, apply explanation techniques to internal layers, reproduce results and run the model in an environment you control. Openness varies widely: "open weights" doesn't mean the training data is published, so check exactly what is disclosed. Many open models are available on Bedrock, SageMaker JumpStart and Hugging Face.

Data transparency

Knowing what data a model was trained and evaluated on is central to judging its biases, limitations and legal risk. Look for documentation of data sources, collection methods, time periods, demographic coverage, filtering and licensing: "datasheets" for data sets, data lineage records in your own pipelines (AWS Glue Data Catalog, Amazon SageMaker Catalog), and providers' published information about their training data.

Licensing

A model's license is part of its transparency: it states what the model may be used for (commercial use, user limits, prohibited uses), whether modifications must be shared, and whether attribution is required. Clear, well-understood licenses (such as Apache 2.0 or MIT) make obligations transparent; custom licenses need legal review. Licensing of training data matters too, since it affects IP risk.

AWS AI Service Cards Extra

AWS's own transparency documentation for its AI services and models (for example, Amazon Rekognition face matching, Amazon Textract, Amazon Nova): intended use cases and limitations, responsible AI design choices, and best practices for deploying and optimizing performance. They are the AWS equivalent of a model card for its managed services.

"Document a model's intended use, risk rating and evaluation results for auditors" is SageMaker Model Cards. "AWS's documentation of its own AI services' intended uses and limitations" is AI Service Cards. "Compare models' accuracy and toxicity" is Bedrock Evaluations. "Inspect the weights and run the model yourself" means an open source model.

Safety and transparency trade-offs

Skill 4.2.3Identify tradeoffs between model safety and transparency (for example, measure interpretability and performance)

More transparency isn't free. It can conflict with performance, with safety and security, and with privacy. Responsible teams make these trade-offs deliberately, and measure them rather than assume.

Interpretability versus performance

Simpler, interpretable models are easier to explain but often less accurate on complex problems; complex models are more accurate but harder to explain. For the lender, a logistic regression might reach 82% accuracy with fully explainable decisions, while a gradient-boosted ensemble reaches 86% but needs SHAP to explain them.

Measure both before choosing: evaluate accuracy (or AUC, F1) for each candidate on the same test data, and assess interpretability (can a reviewer explain a sample of decisions? how many features or rules drive them?). Then ask whether the accuracy gain is worth the loss of explainability for this use. Sometimes the gap is small enough that the interpretable model wins; sometimes a complex model with good post-hoc explanations is acceptable; for high-stakes decisions regulators may require the interpretable one.

Transparency versus safety and security

Revealing too much can help attackers or cause harm:

  • Publishing system prompts and guardrail rules shows people exactly how to get around them.
  • Exposing detailed confidence scores or explanations lets attackers probe the model to reverse-engineer it or craft inputs that evade it (a fraudster learning which factors avoid a fraud flag).
  • Releasing model weights openly enables inspection, but also lets others remove safety training or misuse the model.
  • Disclosing training data could expose personal or confidential information in it.

The balance: be transparent about purpose, capabilities, limitations, data categories and testing; protect details that would enable misuse; and give fuller access to auditors and regulators under controlled conditions.

Transparency versus privacy and IP

Explanations of a decision can reveal information about other people in the training data, and full disclosure of a model or data can expose trade secrets. Explanations should be at the level of the individual's own factors, not raw data.

"Always use the most explainable model" isn't right, and neither is "always use the most accurate". The answer depends on the stakes: measure accuracy and interpretability, then choose what the use case and regulations require.

Human-centered design for explainable AI

Skill 4.2.4Describe principles of human-centered design for explainable AI (for example, user-feedback mechanisms, AI decision transparency)

Human-centered design starts from the people who use or are affected by an AI system, not from the technology: what they need to understand, decide and do, and how the AI should support them. For explainable AI, it means explanations are designed for their audience and people stay in control.

AI decision transparency

People should know when AI is involved and understand its role and its results:

  • Disclose AI use: tell applicants their application is assessed with an automated model, and customers that they are chatting with an AI assistant.
  • Explain in terms the audience understands: an applicant needs the main reasons in plain language ("your existing debt is high relative to your income"); an underwriter needs feature contributions; an auditor needs the model card and test results.
  • Make explanations actionable: say what would change the outcome where possible ("reducing your card balance below $3,000 would improve your assessment").
  • Show confidence and sources: indicate uncertainty, and for generative answers cite the documents used so people can verify them.
  • Be honest about limitations: state what the system can't do and when to consult a person.

User-feedback mechanisms

Ways for people to respond to the AI, which both respect them and improve the system:

  • In-product feedback: thumbs up or down, ratings, "this answer was wrong" with a reason.
  • Corrections and overrides: staff can correct or override the AI's output, and the correction is recorded.
  • Contest and appeal: applicants can challenge an automated decision and have a person review it.
  • Closing the loop: feedback is reviewed, used to find failures, added to evaluation test sets and used to improve prompts, data or models, and users see that their feedback leads to change.

Other human-centered principles

  • Human oversight and control: people make or approve high-impact decisions and can stop the system.
  • Appropriate trust: design so people neither blindly trust nor ignore the AI, for example by showing confidence and highlighting cases needing review.
  • Accessibility and inclusivity: explanations that work for people with different abilities, languages and levels of expertise.
  • Involve users in design: test explanations with real users and affected groups, and iterate.

"Let users rate or correct answers" is a user-feedback mechanism. "Tell users they are interacting with AI and why a decision was made" is AI decision transparency. "Allow customers to appeal to a human" is human oversight.

Key takeaways

  • Transparency is knowing how a system was built, trained and tested; explainability is understanding why it produced an output; interpretable models (linear, decision trees) explain themselves, while black boxes (deep networks, foundation models) need post-hoc techniques such as SHAP, citations or traces.
  • Tools: SageMaker Model Cards (intended use, risk rating, training and evaluation details, versioned, exportable), Bedrock Evaluations reports, open source models (inspectable), documented data and lineage, clear licensing, and AWS AI Service Cards.
  • Trade-offs: interpretability versus performance (measure both on the same data), transparency versus safety and security (don't reveal what enables misuse), and transparency versus privacy and IP.
  • Human-centered design: disclose AI use, explain decisions in the audience's terms with actionable reasons, sources and confidence; provide feedback, correction and appeal; keep humans in control.
