Overview

Task 3.4

Describe methods to evaluate FM performance.

Evaluating generative AI is harder than evaluating a classifier. There is rarely one correct answer: many different summaries of a document can all be good. So evaluation combines several methods: automatic metrics that compare outputs with reference answers, human judgment, other models acting as judges, and business measurements once real users arrive. The exam expects you to know what each method and metric measures and when to use it. The running example is a travel company's assistant that summarizes bookings, translates messages and answers questions from its policy documents.

Approaches to evaluation

Skill 3.4.1Determine approaches to evaluate FM performance (for example, human-in-the-loop evaluation, benchmark datasets, Amazon Bedrock Model Evaluation)

Human-in-the-loop evaluation

People review model outputs and judge their quality: accuracy, helpfulness, tone, style, safety, whether they follow brand guidelines. Reviewers might rate each answer on a scale, give a thumbs up or down, or compare two models' answers to the same prompt and pick the better one. Domain experts are needed where correctness requires expertise (a lawyer reviewing contract summaries, a clinician reviewing medical answers).

Human evaluation captures what automatic metrics miss: subtle errors, whether an answer is actually useful, whether the tone suits the customer. It is the gold standard for subjective qualities, but it is slow, expensive, hard to scale and can vary between reviewers, so it needs clear rating guidelines and is usually applied to a sample. "Human in the loop" also describes ongoing review in production, where people check low-confidence or high-risk outputs before they are used.

Benchmark datasets

Standardized collections of prompts with known correct answers or reference outputs, used to measure and compare models consistently. Public benchmarks test general abilities, for example knowledge across many academic subjects (MMLU), reading comprehension and question answering (BoolQ, TriviaQA, Natural Questions), summarization (Gigaword) and toxicity (RealToxicityPrompts, BOLD, which also tests bias across demographic groups). Holistic benchmark suites such as HELM test many dimensions at once.

Public benchmarks are useful for shortlisting models, but scores don't always predict performance on your task, and models may have seen benchmark questions in training. The most valuable benchmark is your own: a curated set of real prompts from your use case with expert-approved answers, re-run every time you change the model, prompt or data.

Amazon Bedrock Evaluations (Model Evaluation)

A Bedrock capability to evaluate and compare models, and RAG systems, without building your own evaluation tooling. You choose the task type (general text generation, summarization, question answering, classification), the metrics, and either built-in datasets or your own prompt dataset in Amazon S3, and Bedrock runs the job and produces a report. It offers several evaluation types:

  • Automatic (programmatic) evaluation: algorithmic metrics for accuracy, robustness and toxicity, such as BERTScore for summaries, F1 for question answering, word error rate for robustness, and classification accuracy. Built-in datasets include BoolQ, TriviaQA, Natural Questions, Gigaword, RealToxicityPrompts and BOLD.
  • Human evaluation: your own team of reviewers rates outputs through a review interface, with methods such as thumbs up or down, Likert-scale ratings, side-by-side comparison of two models, and ranking. You define custom criteria such as brand voice.
  • LLM-as-a-judge: a judge model scores responses on quality metrics (correctness, completeness, faithfulness, helpfulness, logical coherence, relevance, following instructions, professional style and tone) and responsible AI metrics (harmfulness, stereotyping, refusal), with explanations; custom metrics are supported.
  • RAG evaluation: evaluates a knowledge base's retrieval alone, or retrieval plus generation, with metrics described in Evaluating FM applications.

You can also evaluate responses you generated elsewhere ("bring your own inference responses"), so applications built outside Bedrock can be scored the same way.

"Subjective qualities such as tone, brand voice, helpfulness" points to human evaluation (or LLM-as-a-judge at scale). "Compare models on standard tasks" points to benchmark datasets. "Managed way to compare FMs on Bedrock with automatic metrics, human reviewers or a judge model" points to Amazon Bedrock Evaluations.

Metrics for FM performance

Skill 3.4.2Identify relevant metrics to assess FM performance (for example, Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation [ROUGE], Bilingual Evaluation Understudy [BLEU], BERTScore, LLM-as-a-judge)

ROUGE, BLEU and BERTScore compare a model's output with one or more reference texts written by people. They differ in whether they count matching words or compare meaning. LLM-as-a-judge needs no reference at all.

ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation)

Measures how much of the reference text's content appears in the generated text, by counting overlapping words or word sequences (n-grams). It is recall-oriented: it asks "how much of what should be there did the output capture?", which suits summarization, where a summary should contain the key points of the reference.

  • ROUGE-N: overlap of n-grams: ROUGE-1 counts single words, ROUGE-2 pairs of consecutive words.
  • ROUGE-L: the longest common subsequence, which rewards getting words in the right order.
ROUGE-1 worked example
Reference summary
the flight was cancelled due to weather (7 words)
Generated summary
the flight is cancelled (4 words)
Matching words
the, flight, cancelled = 3
ROUGE-1 recall
3 ÷ 7 = 0.43 (missed "due to weather")
ROUGE-1 precision
3 ÷ 4 = 0.75

LimitationCounts words, not meaning: "the trip was called off because of a storm" says the same thing but scores near zero.

BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy)

Measures how many of the generated text's word sequences (1 to 4 words long) also appear in the reference translations. It is precision-oriented: it asks "how much of what the model wrote is correct?", and adds a brevity penalty so a model can't score well by writing very short outputs. It was designed for, and is mainly used for, machine translation. Scores range from 0 to 1 (often shown as 0 to 100); multiple reference translations make it fairer, since a sentence can be translated correctly in many ways.

LimitationLike ROUGE, it rewards exact wording, so a valid translation using different words scores poorly; it is best for comparing systems on the same test set, not judging single sentences.

BERTScore

Measures semantic similarity between the generated and reference text using embeddings from a BERT-style language model. Each word in one text is matched to its most similar word in the other by meaning (cosine similarity of their embeddings), so synonyms and paraphrases count: "cancelled" matches "called off", "storm" matches "bad weather". It reports precision, recall and F1, and correlates better with human judgment than word-overlap metrics. Amazon Bedrock Evaluations uses BERTScore for summarization accuracy.

LimitationNeeds a reference text, and high similarity doesn't guarantee factual correctness (a summary with one wrong date can still score high).

LLM-as-a-judge

A capable foundation model evaluates another model's outputs against criteria you define: correctness, completeness, faithfulness to a source, helpfulness, coherence, relevance, tone, harmfulness. The judge receives the prompt, the response (and, if available, a reference answer or source documents) and a grading rubric, and returns a score with an explanation. It approximates human evaluation for open-ended outputs where there is no single right answer, at a fraction of the cost and time, so thousands of outputs can be scored.

LimitationsJudges can be biased (for example, favoring longer answers or their own model family's style), can make mistakes and cost tokens. Calibrate them against a sample of human ratings, use clear rubrics, and spot-check with people. Amazon Bedrock Evaluations and AgentCore Evaluations provide built-in LLM-as-a-judge metrics.

Other metrics you may see Extra
  • Perplexity: how "surprised" a language model is by text; lower means it predicts the text better. Used in training, not for judging answers.
  • F1 and exact match: word overlap between a short answer and the reference, used for question answering.
  • Word error rate: used for speech recognition, and by Bedrock to measure robustness (how much answers change when prompts contain small errors).
  • Toxicity scores: how likely an output is harmful, measured by a classifier.

Summarization points to ROUGE (recall of content). Translation points to BLEU (precision with a brevity penalty). "Meaning rather than exact words", "paraphrases" point to BERTScore. "No reference answer", "open-ended", "scale up human-like judgment" point to LLM-as-a-judge.

Meeting business objectives

Skill 3.4.3Determine whether an FM effectively meets business objectives (for example, productivity, user engagement, task engineering)

A model can score well on ROUGE and still fail the business. Technical metrics measure outputs; business evaluation measures outcomes. Define the objective and how to measure it before launch, measure a baseline without the AI, and compare.

Productivity

Does the model help people get more done, faster or with less effort? Measures: time per task (agents write booking change confirmations in 2 minutes instead of 8), tasks completed per person per day, share of work automated, backlog reduction, and time to resolution. Measure net effect: if staff spend extra time correcting the model's drafts, productivity gains shrink, so include review and rework time.

User engagement

Are people actually using it, and coming back? Measures: active users, sessions per user, messages per session, repeat use, feature adoption, and drop-off (users who abandon a conversation midway). Low engagement after launch often means answers aren't helpful enough, the tool doesn't fit how people work, or users don't trust it. Engagement is also a leading indicator: it moves before revenue or cost metrics do.

Task engineering

The exam guide's term isn't defined further. It is best read as how well the application's tasks are designed and performed: whether the business task has been broken into the right steps, prompts and checks, and whether the model completes each engineered task to the required standard. In practice this means defining each task the model does (classify the request, extract the booking reference, draft the reply), setting acceptance criteria for each, and measuring the model against them, then redesigning tasks that fail rather than only changing the model.

Strong benchmark or ROUGE scores don't prove business value. A question about whether the model meets business objectives expects business measurements such as productivity, engagement or completion rates, compared with a baseline.

Evaluating FM applications

Skill 3.4.4Identify approaches to evaluate the performance of applications built with FM (for example, RAG, agents, workflows)

An application's quality depends on every component, not just the model. Evaluating only the final answer tells you something is wrong but not where. Evaluate each component and the end-to-end result.

RAG applications

A RAG answer can fail in two places: retrieval found the wrong passages, or generation misused the right ones. Evaluate both.

Retrieval quality

  • Context relevance: are the retrieved passages relevant to the question?
  • Context coverage: do they contain all the information needed to answer (compared with a ground-truth answer)?

Poor retrieval is fixed in the retrieval pipeline: chunking strategy, embedding model, number of results, metadata filters, reranking.

Generation quality

  • Faithfulness (groundedness): is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved passages, with nothing invented? This is the key hallucination check.
  • Correctness and completeness: is the answer right and does it cover the whole question?
  • Answer relevance and helpfulness: does it actually address what was asked?
  • Citation precision and coverage: do the citations point to passages that support the claims, and are all claims cited?

On AWSAmazon Bedrock Evaluations runs RAG evaluation jobs: retrieve-only (context relevance, context coverage) or retrieve-and-generate (correctness, completeness, helpfulness, logical coherence, faithfulness, citation precision, citation coverage, harmfulness, stereotyping, refusal), using LLM-as-a-judge, for Bedrock Knowledge Bases or for responses from your own RAG system.

Agents

What to measure

An agent can reach a right answer by a wrong route (or a wrong answer through one bad step), so evaluate the path as well as the result:

  • Goal success (task completion): did the agent achieve what the user wanted across the whole session?
  • Tool selection accuracy: did it choose the right tool at each step?
  • Tool parameter accuracy: did it call tools with the right inputs (the right booking reference, the right date)?
  • Response quality: correctness, helpfulness, faithfulness and safety of each reply.
  • Efficiency: number of steps, model calls, tokens, latency and cost per task.
  • Safety and limits: did it stay within permissions, refuse what it should, and hand over to a person when required?

Evaluation uses traces, the recorded sequence of the agent's reasoning, tool calls and results, so each step can be inspected and scored. Test sets should include multi-step scenarios and edge cases, and simulated users can drive conversations automatically.

On AWSAmazon Bedrock AgentCore Evaluations scores agents from their traces with built-in LLM-as-a-judge evaluators at session level (goal success rate), per response (correctness, helpfulness, faithfulness, harmfulness and others) and per tool call (tool selection accuracy, tool parameter accuracy), plus custom evaluators. It runs on demand or continuously on a sample of live traffic. AgentCore Observability provides the traces and dashboards in Amazon CloudWatch.

Workflows

What to measure

Multi-step workflows (prompt chains, routing, parallel steps) are evaluated step by step and end to end:

  • Per step: does each step's output meet its acceptance criteria (the router picks the right category; the extractor gets the right fields)?
  • Error propagation: one early mistake can spoil every later step, so test where failures start.
  • End-to-end quality and success rate on realistic test cases.
  • Operational metrics: latency, failure and retry rates, and cost per run.

Evaluation as an ongoing practice

  1. Before launchBuild a test set of real scenarios; evaluate candidate models, prompts and configurations offline.
  2. At each changeRe-run the test set whenever the model, prompt, documents or tools change, to catch regressions.
  3. In productionSample and score live interactions (online evaluation), collect user feedback, monitor traces and business metrics.
  4. Feed backAdd new failure cases to the test set, then improve prompts, retrieval or the model.

Business alignment metrics

Skill 3.4.5Identify business objective alignment metrics for AI applications (for example, task completion rate, user satisfaction, cost per interaction)

These metrics connect an application's behavior to what the business cares about. They are measured on real usage and tracked over time.

Task completion rate

The share of interactions in which the user's goal was achieved: completed tasks ÷ attempted tasks. If 10,000 customers start a booking change with the assistant and 7,800 finish without needing an agent, the completion rate is 78%. It measures effectiveness directly and reveals where the application fails: which kinds of request end in abandonment or escalation. Related measures are containment rate (resolved without a human) and escalation rate.

User satisfaction

How users feel about the experience: ratings on individual answers (thumbs up or down), post-conversation surveys (CSAT, typically "how satisfied were you" on a 1 to 5 scale), Net Promoter Score, complaints and written feedback. Satisfaction catches problems completion rates miss, for example a task completed but slowly, rudely or after many attempts. Low ratings on specific answers become test cases.

Cost per interaction

Total cost of running the application ÷ number of interactions: model tokens, retrieval and vector database, other AWS services, monitoring, and human review. For the travel assistant: $12,000 a month for 300,000 conversations is $0.04 per conversation, compared with about $4 for a call handled by an agent. It shows whether the application is economical at scale, and is reduced through smaller models, shorter prompts, caching and fewer agent steps. Track it alongside quality, because cutting cost by degrading answers lowers completion and satisfaction.

Related metrics Extra
  • Average handling time for human agents assisted by AI.
  • First-contact resolution: issues solved in the first interaction.
  • Adoption rate among intended users.
  • Revenue metrics such as conversion and average order value, where the application supports sales.

"Did users accomplish what they came to do?" is task completion rate. "How did users feel about it?" is user satisfaction. "Is it economical at scale?" is cost per interaction. Good evaluation tracks all three together.

Key takeaways

  • Combine evaluation approaches: human-in-the-loop for subjective and expert judgment, benchmark datasets (public for shortlisting, your own for decisions), and Amazon Bedrock Evaluations for automatic, human, LLM-as-a-judge and RAG evaluation jobs.
  • ROUGE measures recall of reference content (summarization); BLEU measures precision of n-grams with a brevity penalty (translation); BERTScore measures meaning with embeddings; LLM-as-a-judge scores open-ended outputs against a rubric, calibrated against humans.
  • Business objectives are judged by productivity, user engagement and how well each engineered task meets its acceptance criteria, compared with a baseline.
  • Evaluate RAG on retrieval (context relevance, coverage) and generation (faithfulness, correctness, citations); agents on goal success, tool selection and parameters, efficiency and safety, using traces (AgentCore Evaluations); workflows step by step and end to end.
  • Business alignment metrics: task completion rate, user satisfaction and cost per interaction, tracked together on real usage.
