Overview

Task 3.3

Describe the training and fine-tuning process for FMs.

Prompting and RAG change what a model sees; training changes the model itself, by adjusting its weights. This task is about the different kinds of training, what each needs and achieves, and why data preparation decides the result. You need to recognize which method a scenario describes and what data it requires, not to run training jobs. The running example is a health insurer that wants a model to answer members' benefit questions and summarize claims in its own style and terminology.

Key elements of training an FM

Skill 3.3.1Describe the key elements of training an FM (for example, pre-training, fine-tuning, continuous pre-training, distillation)

The four elements differ in their starting point, the data they use and what they change. In the order a model's life usually goes: pre-training creates a general model; continued pre-training deepens its knowledge of a domain; fine-tuning teaches it specific tasks and behavior; distillation transfers its abilities into a smaller model.

Pre-training

Training a model from scratch, starting with random weights, on an enormous, broad data set: trillions of tokens of web text, books, code, scientific articles and, for multimodal models, images and other media. It uses self-supervised learning: the data supplies its own answers, as the model learns to predict the next token (or hidden parts of the input) across all that text. In doing so it learns grammar, facts, reasoning patterns and how to write code.

Pre-training is where nearly all of a foundation model's general capability comes from, and nearly all of its cost: thousands of GPUs or AI accelerators (such as AWS Trainium) running for weeks or months, huge data pipelines and specialist teams. A pre-trained ("base") model can continue text but isn't yet good at following instructions or being safe; that comes from later training. Very few organizations pre-train; most start from an existing model.

Fine-tuning

Further training of a pre-trained model on a much smaller, task-specific, usually labeled data set: pairs of prompts and ideal responses. It adjusts the model's weights so it performs a particular task better or behaves in a particular way: following the insurer's answer format, using its terminology, summarizing claims in its house style, classifying messages into its categories.

Fine-tuning needs far less data and compute than pre-training (hundreds to tens of thousands of examples, hours rather than months) and produces a private copy of the model. It changes behavior and style well; it is a poor way to add facts that change, which is RAG's job.

On AWSAmazon Bedrock supports supervised fine-tuning of selected models (including Amazon Nova, Meta Llama and Anthropic Claude Haiku models) from training data in Amazon S3, and also reinforcement fine-tuning, which improves a model using a reward function that scores its answers instead of labeled answers. SageMaker AI and SageMaker JumpStart fine-tune open-weight models with full control over the process.

Continuous (continued) pre-training

Continuing the pre-training process on an existing foundation model, using large amounts of unlabeled text from a particular domain: medical journals, legal judgments, financial filings, or a company's internal documents. It uses the same self-supervised next-token objective as pre-training, so no labels are needed. The model absorbs the domain's vocabulary, concepts and knowledge, becoming fluent in, say, clinical and insurance terminology.

It sits between pre-training and fine-tuning: it starts from a trained model (much cheaper than from scratch) but uses much more data than fine-tuning. It can also be repeated as new domain material accumulates, which is why it is called continuous. Often it is followed by fine-tuning for specific tasks. Amazon Bedrock offers continued pre-training for selected models; check the current list of supported models.

Distillation

Transferring knowledge from a large, capable teacher model to a smaller, faster student model. The teacher generates responses to many prompts typical of the use case; the student is trained on those responses, learning to imitate the teacher's outputs for that use case. The student can reach close to the teacher's quality on that task while being much cheaper and faster to run.

It is the answer when a large model does the job well but costs too much or responds too slowly at production volume. Amazon Bedrock Model Distillation automates it: you choose a teacher model and a smaller supported student model and provide prompts (or let it use your past invocation logs); Bedrock generates the teacher's responses, can create extra synthetic examples, and fine-tunes the student.

How they compare

What each starts from and needs

  • Pre-training: random weights; massive unlabeled data; extreme cost. Produces general capability.
  • Continued pre-training: a pre-trained model; large unlabeled domain data; high cost. Adds domain knowledge and language.
  • Fine-tuning: a pre-trained model; smaller labeled examples; moderate cost. Adds task skill and behavior.
  • Distillation: a teacher and a smaller student; prompts (responses come from the teacher); moderate cost. Produces a cheaper model with similar task quality.

"Unlabeled domain documents to teach industry terminology" means continued pre-training. "Labeled prompt-response pairs for a specific task" means fine-tuning. "Smaller, cheaper model that mimics a larger one" means distillation. "Build a model from scratch on massive data" means pre-training.

Methods for fine-tuning

Skill 3.3.2Define methods for fine-tuning an FM (for example, instruction tuning, adapting models for specific domains, transfer learning, continuous pre-training)

Instruction tuning

Fine-tuning on examples of instructions paired with the desired responses, across many kinds of task: "Summarize this…" with a good summary, "Classify this…" with the right label, "Explain…" with a clear explanation. It teaches the model to understand and follow instructions in general, which is what turns a raw pre-trained model (which just continues text) into a helpful assistant. Model providers instruction-tune their models before release; businesses use the same method on their own task examples to make a model follow their specific instructions and formats reliably.

Instruction-tuning examples in JSONL (one example per line) 
{"prompt": "Summarize this claim note for the member in plain English: Member presented with acute lumbar strain; PT x6 authorized under plan B2; copay applies.", "completion": "Your claim for back pain treatment is approved. It covers 6 physical therapy sessions. Your usual copay applies to each visit."}
{"prompt": "Classify the member message as billing, coverage or provider_search: Is acupuncture covered on my plan?", "completion": "coverage"}

Adapting models for specific domains

Specializing a general model for an industry or field, such as healthcare, law, finance or engineering, so it understands the domain's terminology, conventions and reasoning. It can be done with continued pre-training on unlabeled domain text, fine-tuning on labeled domain tasks, or both. A general model may confuse "PT" (physical therapy) with "part-time", or not know that "EOB" is an explanation of benefits; a domain-adapted model gets these right without being told each time.

Transfer learning

The general principle behind all fine-tuning: knowledge a model learned on one task or data set is transferred and reused for a different but related task, so the new task needs far less data and training. A model pre-trained on general language already knows grammar and world knowledge; fine-tuning transfers that to classifying insurance messages with only a few thousand examples. In computer vision, a model trained on millions of everyday images can be adapted to spot defects in X-rays of welds with a few hundred labeled images.

Transfer learning is why foundation models are economical: the expensive learning is done once and reused by many organizations for many tasks.

Continuous pre-training

Listed in the exam guide as a fine-tuning method as well as a training element, because it is a way of adapting an existing model: continuing its self-supervised training on unlabeled domain or company text so it absorbs new knowledge and vocabulary. Unlike instruction tuning, it needs no labels, and it updates knowledge rather than teaching task behavior.

Related techniques Extra

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) and LoRA

Instead of updating all of a model's billions of weights (full fine-tuning), PEFT methods train a small number of extra parameters and leave the original weights frozen. The most common, LoRA (low-rank adaptation), adds small adapter matrices. It cuts training cost and memory dramatically, and the small adapters are cheap to store and swap.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and reinforcement fine-tuning

Methods that improve a model by rewarding preferred responses rather than showing exact answers. RLHF is covered with data preparation below; reinforcement fine-tuning on Bedrock uses a reward function (code or a judge model) to score responses.

Catastrophic forgetting

A risk of fine-tuning: training heavily on a narrow task can make the model lose some general abilities it had. Mitigated by mixing in general examples, using PEFT, and evaluating general capabilities as well as the target task after training.

Preparing data for fine-tuning

Skill 3.3.3Describe how to prepare data to fine-tune an FM (for example, data curation, governance, size, labeling, representativeness, reinforcement learning from human feedback [RLHF])

A fine-tuned model learns exactly what its examples teach, including their mistakes, gaps and biases. Data preparation is therefore most of the work, and the biggest single factor in the result. Quality beats quantity: a few thousand excellent examples outperform many more mediocre ones.

Data curation

Selecting, cleaning and organizing the examples. Remove duplicates, errors, irrelevant and low-quality examples; fix inconsistent formatting; make sure each response is one you would be happy for the model to reproduce, because it will. For the insurer, that means using only claim summaries reviewed as accurate and on-brand, not every summary ever written. Curation also removes sensitive information that the model shouldn't learn, such as members' names and policy numbers.

Governance

Making sure the data may be used, and that its use is controlled and documented:

  • Rights and consent: the organization has the legal right to use the data for training (licenses, contracts, privacy law, member consent).
  • Privacy: personal and health information is removed, masked or properly authorized; regulations such as HIPAA or GDPR are respected.
  • Lineage and documentation: where each data set came from, how it was processed and which model versions used it, so results can be traced and audited.
  • Access control and security: training data is stored encrypted, with access restricted (for example, in Amazon S3 with IAM policies and AWS KMS keys).

Size

Enough examples to teach the task, but more isn't always better. Simple format or style tasks may need a few hundred examples; complex or highly varied tasks thousands or more. Too few examples and the model may overfit (memorizing them rather than learning the pattern) or barely change. Too many low-quality ones teach the wrong things and cost more to train. Hold back a validation set to measure whether the model generalizes, and each service sets minimum and maximum data set sizes.

Labeling

Creating the correct output for each example: the ideal response, the right category, the correct extraction. Labels must be accurate and consistent: if two reviewers would summarize the same claim differently, write labeling guidelines and check agreement between labelers, because the model learns the inconsistency. Labeling is usually done by domain experts (claims specialists, clinicians), sometimes helped by a model drafting labels for people to correct.

Representativeness

The examples should reflect the real inputs the model will see in production: all the claim types, member groups, languages, writing styles, lengths and edge cases, in realistic proportions. A data set of only simple, English-language dental claims produces a model that handles complex, Spanish-language hospital claims badly. Unrepresentative data also causes bias: groups under-represented in the examples get worse results. Check coverage of key groups and scenarios before training, and evaluate per group afterwards.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)

A way to align a model with human preferences (helpful, honest, harmless, in the right tone) when it is easier to judge answers than to write the perfect one. The process:

  1. GenerateThe model produces several responses to each of many prompts.
  2. RankHuman reviewers compare and rank the responses from best to worst.
  3. Train a reward modelA separate model learns to predict which responses people prefer.
  4. OptimizeThe foundation model is trained with reinforcement learning to produce responses the reward model scores highly.

RLHF is a major reason assistant models are helpful and refuse harmful requests. For data preparation, it means the data is human preference judgments rather than labeled answers, and reviewers need clear guidelines and a representative mix of backgrounds so the preferences learned aren't narrow or biased.

A data preparation checklist

  • Every example is accurate, high quality and in the exact format the service expects (on Bedrock, JSONL files in Amazon S3).
  • The organization has the right to use the data; personal data is removed or authorized.
  • Sources, processing and versions are documented.
  • Labels follow written guidelines and labelers agree.
  • The examples cover the real mix of inputs, groups and edge cases.
  • A held-back validation and test set exists to measure the result.

Adding more data doesn't fix bad data. If the fine-tuned model gives biased or wrong outputs, review the curation, labeling and representativeness of the training examples before collecting more.

Key takeaways

  • Pre-training builds general capability from scratch with self-supervised learning on massive unlabeled data; continued pre-training adds domain knowledge from unlabeled domain text; fine-tuning teaches tasks and behavior from labeled examples; distillation trains a small student to imitate a large teacher for cheaper inference.
  • Instruction tuning trains on instruction-response pairs to make models follow instructions; domain adaptation specializes a model for an industry; transfer learning reuses knowledge learned on one task for another, which is why fine-tuning needs little data.
  • Bedrock supports fine-tuning, reinforcement fine-tuning and distillation for selected models, with training data as JSONL in S3; SageMaker AI fine-tunes open-weight models with full control.
  • Prepare data with careful curation, governance (rights, privacy, lineage, security), the right size, consistent labeling and representativeness; quality beats quantity.
  • RLHF uses human rankings of responses to train a reward model, then optimizes the model toward preferred answers: helpful, honest and harmless.
