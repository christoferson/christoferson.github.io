Overview

Topic 5.1

Supply Chain Security. Image Repository.

The curriculum gives only these names. This page takes supply chain security to mean protecting every step from source code to running workload: the threats, SLSA levels, provenance, signatures, SBOMs, in-toto and TUF. It takes image repository to mean the registry side: Harbor and other registries, mirrors, and checking signatures before images run. Image basics (digests, pull secrets, scanning) are in topic 1.3; admission control in general is in topic 5.4; supply chain compliance is in topic 6.1.

Sources: kubernetes.io and the CNCF® TAG Security supply chain whitepaper (CC BY 4.0), slsa.dev, sigstore.dev, notaryproject.dev, in-toto.io, theupdateframework.io, the Harbor, containerd and Kyverno docs, and NIST and CISA publications.

Running example. The shop's CI builds shop/api and pushes it to the company's Harbor registry. The goal: every image is built by CI, signed, comes with an SBOM, and the cluster refuses anything else.

The software supply chain

Topic 5.1What the supply chain is, where it can be attacked, and recent examples.

The CNCF supply chain paper (v2, 2025): "Software supply chain attacks occur when the materials or processes of producing software are themselves compromised, resulting in vulnerabilities targeting downstream consumers." "The software supply chain consists of all of the steps taken to move code from source to production." It has five stages: source code, materials (dependencies), build pipelines, artifacts, and deployments and distribution.

SLSA threatWhereExample
A, B, CProducer, authoring and review, source controlA malicious change slips through review; the repository is compromised
DependenciesEvery upstream library, recursivelyA compromised open source package
D, EBuild parameters, build processThe build system itself is compromised
FArtifact publicationA different artifact is published than was built
G, H, IDistribution channel, package selection, usageA compromised registry or mirror; typosquatting
  • SolarWinds (2020): attackers had "longstanding, covert access to the build process" and inserted a backdoor into signed releases (threat E).
  • Codecov (2021): a modified uploader script exfiltrated CI environment variables; a customer noticed because the checksum didn't match (F and G).
  • xz-utils (2024, CVE-2024-3094, CVSS 10.0): after years of gaining a maintainer's trust, the attacker added a backdoor that appeared only in the release tarballs, not in the git source.
  • Trivy (March 2026): release tags were repointed to malicious code; users pinned to digests were unaffected (topic 1.3).

SLSA and provenance

Topic 5.1Graded levels of build and source integrity, and the provenance that proves them.

SLSA (Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts) is an OpenSSF project. The current spec is v1.2 (November 2025), with two tracks.

Build levelRequiresProtects against
L0Nothing: "L0 represents the lack of SLSA"-
L1: Provenance existsThe build platform automatically generates provenance; it "may be incomplete and/or unsigned"Mistakes
L2: Hosted build platformA hosted platform generates and signs the provenance"Tampering after the build"
L3: Hardened buildsBuilds are isolated from each other, and signing secrets are kept away from user-defined build steps"Tampering during the build"

The Source track, new in v1.2, has four levels: version controlled, history and provenance, continuous technical controls, and two-party review ("The SCS requires two trusted persons to review all changes to protected branches").

Provenance is verifiable information about where, when and how an artifact was built. SLSA provenance is an in-toto attestation: a statement whose subject lists the artifacts and their digests, with a predicateType of https://slsa.dev/provenance/v1 and a predicate describing the build definition and the builder that ran it.

There's no Build L4. Material that mentions "SLSA 4" (two-person review plus hermetic builds) uses the old v0.1 draft. Since v1.0 (April 2023) the Build track stops at L3, and two-party review now sits at Source L4. Kubernetes announced SLSA Level 1 provenance for its releases in v1.23 (under the old numbering); kubernetes.io doesn't claim Build L3.

Signing artifacts

Topic 5.1Keyless and key-based signing, and where signatures are stored.

Sigstore (an OpenSSF project) has three parts. In its own words, artifacts are "Signed: By using a Sigstore client (Cosign). Associated: With an identity through our certificate authority (Fulcio). Witnessed: By recording the signing information in a permanent transparency log (Rekor)."

  1. Identifycosign creates a one-time key pair and gets an OIDC token for the signer: a person's login, or a CI workflow's identity.
  2. CertifyFulcio "issues a short-lived certificate bound to the provided identity and public key", valid for 10 minutes.
  3. Sign and recordThe image digest is signed and the entry is written to Rekor, an append-only log; "the private key is discarded after a single signing".
  4. VerifyThe verifier checks the signature, that the certificate's identity "matches an expected identity", the chain to Sigstore's root, and the Rekor entry proving it was signed while the certificate was valid.
Sign and verify the shop image (shell) 
IMG=registry.example.com/shop/api
IMG=$IMG@sha256:<digest>
ID=ci@shop.example.com
ISS=https://accounts.google.com

# keyless: OIDC login, Fulcio, Rekor
cosign sign "$IMG"

# both identity and issuer required
cosign verify "$IMG" \
  --certificate-identity "$ID" \
  --certificate-oidc-issuer "$ISS"

# key-based alternative
cosign generate-key-pair
cosign sign --key cosign.key "$IMG"
ApproachTrust comes fromTrade-off
cosign keylessAn OIDC identity, Fulcio and the public Rekor logNo long-lived keys to protect; depends on the identity provider and Sigstore (public or private instance)
cosign with keysA key pair you manage, often in a cloud KMSWorks offline; you must protect and rotate the key
Notation (Notary Project)Your own X.509 CA, a trust store and a trust policyFits existing PKI; no public transparency log

Where signatures live

Signatures, SBOMs and attestations are stored in the registry next to the image, without changing its digest. The older cosign scheme uses a tag named sha256-<digest>.sig, still used on registry.k8s.io. OCI 1.1 (2024) added a subject field and a referrers API that lists every artifact pointing at an image; cosign v3 (October 2025) and Notation use it by default. cosign tree and oras discover show what's attached.

Keyless doesn't mean no keys. A key still signs, but it's used once and thrown away; trust comes from the identity in a certificate that expires in minutes. That's why verification must name both the expected identity and the OIDC issuer: required since cosign 2.0. Checking only that "some valid signature exists" would accept anyone's signature.

Kubernetes' own signatures

Topic 5.1Verifying the Kubernetes images and binaries you install.

The Kubernetes project signs its own release artifacts: container images since v1.24, and binaries, SBOMs and provenance since v1.26, when "All release artifacts are signed keyless using cosign". Images are signed by krel-trust@ and binaries by krel-staging@, both service accounts of the release project, with issuer https://accounts.google.com.

Verify a Kubernetes image and binary (shell) 
SA=k8s-releng-prod.iam.gserviceaccount.com
K=registry.k8s.io/kube-apiserver
cosign verify "$K:v1.37.2" \
  --certificate-identity \
  "krel-trust@$SA" \
  --certificate-oidc-issuer \
  https://accounts.google.com

U=https://dl.k8s.io/release/v1.37.2
U=$U/bin/linux/amd64
for f in kubectl kubectl.sig kubectl.cert
do curl -sSfLO "$U/$f"; done
cosign verify-blob kubectl \
  --signature kubectl.sig \
  --certificate kubectl.cert \
  --certificate-identity \
  "krel-staging@$SA" \
  --certificate-oidc-issuer \
  https://accounts.google.com

After verifying, kubernetes.io advises: "specify the image by its digest in your Pod manifests". The release SBOM is an SPDX 2.3 document. The page still labels the feature beta (since v1.26) and refers to cosign 2.0.

SBOMs

Topic 5.1Software bills of materials: formats, generation and the minimum contents.

An SBOM is "a detailed inventory of all components, libraries, and dependencies used in a software application". The CNCF paper adds that "just having an SBOM has little value without tooling to interrogate, aggregate, and otherwise reason about it": its value is answering "are we affected?" the day a new CVE appears.

FormatOwner and standardOrigin
SPDXLinux Foundation; ISO/IEC 5962:2021; current version 3.0Licence compliance
CycloneDXOWASP; ECMA-424; current version 1.7 (October 2025)Security; also covers VEX, hardware and AI models
Generate and attach an SBOM (shell) 
# generate (Syft or Trivy)
syft "$IMG" -o spdx-json=sbom.json
# sign it and attach it to the image
cosign attest --type spdxjson \
  --predicate sbom.json "$IMG"
cosign verify-attestation \
  --type spdxjson \
  --certificate-identity "$ID" \
  --certificate-oidc-issuer "$ISS" \
  "$IMG"

The US Executive Order 14028 (May 2021) led to NTIA's 2021 SBOM minimum elements and NIST's Secure Software Development Framework (SP 800-218). VEX documents (topic 1.3) complement SBOMs by stating which listed vulnerabilities actually matter.

NTIA's minimum elements were replaced in 2026. CISA's "2026 Minimum Elements for a Software Bill of Materials" (29 July 2026), written with the NSA, FBI and international partners, "updates and replaces" the 2021 NTIA list. It adds component hashes, licences, SBOM tool name and version, the generation context (before, during or after the build) and an author signature, and replaces "Supplier Name" with "Component Producer".

in-toto and TUF

Topic 5.1Two CNCF frameworks: one for the steps that produce software, one for delivering it.

ProjectPurposeCNCF status
in-toto"Designed to ensure the integrity of a software product from initiation to end-user installation" by "making it transparent to the user what steps were performed, by whom and in what order". Its attestation format (a statement in a signed DSSE envelope) carries SLSA provenance and cosign attestationsGraduated (2025)
TUF (The Update Framework)"A framework for securing software update systems", "providing protection even against attackers that compromise the repository or signing keys"Graduated (2019), the first security project to graduate

TUF separates trust into four roles, each with its own keys and signature thresholds: root (delegates to the others; its keys "should be kept offline"), targets (signs what files are trustworthy), snapshot (the current set of metadata, preventing mix-and-match attacks) and timestamp (re-signed often, so clients can't be fed stale metadata). Sigstore distributes its own root of trust using TUF.

Telling them apart. in-toto proves how something was made (the steps). TUF secures delivering it (updates and metadata). SLSA grades how trustworthy the build is. Sigstore and Notation sign the result. Of these, only in-toto, TUF and the Notary Project (incubating) are CNCF projects; Sigstore and SLSA belong to OpenSSF.

Securing the image repository

Topic 5.1Registry features that keep images trustworthy, and safe mirroring.

Harbor (CNCF graduated) is the shop's registry. Beyond the scanning and tag immutability in topic 1.3, it offers:

FeatureSecurity use
Private projects with member rolesOnly named people and accounts can pull or push
Robot accountsScoped credentials for CI (push) and nodes (pull); they "cannot log in to the Harbor interface", and the secret is shown once
Signature policy (Cosign and/or Notation)"Harbor will only allow verified images to be pulled from the project"
ReplicationCopies images, and their Cosign signatures, between registries
Proxy cachePulls public images through a controlled cache; you can't push to a proxy project. "Only configure proxy cache endpoints that are within your trust boundary"
Tag retentionRemoves stale images by rule; "You cannot revert a rule after you run it", so use Dry Run

Mirrors on the nodes

containerd reads per-registry mirror settings from hosts.toml files under the directory named by config_path in its CRI registry plugin settings. The older registry.mirrors configuration is deprecated.

Pull Docker Hub images through an internal mirror (hosts.toml) 
# /etc/containerd/certs.d/
#   docker.io/hosts.toml
server = "https://registry-1.docker.io"

[host."https://mirror.internal"]
  capabilities = ["pull", "resolve"]
  ca = "/etc/certs/mirror-ca.pem"

Only trusted mirrors may resolve tags. containerd's docs: "Resolving (the process of converting a name into a digest) must be considered a trusted operation", and "A public mirror should never be trusted to do a resolve action." Give a public mirror only pull, and push only to the real upstream. Remove the server line to stop falling back to the upstream at all.

  • Credentials: push only from CI; nodes pull with read-only credentials; no shared admin accounts; TLS everywhere, never "insecure registry" settings.
  • Air-gapped clusters: copy images together with their signatures and SBOMs (cosign copy, oras copy) into an internal registry, and use key-based signatures, Notation, or a private Sigstore instance, since the public Fulcio and Rekor can't be reached. kubernetes.io recommends mirroring release images "to a private registry" in restricted environments.
  • Other CNCF projects: Distribution (the reference registry, sandbox), zot (an OCI-native registry, sandbox) and Dragonfly (peer-to-peer image distribution, graduated in 2025-2026).

Verifying at deploy time

Topic 5.1Making the cluster refuse images that aren't signed by the right identity.

Signing helps only if something checks. In Kubernetes that check happens at admission (topic 5.4):

Only CI-signed shop images may run (Kyverno ImageValidatingPolicy) 
apiVersion: policies.kyverno.io/v1
kind: ImageValidatingPolicy
metadata:
  name: require-signed-shop
spec:
  validationActions: [Deny]
  matchConstraints:
    resourceRules:
    - apiGroups: [""]
      apiVersions: [v1]
      resources: [pods]
      operations: [CREATE, UPDATE]
  matchImageReferences:
  - glob: "registry.example.com/shop/*"
  attestors:
  - name: ci
    cosign:
      keyless:
        identities:
        - subject: ci@shop.example.com
          issuer: >-
            https://accounts.google.com
      ctlog:
        url: https://rekor.sigstore.dev
  validations:
  - expression: >-
      images.containers.map(i,
      verifyImageSignatures(i,
      [attestors.ci])).all(e, e > 0)
    message: image not signed by CI

Kyverno's verifyImages is deprecated. Kyverno (CNCF graduated, March 2026) introduced the dedicated ImageValidatingPolicy in v1.14 (2025). In v1.19 (August 2026) it deprecated ClusterPolicy, including its verifyImages rules, with removal planned for v1.20. Older material and exam questions may still show verifyImages with imageReferences and keyless attestors; know both.

  • Sigstore policy-controller, the tool kubernetes.io names: ClusterImagePolicy objects with keyless or key authorities. It applies only to namespaces labelled policy.sigstore.dev/include=true, and in those namespaces images that match no policy are rejected by default.
  • Ratify (CNCF sandbox, now under the Notary Project) runs with OPA Gatekeeper and verifies Notation and Cosign signatures and other attached artifacts.
  • Connaisseur (not CNCF) is another admission controller for Notary and Cosign signatures. CRI-O can also verify Sigstore signatures at pull time.

Pin what you verified. Verifying a tag at admission and then pulling it later leaves a gap if the tag moves. Policy engines can replace the tag with the verified digest (Kyverno's mutateDigest), and policy-controller resolves tags to digests, so the Pod runs exactly what was checked.

Key takeaways

  • Supply chain stages: source, materials, build, artifacts, deployment and distribution. SLSA threats A to I. Examples: SolarWinds (build), Codecov (artifact), xz-utils (maintainer and tarball), Trivy (mutable tags).
  • SLSA v1.2: Build L1 provenance exists, L2 hosted and signed, L3 hardened (isolation, signing secrets out of reach); no Build L4. Source track L1 to L4 (L4 two-party review). Provenance is an in-toto attestation with subject digests.
  • Sigstore (OpenSSF): cosign signs, Fulcio issues 10-minute certificates from OIDC identities, Rekor logs. Verify with identity and issuer. Notation uses your own PKI. Signatures live as .sig tags or OCI 1.1 referrers.
  • Kubernetes signs images (since 1.24) and binaries and SBOMs (since 1.26), keyless: krel-trust@ for images, krel-staging@ for binaries, issuer accounts.google.com. Pin verified images by digest.
  • SBOMs: SPDX (ISO/IEC 5962) and CycloneDX (ECMA-424); syft or trivy, then cosign attest. CISA's 2026 minimum elements replace NTIA 2021. in-toto (steps) and TUF (delivery: root, targets, snapshot, timestamp) are CNCF graduated.
  • Registry: Harbor private projects, robot accounts, Cosign and Notation pull policy, replication, proxy cache, retention. containerd hosts.toml: public mirrors pull only, never resolve.
  • Enforce at admission: Kyverno ImageValidatingPolicy (verifyImages deprecated in 1.19), Sigstore policy-controller (opt-in namespaces), Ratify with Gatekeeper; mutate tags to verified digests.
