Overview

Topic 6.2

Threat Modeling Frameworks. Automation and Tooling.

The curriculum gives only the names. This page takes threat modeling frameworks to mean the methods for finding threats in a design (STRIDE above all, plus PASTA, LINDDUN, OCTAVE and attack trees) and how they differ from threat matrices. It takes automation and tooling to mean the defender tools that check configuration and policy automatically: in CI, at admission and continuously in the cluster. Trust boundaries and data flow diagrams were covered in topic 4.1; the frameworks and evidence those scanners report against in topic 6.1; image scanners in topic 1.3; runtime detection in topic 5.2.

Sources: the Threat Modeling Manifesto, the CNCF® Cloud Native Security Whitepaper, the CNCF "Open and Secure" threat modeling manual, Microsoft Learn and the Microsoft Threat Matrix for Kubernetes (all CC BY 4.0), the OWASP Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet (CC BY-SA 4.0), kubernetes.io (CC BY 4.0), and project documentation. Other method descriptions are paraphrased. Tool versions were checked on 8 November 2026.

Running example. Before the shop adds a loyalty-points service, the team runs a threat modeling session on the shop namespace (web, api, db, worker), then turns the controls it agrees on into checks that run on every pull request and in the cluster.

What threat modeling is

Topic 6.2The purpose of threat modeling, the four questions, and the CNCF's approach.

The Threat Modeling Manifesto (2020, written by 15 practitioners including Adam Shostack and Kim Wuyts) defines it: "Threat modeling is analyzing representations of a system to highlight concerns about security and privacy characteristics." It's built around four questions:

  1. What are we working on?Model the system: components, data flows, data stores, external entities and trust boundaries (topic 4.1).
  2. What can go wrong?Find threats, usually with a framework such as STRIDE as prompts.
  3. What are we going to do about it?Decide how to treat each threat: mitigate, eliminate, transfer or accept.
  4. Did we do a good enough job?Review the model and the result, and repeat as the system changes.

Its five values include "A culture of finding and fixing design issues over checkbox compliance" and "Continuous refinement over a single delivery." The OWASP cheat sheet uses the same four steps, says threat modeling "is ideally performed early in the SDLC, such as during the design phase", and that the model "should be maintained, updated and refined alongside the system".

Kubernetes and the CNCF say the same. kubernetes.io: "Build a threat model of your system or application that identifies trust boundaries. Use that threat model to identify risks and determine how to treat them." The CNCF whitepaper calls threat modeling "a primary mechanism for identifying risks, controls, and mitigations", describes its guidance as "an enhancement of the four-step OWASP threat modeling", and pays "special attention ... to any interactions that cross security boundaries". It names three kinds of threat actor: the malicious insider, the uninformed insider ("assuming anyone can be duped") and the malicious outsider.

Threat models feed the rest of the lifecycle. The whitepaper says security requirements "can be an outcome of a threat modeling exercise", that threat modeling finds "high-risk and high-impact code hotspots" worth testing, and that security tests should be "continuously updated to replicate threats in-line with the organizational threat model". That's the link to the automation in the second half of this page.

STRIDE

Topic 6.2The six STRIDE categories and the security property each one violates.

STRIDE was developed by Loren Kohnfelder and Praerit Garg at Microsoft in 1999. Microsoft says it "categorizes different types of threats and simplifies the overall security conversations"; OWASP calls it "a mature and popular threat modeling technique" and suggests using its "six categories as prompts while reviewing the system model". It's the framework the KCSA most expects you to know.

ThreatMeaning (Microsoft)Property violated
SpoofingUsing another user's authentication information, such as a username and passwordAuthentication
Tampering"the malicious modification of data", stored or in transitIntegrity
RepudiationUsers denying an action "without other parties having any way to prove otherwise"Non-repudiation
Information disclosure"the exposure of information to individuals who are not supposed to have access to it"Confidentiality
Denial of serviceDenying service to valid users, for example by making a web server unavailableAvailability
Elevation of privilege"An unprivileged user gains privileged access"Authorization

Microsoft's own STRIDE page lists only the threats; the property column comes from OWASP and the CNCF manual. A common way to apply STRIDE is per element: walk every process, data store, data flow and external entity in the diagram and ask which categories apply. Most threats appear where a flow crosses a trust boundary.

Repudiation's property has two names. The classic mapping, used by the CNCF manual and older OWASP pages, is non-repudiation. The OWASP cheat sheet, rewritten in October 2026, now says "Accounting". Expect non-repudiation on the exam, and remember that audit logging is the usual control (topic 3.5). Also note the CNCF manual says "Escalation" instead of "Elevation" of privilege, and that in distributed systems it is often lateral movement rather than a jump to admin.

Other frameworks

Topic 6.2PASTA, LINDDUN, OCTAVE, attack trees and other methods, and when each fits.

MethodFocusEssentials
PASTA (Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis)Risk and business impactSeven stages: define objectives, define technical scope, decompose the application, threat analysis, vulnerability and weakness analysis, attack modeling, risk and impact analysis. OWASP: "Connect business objectives and technical requirements to application threats"
LINDDUN (KU Leuven, 2010)PrivacySeven threat types: Linking, Identifying, Non-repudiation, Detecting, Data disclosure, Unawareness and unintervenability, Non-compliance. LINDDUN GO is a card game with 33 threat cards; LINDDUN PRO works through a data flow diagram
OCTAVE (Carnegie Mellon SEI)Organizational riskOperationally Critical Threat, Asset, and Vulnerability Evaluation. OCTAVE Allegro (2007) streamlines risk assessment around information assets and the people, technology and facilities that hold them
Attack trees (Bruce Schneier, 1999)One attacker goalThe goal is the root; children are ways to reach it. OR nodes are alternatives, AND nodes are steps that are all needed. Values such as cost propagate up the tree. Also called threat trees
VASTAgile teams at scaleVisual, Agile, and Simple Threat modeling: separate application and operational threat models

The CNCF whitepaper recommends "a mature, well-used model of threats such as STRIDE or OCTAVE". The CNCF's Financial User Group built six Kubernetes attack trees from STRIDE workshops at trust boundaries (topic 4.1); they show attack paths but no mitigations, and modeled Kubernetes around version 1.14.

LINDDUN is about privacy, and flips non-repudiation. In STRIDE, non-repudiation is a property to protect. In LINDDUN, non-repudiation is a threat: being able to tie a claim or action to a person can harm their privacy. Older material uses the earlier names Linkability, Identifiability, Detectability and Disclosure of information.

DREAD. DREAD scores a threat on Damage, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected users and Discoverability (each 1 to 10, then averaged). It's widely criticised as subjective; the CNCF manual notes that discoverability is hard to judge and suggests always scoring it 10. The current OWASP cheat sheet no longer mentions it and ranks threats by likelihood and impact instead, though a Microsoft Learn page on driver threat modeling still teaches it.

STRIDE for the shop

Topic 6.2Applying STRIDE to a Kubernetes workload, using the CNCF whitepaper's examples.

The whitepaper gives one Kubernetes example per category. Adapted to the shop, with the controls from earlier topics, the output of the session looks like this:

CategoryThreat to the shopControls
SpoofingA cluster admin's credentials are stolen through social engineeringOIDC with MFA, short-lived credentials, no shared kubeconfigs (topic 3.2)
TamperingAn API server config file or certificate is altered, so the API server fails to restart or mutual TLS failsFile permissions on control plane nodes, benchmark checks, GitOps drift correction (topic 2.1, below)
RepudiationAuditing is disabled or misconfigured, so there's no evidence of who changed the shopAn audit policy, logs shipped off-cluster to write-once storage (topic 3.5)
Information disclosureA compromised api Pod sends customer data to an outside hostDefault-deny egress NetworkPolicy, Secrets encryption, runtime detection (topic 3.4, 3.3)
Denial of serviceweb has no resource limits, uses all the node's CPU and memory, and the node is lostRequests and limits, LimitRange, ResourceQuota (topic 4.2)
Elevation of privilegeapi runs privileged, or someone changes its security contextPod Security restricted, least-privilege RBAC, admission policy (topic 3.1, 5.4)

Turn controls into checks. Several of these controls ("Pods run as non-root", "every container has limits", "no privileged containers") can be written as policies and tested automatically. Sections 6 to 9 show where those checks run.

Frameworks, matrices and modeling tools

Topic 6.2How threat matrices differ from frameworks, and the tools that support modeling.

Frameworks such as STRIDE help you find threats in your design. Threat matrices are catalogues of attacker behaviour that has been seen in practice; defenders use them to check detection coverage and to validate controls (topics 4.2 and 4.3). The CNCF manual says MITRE ATT&CK® is "more suitable for incident response and remediation rather than proactive risk identification".

MatrixTactics
MITRE ATT&CK Containers (v20.1)10: Initial Access, Execution, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, Stealth, Defense Impairment, Credential Access, Discovery, Lateral Movement, Impact. Version 19 (April 2026) split Defense Evasion into Stealth and Defense Impairment
Microsoft Threat Matrix for Kubernetes10: Initial Access, Execution, Persistence, Privilege Escalation, Defense Evasion, Credential Access, Discovery, Lateral Movement, Collection, Impact

The whitepaper's Threat Matrix for Containers section pairs tactics with defender advice: mutual authentication and limited host mounts against initial access, trusted registries and audited image pulls against execution and persistence, least privilege against privilege escalation, auditing of shell and exec calls, short-lived credentials, and incident response playbooks.

Threat modeling tools

  • OWASP Threat Dragon (v2.6.2, an OWASP Production project, Apache-2.0): draw data flow diagrams as a desktop or self-hosted web app; supports STRIDE, LINDDUN, CIA and others, and suggests threats and mitigations.
  • Microsoft Threat Modeling Tool: a free Windows application, part of the Microsoft SDL, that generates STRIDE threats per element from a diagram. The latest release, 7.3.51110.1 (November 2025), came two years after the previous one.
  • CNCF "Open and Secure": a 2023 manual by Justin Cappos with Andrés Vega for open source maintainers, covering STRIDE, LINDDUN, attack trees and DREAD, and the TAG Security assessment process with its lightweight threat model template. TAG Security is now TAG Security and Compliance.

Manifest and configuration scanners

Topic 6.2Static checks of Kubernetes YAML, Helm charts and infrastructure code before anything is deployed.

The whitepaper says tools should scan "Infrastructure as Code templates as well as application manifests within the developer IDE or when a pull request is made", and "It is vital to scan application manifests in the CI/CD pipeline." Automated tests "increase security and release velocity by removing manual security gates".

ToolWhat it checksStatus
KubeLinterYAML, Helm charts and Kustomize against 63 built-in checks (privileged containers, latest tags, missing non-root, cluster-admin bindings); non-zero exit code on failurev0.8.3; StackRox / Red Hat; not CNCF
CheckovTerraform, CloudFormation, Kubernetes, Helm, Dockerfiles and more; Kubernetes checks are CKV_K8S_*3.4.3; Prisma Cloud; not CNCF
kubesecA security score and advice for a manifest; also as an admission webhook or kubectl pluginv2.15.0; ControlPlane; not CNCF
PolarisBest-practice checks (30+ built in, custom ones in JSON Schema) as a CLI, dashboard or admission controllerv10.2.6; Fairwinds; not CNCF
ConftestAny structured config (Kubernetes, Terraform, Tekton) against your own Rego policiesv0.71.1; part of OPA (CNCF Graduated)
PopeyeA read-only "live cluster linter" that checks what's already deployedv0.22.1 (January 2025); not CNCF
Lint the shop's manifests (shell) 
# List built-in checks, then lint
kube-linter checks list
kube-linter lint ./deploy/

# SARIF for code-scanning UIs
kube-linter lint ./deploy/ \
  --format sarif --output kl.sarif

# Checkov: Kubernetes checks only
checkov -d ./deploy --framework kubernetes

# Polaris: fail on any danger finding
# or a score below 90
polaris audit --audit-path ./deploy/ \
  --set-exit-code-on-danger \
  --set-exit-code-below-score 90

A scanner is only a gate if it fails the build. KubeLinter exits non-zero when a check fails, but Polaris needs the --set-exit-code-* flags, Checkov's --soft-fail turns failures into warnings, and Trivy exits 0 unless you pass --exit-code (topic 1.3).

Tools that are gone. Datree: the company closed in July 2023 and the repository is archived. kubeaudit (Shopify): archived, and its README points to kube-bench. kube-hunter (Aqua): "not under active development anymore", last release 2022, and it points to Trivy's Kubernetes scanning. Older courses still list all three.

Compliance scanners

Topic 6.2Checking a running cluster against benchmarks and frameworks, and keeping the results as evidence.

These tools turn the benchmarks and frameworks of topic 6.1 into repeatable scans. Their output (JSON, JUnit, SARIF) is the evidence auditors ask for.

ToolChecks againstStatus
kube-benchThe CIS Kubernetes Benchmark, plus managed-service variants (EKS, AKS, GKE) and a STIG config; runs on each node, often as a Jobv0.16.0; Aqua; not CNCF
Trivy (trivy k8s --compliance)Built-in specs such as k8s-cis-1.23, k8s-nsa-1.0 and k8s-pss-restricted-0.1, or a custom specv0.76.2; Aqua; not CNCF
KubescapeFrameworks: NSA, MITRE, CIS (cis-v1.12.0 and managed-service versions), SOC2 and others; can fail CI below a scorev4.0.20; CNCF Incubating
OpenSCAP / Compliance OperatorNIST's SCAP content for operating systems and OpenShift, run in-cluster by the operatorRed Hat; not CNCF
OSCAL-COMPASSMachine-readable controls and results in NIST's OSCAL format, linked to policy engines such as KyvernoCNCF Sandbox (2024)
Benchmark scans with evidence (shell) 
# CIS checks for control plane + node
kube-bench run --targets master,node

# Pin the benchmark (K8s 1.36+ has no
# auto-mapping in v0.16.0)
kube-bench run --benchmark cis-2.0 \
  --json --outputfile kb.json

# Trivy: NSA/CISA checks as JSON
trivy k8s --compliance k8s-nsa-1.0 \
  --report all --format json \
  --output nsa.json

# Kubescape: fail CI below 80 %
kubescape scan framework nsa \
  --compliance-threshold 80 \
  --format json --output ks.json

A pass means "passes this tool's version of the checks". The tools lag the benchmark: the CIS benchmark is at 2.1.0, but kube-bench's newest config is cis-2.0, Trivy's CIS spec is 1.23 and Kubescape's is cis-v1.12.0. Kubescape's "SOC2" framework is seven technical controls, not a SOC 2 audit. And on a managed service you can only check the nodes, not the provider's control plane.

Policy tests in the pipeline

Topic 6.2Testing manifests against the same policies the cluster enforces, before deploying.

Policy as code means the shop's rules live in Git as files, are reviewed like code and run in two places: in CI against manifests, and at admission in the cluster (topic 5.4). Running the same policy in both places means a pull request fails for the same reason the API server would reject it.

policy/deny.rego (Rego v1, for conftest) 
package main

# Deployments must set runAsNonRoot
deny contains msg if {
  input.kind == "Deployment"
  c := input.spec.template.spec
  not c.securityContext.runAsNonRoot
  msg := "Pods must set runAsNonRoot"
}
Run it (shell) 
conftest test deploy/web.yaml
# FAIL - deploy/web.yaml - Pods must
#   set runAsNonRoot

Conftest reads policies from policy/ and the main package by default, and now uses Rego v1 syntax by default (deny contains msg if { ... }); the old deny[msg] { ... } form needs --rego-version v0.

EngineCLIBehaviour
Kyvernokyverno apply, kyverno testapply runs policies against resource files or a live cluster and exits 1 on failures; -p prints a policy report. test runs test cases from a kyverno-test.yaml. Both support Kyverno's CEL ValidatingPolicy (policies.kyverno.io/v1) and the built-in ValidatingAdmissionPolicy
OPA Gatekeepergator test, gator verifygator test checks objects against ConstraintTemplates and Constraints and exits 1 on violations (only for deny enforcement, not dryrun); gator verify runs test suites
CI gates for the shop (shell) 
#!/bin/sh
set -eu  # stop at first failing gate
IMG="$REG/shop/api@$DIGEST"

# 1 Image: fail on HIGH/CRITICAL
trivy image --exit-code 1 \
  --severity HIGH,CRITICAL "$IMG"

# 2 Manifests: lint (non-zero on fail)
kube-linter lint deploy/

# 3 Admission policies, before deploy
kyverno apply policies/ \
  --resource deploy/ -p

# 4 Policy unit tests
kyverno test policies/tests/

The gator commands swapped names. "The gator verify command was first called gator test." Older guides use gator test for running test suites. Also, Kyverno's legacy ClusterPolicy is deprecated (Kyverno 1.19) in favour of the CEL policy types.

Security tools are supply chain inputs too. Pin CI actions and tool images to a full commit SHA or digest. In March 2026, tags of a popular Trivy GitHub Action were hijacked (topic 1.3).

Automation in the cluster

Topic 6.2Continuous scanning, reporting, alert routing, automated response and drift correction.

kubernetes.io's security checklist warns that "Checklists are not sufficient for attaining a good security posture on their own." Images should be "regularly scanned during creation and in deployment", and signatures "automatically verified with an admission controller". So checks run at three points: in CI, at admission, and continuously against what's already running.

JobTools
Continuous scanningTrivy Operator (v0.36.1, Aqua) scans workloads in the cluster and writes results as custom resources; scheduled kube-bench, Trivy or Kubescape scans (above); policy engines check existing objects in the background
ReportingPolicy Reporter (3.x, Kyverno project) shows PolicyReports and OpenReports in a UI and sends them on
Alert routingFalcosidekick (stable) forwards Falco events "in a fan-out way" to chat, alerting and SIEM tools
Automated responseFalco Talon, "a Response Engine for managing threats in your Kubernetes", can label or terminate a Pod, apply a NetworkPolicy or cordon a node; still incubating (v0.3.0)
Drift correctionGitOps controllers put the cluster back to the state in Git: Argo CD with self-heal, Flux on every interval

GitOps as a security control

The whitepaper: immutable, declarative infrastructure "makes it easier to detect and reverse environment changes based on the declarative state in the Git repository", and GitOps gives "an audit of all changes, a declarative single source of truth, policy enforcement". The catch: "if that process is compromised, then the adversary may open infrastructure backdoors". So protect the repository: restricted branch access, reviews on a protected main branch, signed commits, no force pushes, no unencrypted Secrets in Git, and rotated, short-lived credentials.

Argo CD: revert manual changes (YAML) 
apiVersion: argoproj.io/v1alpha1
kind: Application
metadata:
  name: shop
  namespace: argocd
spec:
  # source, destination omitted
  syncPolicy:
    automated:
      prune: true     # delete removed
      selfHeal: true  # undo live edits

Argo CD doesn't revert drift by default. Its docs: "By default, changes that are made to the live cluster will not trigger automated sync." You need selfHeal: true. Flux's kustomize-controller detects and corrects drift on every reconcile interval.

Keep people in the loop. The whitepaper: "automated detection should not yet replace humans who can reason about more complicated scenarios." Automated response such as killing a Pod is best kept for well-understood, high-confidence alerts.

Few security tools are CNCF projects. In the CNCF Landscape, "Security & Compliance" is a subcategory of Provisioning with 133 entries, most of them not CNCF projects. The CNCF ones you're likely to meet: Falco, OPA, Kyverno, cert-manager, in-toto and TUF (Graduated); Kubescape and Notary Project (Incubating); KubeArmor and Kubewarden (Sandbox). Trivy, Checkov, KubeLinter, Polaris and kube-bench are not CNCF projects.

Key takeaways

  • Threat modeling is "analyzing representations of a system to highlight concerns about security and privacy characteristics." Four questions: what are we working on, what can go wrong, what are we going to do about it, did we do a good enough job. Do it early and keep it current.
  • STRIDE (Microsoft, 1999): Spoofing/authentication, Tampering/integrity, Repudiation/non-repudiation, Information disclosure/confidentiality, Denial of service/availability, Elevation of privilege/authorization. Apply it per element, especially across trust boundaries.
  • PASTA: seven risk-centric stages. LINDDUN: privacy (non-repudiation is a threat there). OCTAVE: organizational risk (SEI). Attack trees: goal at the root, AND/OR children. DREAD: criticised, dropped by OWASP's cheat sheet.
  • The CNCF whitepaper builds on OWASP's four steps and names STRIDE or OCTAVE; its six Kubernetes STRIDE examples map to RBAC and MFA, file protection, audit logs, egress policy, resource limits and Pod Security.
  • Frameworks find threats in your design; ATT&CK and the Microsoft matrix catalogue attacker behaviour for detection and validation. Modeling tools: OWASP Threat Dragon, Microsoft Threat Modeling Tool.
  • Compliance scans: kube-bench (CIS), trivy k8s --compliance, Kubescape frameworks (CNCF Incubating); tools lag the benchmark versions, so record which version you checked.
  • Scan manifests in the IDE and CI (KubeLinter, Checkov, kubesec, Polaris, Conftest), test admission policies before deploying (kyverno apply/test, gator), and make sure each tool fails the build. Datree, kubeaudit and kube-hunter are no longer maintained.
  • In the cluster: continuous scanning (Trivy Operator, Kubescape), policy reports, Falcosidekick for alerts, Falco Talon for response, and GitOps drift correction (Argo CD needs selfHeal). Checklists alone aren't enough, and humans stay in the loop.
