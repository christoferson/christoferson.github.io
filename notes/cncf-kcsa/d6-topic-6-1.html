6.1: Compliance Frameworks and Supply Chain Compliance

The frameworks and laws a Kubernetes® platform may have to satisfy, how their requirements map to cluster controls, how to prove compliance, and the rules now emerging for software supply chains.

Overview

Topic 6.1

Compliance Frameworks. Supply Chain Compliance.

The curriculum gives only the names. This page takes compliance frameworks to mean the major frameworks, standards and regulations, what they ask for, how they map to Kubernetes, and how compliance is shown. It takes supply chain compliance to mean the rules for how software is built and documented: the SSDF, SBOM requirements, attestations and the EU Cyber Resilience Act. The framework families themselves were introduced in topic 1.2; SLSA and SBOM formats in topic 5.1; compliance scanning tools are in topic 6.2.

Sources: the CNCF® Cloud Native Security Whitepaper and supply chain paper (CC BY 4.0), NIST and US government publications (public), EU regulations (EUR-Lex, reused with attribution), and the PCI, AICPA, ISO and CIS publications, which are copyrighted, so their content is paraphrased here.

Running example. The shop takes card payments and sells into the EU. Its auditor asks which frameworks apply, how the cluster meets them, and what evidence the team keeps, and a large customer asks how its software is built.

Compliance and security

Topic 6.1Why passing an audit and being secure are different things.

The CNCF whitepaper: "A compliant system is not guaranteed to be secure, nor a secure system is guaranteed to be compliant." The two "are complementary processes but are not interchangeable." Compliance results "are binary (pass or fail)" but can contain false positives and false negatives.

KindWhat it isExamples
FrameworkA structure of outcomes or controls you choose from, usually voluntaryNIST CSF 2.0, NIST SP 800-53
Standard with certification or auditRequirements an independent auditor checksISO/IEC 27001, SOC 2, PCI DSS
Regulation or lawLegally binding obligations, with penaltiesGDPR, HIPAA, NIS2, DORA, the EU Cyber Resilience Act
Benchmark or hardening guideSpecific, testable configuration settingsCIS Kubernetes Benchmark, DISA STIG, NSA/CISA guidance

The whitepaper's advice on benchmarks applies to all of them: they "cannot consider data flows and custom usage of the platforms under testing. Security practitioners should implement them as a guide rather than a checklist." And designing controls in from the start makes certification "easier, particularly if the system design and planning is done to allow automated compliance".

Frameworks and standards

Topic 6.1The frameworks and audited standards most often applied to cloud platforms.

FrameworkEssentials
NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (February 2024)Voluntary and outcome-based ("does not prescribe how outcomes should be achieved"). Six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Govern is new in 2.0 and sits "in the center"
NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5The US federal control catalogue: 20 families; release 5.2.0 (August 2025) added patch and update controls (topic 1.2)
ISO/IEC 27001:2022A certifiable information security management system; Annex A has 93 controls in four themes (organizational, people, physical, technological). The transition from the 2013 edition ended in October 2025. ISO/IEC 27017 and 27018 add cloud and cloud-PII guidance
SOC 2 (AICPA)An attestation by an independent CPA against the Trust Services Criteria: security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, privacy. Proposed 2027 criteria were published for comment in October 2026
CIS Kubernetes BenchmarkConsensus configuration checks for the control plane, etcd and nodes, now at 2.1.0, with separate benchmarks for managed services (described only; its text may not be reproduced)
DISA Kubernetes STIGUS Department of Defense hardening rules for Kubernetes (V2R6, February 2026)
NSA/CISA Kubernetes Hardening GuidanceUS government guidance, v1.2 (August 2022), partly dated

CSF has six functions now. Older material lists five (Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover). CSF 2.0 added Govern. Also, CSF describes outcomes; SP 800-53 is the catalogue of controls that achieve them.

Regulations

Topic 6.1The laws and industry rules that commonly apply, and their headline obligations.

The whitepaper: "Every organization should evaluate which regulatory standards apply to them (e.g., PCI-DSS, HIPAA, FedRAMP, GDPR, etc.)".

RuleApplies toHeadline obligations
PCI DSS v4.0.1 (June 2024)Anyone storing, processing or transmitting payment card data12 requirements, from network security controls and secure configuration through encryption, access control, logging and testing; v4.0's future-dated requirements became mandatory on 31 March 2025
HIPAA Security RuleUS health data (protected health information)Administrative, physical and technical safeguards (access control, audit controls, integrity, authentication, transmission security); breach notice to individuals within 60 days
GDPRPersonal data of people in the EUArticle 32: security "appropriate to the risk", including encryption, resilience, restore and regular testing; Article 33: notify the authority within 72 hours of a breach
NIS2 (Directive 2022/2555)Essential and important entities in the EUTen minimum measures, including "supply chain security" and multi-factor authentication; incident reports at 24 hours, 72 hours and one month
DORA (Regulation 2022/2554)EU financial entities, from 17 January 2025ICT risk management, incident reporting, resilience testing and oversight of critical ICT providers
FedRAMPCloud services sold to US federal agenciesSP 800-53 baselines; now called "FedRAMP Certification", moving to "20x" with Key Security Indicators shown "in near real time"; new Rev5 certifications end in June 2027
EU Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation 2024/2847)Products with digital elements sold in the EUSecure by design, vulnerability handling, an SBOM, at least five years of support; reporting of exploited vulnerabilities since 11 September 2026, main obligations from 11 December 2027 (below)

"72 hours" means different things. It's the GDPR deadline to notify the supervisory authority, the second step after a 24-hour early warning under NIS2 and the CRA, and, in the proposed HIPAA update, a target for restoring systems. HIPAA's notice to individuals allows up to 60 days. Also: NIS2 is a directive (each country writes its own law; the deadline was 17 October 2024), while DORA and the CRA are regulations that apply directly.

Proposals and guidance, not new rules. The 2025 HIPAA Security Rule proposal (published 6 January 2025) would remove the "addressable" category and require encryption, MFA and 72-hour restore plans, but it's still only a proposal: the current rule still lists encryption as "Addressable" (which never meant optional). PCI's "Guidance for Containers and Container Orchestration Tools" is a 2022 information supplement that adds no requirements.

Mapping to Kubernetes controls

Topic 6.1Turning framework language into cluster settings, using card-payment requirements as the example.

Most frameworks ask for the same kinds of control. This table maps the PCI DSS requirement areas (paraphrased) to the Kubernetes features covered in this guide; the same mapping works for ISO 27001 Annex A or SOC 2.

Requirement areaKubernetes controls
Network security controls, segmentation of card dataSeparate namespaces or clusters for payment workloads, default-deny NetworkPolicy (topic 3.4)
Secure configurationCIS checks, Pod Security Standards, admission policies (topic 5.4)
Protect stored data and data in transitSecrets encryption with KMS, volume encryption, TLS and mesh mTLS (topic 3.3, 5.3)
Malware protection and secure softwareImage scanning, runtime detection, signed images, patching (topic 1.3, 5.1)
Restrict access by need to know; identify and authenticate usersLeast-privilege RBAC, OIDC with MFA, no shared or system:masters credentials (topic 3.2)
Log and monitor all accessAPI audit logging, shipped off-cluster and protected (topic 3.5)
Test security regularlyScheduled benchmark scans, penetration tests, runtime alerts
Policies and programPolicy as code in Git, reviewed changes, documented exceptions

Shrink the scope. Auditors assess everything that handles or can reach card data. Keeping payment workloads in their own namespaces, nodes or cluster, with strict network policy around them, reduces what's in scope for PCI.

Showing compliance

Topic 6.1Evidence, continuous checks, and who is responsible for what on a managed service.

The whitepaper says evidence gathering "should be automated with non-repudiation guarantees whenever possible", and lists "immutable audit logging" among finance's needs. Typical evidence from a cluster:

  • API audit logs, kept off-cluster in write-once storage;
  • benchmark and scan results (CIS, NSA/CISA, Pod Security), and admission policy reports on existing objects;
  • signed attestations: provenance, SBOMs, VEX and test results (below);
  • change history from Git, and records of approved exceptions.

Traditional audits are point-in-time: a SOC 2 or a yearly PCI assessment. Continuous compliance (an industry term, not the whitepaper's) replaces that with scheduled scans and background policy checks; FedRAMP 20x's Key Security Indicators and machine-readable formats such as NIST's OSCAL push in the same direction. The tools that do this are covered in topic 6.2.

The provider's certificate covers the provider's part. A managed Kubernetes provider's SOC 2 report, PCI attestation, ISO certificate or FedRAMP certification covers what it runs: data centers, the control plane, etcd. You still need evidence for your own controls: RBAC, workloads, network policies, images, Secrets and logging. You also can't run control plane CIS checks on a managed service, which is why the CIS has separate benchmarks for each major provider.

Supply chain compliance

Topic 6.1The rules for how software is developed and documented.

NIST Secure Software Development Framework (SP 800-218)

The SSDF v1.1 (February 2022) is the reference most supply chain rules point to. It has four practice groups:

GroupAimExamples
PO: Prepare the Organization"People, processes, and technology are prepared to perform secure software development"Secure toolchains (PO.3), secure build environments (PO.5)
PS: Protect the Software"Protect all components of their software from tampering and unauthorized access"Protect code (PS.1), let users verify releases (PS.2), keep provenance for each release (PS.3)
PW: Produce Well-Secured Software"Well-secured software with minimal security vulnerabilities in its releases"Reuse well-secured components (PW.4), review and test code (PW.7, PW.8), secure defaults (PW.9)
RV: Respond to VulnerabilitiesFind remaining vulnerabilities, fix them, and prevent similar onesIdentify, remediate, find root causes (RV.1 to RV.3)

A v1.2 draft (December 2025) adds two practices, continuous improvement (PO.6) and reliable updates (PS.4), but isn't final. The SSDF cites the CNCF's 2021 supply chain paper as a reference.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act

In force since 10 December 2024. Manufacturers must "identify and document vulnerabilities and components contained in products with digital elements, including by drawing up a software bill of materials in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the top-level dependencies". The SBOM goes in the technical documentation for authorities; publishing it is optional. Actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents must be reported through ENISA's single reporting platform (live since 11 September 2026) with an early warning within 24 hours and a notification within 72 hours. Fines reach EUR 15 million or 2.5% of worldwide turnover. Open source "stewards" that support projects used commercially get a lighter regime.

United States: from mandate to option

  1. EO 14028 (May 2021)Ordered secure development guidance (the SSDF), SBOM minimum elements and supplier attestation.
  2. OMB M-22-18 and M-23-16 (2022, 2023)Required agencies to collect a self-attestation from software producers.
  3. CISA attestation form (March 2024)Signed by the CEO or a designee, covering four areas: secure build environments (including MFA), a trusted source code supply chain, provenance, and automated vulnerability checks with a disclosure program. It doesn't require an SBOM.
  4. EO 14144 (January 2025), amended by EO 14306 (June 2025)EO 14306 removed the planned machine-readable attestation and CISA validation regime, and ordered the SSDF update.
  5. OMB M-26-05 (23 January 2026)"OMB Memoranda M-22-18 and M-23-16 ... are hereby rescinded." Agencies "may choose to use" the form and may ask for an SBOM by contract.

The federal attestation is now optional. Material from 2022 to 2025 presents the CISA self-attestation as mandatory for software sold to US agencies. Since OMB M-26-05, it's one option in each agency's risk-based approach. SBOM minimum elements themselves moved on too: CISA's 2026 version replaced NTIA's 2021 list (topic 5.1).

Attestations as evidence

Topic 6.1Turning build records into audit evidence.

The CNCF supply chain paper: "Attestations are signed records of actions that occurred in the software supply chain", and "If a vulnerability is discovered later, attestations can be used as an audit trail to determine where in the supply chain the vulnerability was introduced." Test results "should be captured as signed, verifiable attestations". For an auditor or customer, these answer the questions every supply chain rule asks:

QuestionEvidence
Where and how was it built?SLSA provenance (Build L2 or L3 for signed, hardened builds); SSDF PS.3, CRA technical documentation
What's in it?A signed SBOM attestation (SPDX or CycloneDX); CRA, CISA minimum elements
Is it affected by this CVE?VEX statements and scan results
Was it tested and reviewed?Test and review attestations (in-toto)
Is this the artifact that was checked?Signatures verified at admission, pinned digests (topic 5.1)
Check an image's SLSA provenance (shell) 
IMG=registry.example.com/shop/api
IMG=$IMG@sha256:<digest>
# slsaprovenance1 = SLSA v1 format
cosign verify-attestation \
  --type slsaprovenance1 \
  --key cosign.pub "$IMG" \
  | jq -r .payload | base64 -d \
  | jq .predicateType

Pick the right predicate type. In cosign, --type slsaprovenance means the old SLSA v0.2 format; v1 provenance needs slsaprovenance1. Similarly, use spdxjson or cyclonedx for SBOM attestations.

Key takeaways

  • "A compliant system is not guaranteed to be secure, nor a secure system is guaranteed to be compliant." Benchmarks are "a guide rather than a checklist"; automate evidence "with non-repudiation".
  • Frameworks: NIST CSF 2.0 (six functions, Govern new), SP 800-53 (controls), ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (93 Annex A controls, four themes), SOC 2 (five Trust Services Criteria), CIS benchmark 2.1.0, DISA STIG, NSA/CISA v1.2.
  • Regulations: PCI DSS 4.0.1 (12 requirements), HIPAA (three safeguard types; 2025 proposal not final), GDPR (Art. 32 security, Art. 33 72-hour notice), NIS2 (directive; 24 h / 72 h / one month), DORA (regulation, January 2025), FedRAMP ("Certification", 20x), EU CRA.
  • Map requirements to cluster controls: segmentation and NetworkPolicy, CIS and PSS, encryption, scanning and signing, least-privilege RBAC with MFA, audit logs, regular testing, policy as code. Shrink scope by isolating regulated workloads.
  • Evidence: off-cluster audit logs, scan and policy reports, signed attestations, Git history. A provider's certificate covers only its share; managed control planes need provider-specific benchmarks.
  • SSDF: PO, PS, PW, RV (v1.1; v1.2 draft adds PO.6 and PS.4). CRA: SBOM of at least top-level dependencies, exploited-vulnerability reporting since September 2026 (24 h / 72 h), full obligations December 2027. US: CISA self-attestation (four areas, no SBOM) made optional by OMB M-26-05 (January 2026).
  • Attestations (SLSA provenance, SBOMs, VEX, tests) are supply chain audit evidence; verify with cosign (slsaprovenance1 for SLSA v1).
