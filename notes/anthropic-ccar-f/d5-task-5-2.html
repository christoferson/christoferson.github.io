5.2: Escalation and Ambiguity Resolution

When a support agent should hand over to a person and when it should keep going: requests for a human, gaps in policy, being stuck, and the signals that look like reasons to escalate but aren't. Plus what to do when a lookup can't tell two customers apart.

Overview

Task 5.2

Design effective escalation and ambiguity resolution patterns.

The exam guide asks you to know three things. The real escalation triggers are a customer asking for a person, a policy gap or exception (not merely a complex case), and being unable to make progress. An explicit demand for a human is honoured at once, while a straightforward issue gets an offer to resolve it. And sentiment and self-reported confidence are unreliable stand-ins for how hard a case is. When a lookup returns several customers, the agent asks for another identifier instead of picking one. In practice it asks you to:

  • write explicit escalation criteria with few-shot examples into the system prompt;
  • escalate immediately on a request for a person;
  • acknowledge frustration but offer the fix first;
  • escalate when policy is silent or ambiguous;
  • instruct the agent to ask for more identifiers when there are several matches.

Review queues and calibrating confidence for extraction are task 5.5; few-shot technique in general is task 4.2.

Sources: the exam guide for the triggers and anti-patterns; Claude's customer support agent guide and prompting best practices for the supporting practice, checked on 19 November 2026 and paraphrased. The agent is my own and was tested with anthropic 2.10.0 against a mocked API.

Running example. The outdoor-gear shop's support agent handles returns, refunds and lost deliveries. It can look customers up and open a ticket for the human team.

The three triggers

Task 5.2When handing over is the right answer.

  • The customer asks for a person. That's their decision, not the agent's.
  • Policy doesn't cover the request, or covers it ambiguously. The agent has no rule to apply, so a person with authority has to decide. What counts is the policy, not how complicated the case is: a long, multi-part refund that policy fully covers isn't a reason to escalate.
  • No meaningful progress: the tools can't do what's needed, or the same step keeps failing. Going round again helps nobody.

Everything else the agent should try to resolve itself. Escalating too readily defeats the point of the agent, and too rarely leaves customers stuck.

Criteria and examples in the prompt

Task 5.2Explicit rules, shown in action.

escalation_agent.py (Python, anthropic SDK) 
"""Support agent with explicit escalation criteria, few-shot examples, and a lookup that never guesses between customers."""
import json

import anthropic

client = anthropic.Anthropic()

SYSTEM = """You resolve returns, refunds and delivery problems for an outdoor-gear shop.

<escalation_rules>
Call escalate_to_human when any of these is true:
1. The customer asks for a person. Do it straight away: don't investigate or try to resolve first.
2. Policy doesn't cover the request, or covers it ambiguously. Example: the price-match policy covers our own
   site's price drops only, so a request to match a competitor's price is a gap, not a "no".
3. You can't make progress: the tools can't do what's needed, or the same step has failed twice.
Do NOT escalate just because the customer is upset or the case has several parts. If the issue is within the
returns and refunds policy, acknowledge the frustration and offer the fix. Escalate if they then repeat that
they want a person.
</escalation_rules>

<identity_rule>
If find_customer returns more than one match, ask for one more identifier (order number or postcode).
Never pick a match by name similarity, recency or any other guess.
</identity_rule>

<examples>
<example>
Customer: I want to talk to a real person.
Agent: [calls escalate_to_human, reason "customer_request"] I'm passing you to a colleague now.
</example>
<example>
Customer: This is ridiculous, the footprint tore after one trip!
Agent: I'm sorry, that's frustrating. It's within the 60-day window, so I can refund the 89.00 to your card
now. Shall I go ahead?
</example>
<example>
Customer: Rival A sells this tent for 40 less. Will you match it?
Agent: [calls escalate_to_human, reason "policy_gap"] Our price policy doesn't cover other shops' prices, so
I've asked a colleague to decide. They'll reply today.
</example>
<example>
Customer: The stove never came. My name is Sam Lee.
Agent: [find_customer returns two matches] I've found more than one account under that name. Could you give
me the order number or your postcode?
</example>
</examples>"""

TOOLS = [
    {"name": "find_customer", "description": "Look up customers by name, email, order number or postcode. "
     "Returns every match; more than one match means you must ask for another identifier.",
     "input_schema": {"type": "object", "properties": {"query": {"type": "string"}}, "required": ["query"]}},
    {"name": "escalate_to_human", "description": "Hand the conversation to a human agent with a summary.",
     "input_schema": {"type": "object", "properties": {
         "reason": {"type": "string", "enum": ["customer_request", "policy_gap", "no_progress"]},
         "summary": {"type": "string", "description": "Order IDs, amounts, what was tried, what the customer wants"}},
         "required": ["reason", "summary"]}},
]

CUSTOMERS = [{"id": "C-17", "name": "Sam Lee", "postcode": "AB1 2CD"}, {"id": "C-88", "name": "Sam Lee", "postcode": "XY9 8ZW"}]
handoffs = []


def find_customer(query: str) -> dict:
    q = query.lower()
    matches = [c for c in CUSTOMERS if q in (c["name"].lower(), c["postcode"].lower(), c["id"].lower())]
    if len(matches) > 1:
        # No IDs or postcodes returned, so there's nothing to guess from: the only way forward is to ask.
        return {"status": "multiple_matches", "count": len(matches),
                "next_step": "Ask the customer for an order number or postcode."}
    return {"status": "found", "customer": matches[0]} if matches else {"status": "not_found"}


def run_tool(name: str, args: dict) -> dict:
    if name == "find_customer":
        return find_customer(args["query"])
    handoffs.append(args)                       # in production: open a ticket and route it to the human queue
    return {"status": "queued", "ticket": f"T-{len(handoffs)}"}


def turn(messages: list[dict]) -> str:
    while True:
        response = client.messages.create(model="claude-sonnet-5-5", max_tokens=1500,
                                          system=SYSTEM, tools=TOOLS, messages=messages)
        messages.append({"role": "assistant", "content": response.content})
        if response.stop_reason != "tool_use":
            return "".join(b.text for b in response.content if b.type == "text")
        messages.append({"role": "user", "content": [
            {"type": "tool_result", "tool_use_id": b.id, "content": json.dumps(run_tool(b.name, b.input))}
            for b in response.content if b.type == "tool_use"]})
  • Explicit criteria: the three triggers are listed, and so is what is not a trigger (an upset customer, a multi-part case). A vague "escalate when appropriate" leaves the model to guess, and it guesses inconsistently.
  • Few-shot examples: four short exchanges show the boundary, one for each hard decision. Claude's support-agent guide recommends at least four or five sample good interactions in the prompt, including guardrails and tool calls.
  • A dedicated tool: escalate_to_human takes a reason from a fixed list and a summary with order IDs, amounts and what was tried. The person who picks it up doesn't have to start over (task 1.4 covers handoff summaries).

A request for a person vs frustration

Task 5.2Honour the first at once; answer the second with a fix.

  • "I want to talk to a person": escalate immediately. Don't look the order up first, try to solve it, or ask why. Investigating before handing over makes the customer repeat a request they already made clearly.
  • "This is ridiculous, it tore after one trip!": frustration, not a request. If the issue is within the agent's powers (a refund inside the return window), acknowledge the feeling and offer the fix. If the customer then says again that they want a person, escalate.

Two opposite wrong answers. Trying to resolve the issue after an explicit request for a human ignores the customer. Escalating every angry message sends easy cases to the human queue that the agent could have fixed in one turn.

Policy gaps

Task 5.2Silent or ambiguous policy means a person decides.

The shop adjusts the price if its own site drops the price within 14 days. A customer asks it to match a competitor. The policy doesn't say yes, and it doesn't say no: it's silent. The agent shouldn't extend the rule ("it's a price adjustment, so yes"), and it shouldn't refuse on the policy's behalf ("not covered, so no"). Both invent policy. The right move is to escalate with the details, and the example tells the customer a colleague will decide.

The same applies to exceptions: a return a week outside the window from a long-standing customer, or a damaged item with no photo. If policy allows exceptions, someone with the authority to grant them decides.

Signals that mislead

Task 5.2Why sentiment and self-reported confidence don't decide escalation.

  • Sentiment measures how the customer feels, not how hard the case is. An angry customer with a simple refund gets escalated needlessly; a polite customer whose request falls in a policy gap doesn't get escalated at all.
  • Self-reported confidence ("rate your confidence 1 to 10, escalate below 7") measures how sure the model says it is. A model can be confidently wrong about a case policy doesn't cover, and unsure about an easy one. The guide's point is that neither number tracks actual complexity.

The reliable signals are the three triggers, which are facts about the conversation: did the customer ask, does policy cover this, is anything moving. Sentiment still has a use as a quality metric: the support guide measures it at the start and end of conversations, not as a routing rule.

Several matching customers

Task 5.2Ask for another identifier; never guess.

Two accounts are called Sam Lee. Picking one by a heuristic (the most recent order, the closer name match, the first result) risks refunding the wrong person or disclosing someone else's order. The rule in the prompt says to ask for an order number or postcode instead. The example also enforces it in the tool: when find_customer finds more than one match it returns only the count and the next step, not the candidates, so the model has nothing to choose between. That's the same structural thinking as task 1.4: where a mistake is costly, don't rely on the prompt alone.

Handing over and measuring

Task 5.2A useful handoff and a way to tell whether the criteria work.

  • The handoff carries the case: the reason, order IDs, amounts, what was tried and what the customer wants (the case facts from task 5.1). The customer shouldn't have to repeat themselves.
  • Measure escalation accuracy: the support guide suggests tracking correctly escalated conversations against those that should have been escalated but weren't, aiming for 95% or more. It also tracks deflection, the share handled without a person (typically 70 to 80%). Look at both: chasing deflection alone rewards not escalating.
  • Refine with examples: when reviews find a wrong decision, add the case, or a paraphrase of it, as a new example on the right side of the line.

Key takeaways

  • Escalate on three triggers: the customer asks for a person, policy is silent, ambiguous or needs an exception, or the agent can't make progress. Complexity alone isn't one.
  • An explicit request for a human is honoured immediately, with no investigation first. Frustration about a fixable issue gets acknowledgement and an offer; escalate if the customer repeats the request.
  • When policy is silent (competitor price matching under an own-site policy), escalate rather than stretching or refusing on the policy's behalf.
  • Sentiment and self-reported confidence are unreliable escalation signals; they don't measure case complexity.
  • Several matching customers: ask for another identifier, never pick heuristically. Put criteria and few-shot examples in the system prompt, and measure escalation accuracy.
