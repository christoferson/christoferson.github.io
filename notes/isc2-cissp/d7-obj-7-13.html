Overview

Objective 7.13

7.13 Participate in Business Continuity (BC) planning exercises. The objective has no sub-bullets.

Business impact analysis is on the 1.7 page; DR testing on the 7.12 page; continuity basics in CC 2.2. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its crisis management team, led by the chief operating officer, runs two business continuity exercises a year, one of them a cyber scenario.

Business continuity planning and the security role

Objective 7.13Where business continuity sits, and why security professionals take part.

Business continuity (BC) keeps the organization's critical functions running during and after a disruption, whatever its cause: pandemic, supplier failure, loss of a building, or cyberattack. It is broader than disaster recovery, which restores IT systems. ISO 22301 sets requirements for a business continuity management system (BCMS), and SP 800-34 places the BCP, the DRP, the incident response plan and others in one family of contingency plans.

  • Inputs: the BIA (critical functions, MTD, RTO, RPO) and risk assessment (objective 1.7).
  • Plans: continuity strategies for people, premises, technology, information, suppliers and communications, and a crisis management structure for decisions.
  • Exercises: practice of the plans and decisions by the people involved, which is what this objective asks security professionals to take part in.

Security professionals contribute knowledge of cyber scenarios, IT recovery capabilities and dependencies, make sure security and privacy controls continue in continuity mode, and bring lessons back into security plans.

Types of exercise

Objective 7.13Choosing the right exercise for the goal and the maturity of the plan.

FEMA's Homeland Security Exercise and Evaluation Program (HSEEP) groups exercises into two kinds:

  • Discussion-based: seminars (orienting people to plans), workshops (building a product such as a plan or policy), tabletop exercises (talking through a scenario and decisions) and games. Low cost and low risk, good for new plans and leadership teams.
  • Operations-based: drills (practicing one function, such as a call tree or evacuation), functional exercises (testing coordination and command in a realistic, time-pressured simulation without moving real resources), and full-scale exercises (real people and resources deployed across organizations).

The HSEEP cycle is design and development, conduct, evaluation, and improvement planning, ending in an after-action report and improvement plan. Programs usually progress from discussion-based to operations-based exercises as plans mature, a building-block approach.

NIST SP 800-84 gives a similar approach for IT plans: tests, training and exercises, with tabletop and functional exercises as the main exercise types.

Running a continuity exercise

Objective 7.13Who does what during an exercise, and how to make it realistic.

  • Roles: a facilitator guides discussion; players respond as they would for real; controllers manage the scenario and deliver injects (new developments such as "the media has the story" or "backups are also encrypted"); evaluators observe against objectives and record findings.
  • Scenario: plausible for the organization, based on its risk assessment, and stressful enough to force real decisions and trade-offs.
  • Objectives: specific capabilities to test, such as "decide within one hour whether to close online sales" or "notify the regulator within the required time".
  • No-fault environment: players are encouraged to make decisions and reveal gaps; the exercise tests the plan and the organization, not individuals.
  • Hot wash: an immediate debrief at the end while memories are fresh, followed by the formal after-action report.

The retail group's last exercise simulated ransomware during the holiday peak. Injects included encrypted store systems, a ransom demand, a journalist's call and the loss of the email system. The crisis team found that nobody had the authority to switch stores to offline card processing; the plan now names who does.

Cyber scenarios and the decisions they force

Objective 7.13Bringing cyber risks into continuity exercises, where many decisions are business decisions.

  • Ransomware: which services to shut down, how long the business can run manually, whether to consider paying, and who decides.
  • Data breach: notification deadlines, customer communications, regulator contact and legal privilege.
  • Supplier compromise: a critical SaaS or managed service provider is down or breached; what workarounds exist and what the contract says.
  • Destructive attack: wiped systems and backups, testing the clean rebuild strategy and the minimum viable company.
  • Loss of communications: email and chat unavailable or compromised, testing out-of-band channels.

These exercises show leaders that cyber incidents are business continuity events, and test the links between the incident response plan, the DR plan and the crisis management plan.

Suppliers and improvement

Objective 7.13Extending exercises to the supply chain and making sure lessons change things.

  • Supplier continuity: ask critical suppliers for their continuity plans and test results, include them in exercises where possible, and plan for a supplier that cannot deliver.
  • Concentration risk: many functions may depend on the same cloud provider, payment processor or logistics company; exercises reveal where one failure stops several processes.
  • Improvement plan: each finding becomes an action with an owner and date, tracked by the continuity manager and reported to leadership.
  • Training: gaps in knowledge found in exercises feed training for crisis team members and staff.
  • Program cycle: exercise results feed plan updates, the BIA and the next exercise's objectives.

Crisis management structure

Objective 7.13Who makes which decisions when a disruption becomes a crisis.

Many organizations use three levels of decision making, sometimes called strategic, tactical and operational (or gold, silver and bronze):

  • Strategic: the crisis management team of senior leaders sets priorities, makes major decisions (closing operations, public statements, large spending, ransom questions) and handles reputation, regulators and the board.
  • Tactical: managers translate priorities into plans, coordinate between teams and allocate resources.
  • Operational: teams carry out recovery, incident response and business workarounds.

The plan names members and alternates for each level, how the crisis team is convened, where it meets (physically or virtually, out of band), and how decisions are logged. Security and IT leaders usually sit on the crisis team for cyber scenarios and advise it in others. Exercises test whether the levels work together: strategic teams that get drawn into technical detail, or technical teams that wait for decisions nobody has been asked to make, are common findings.

People and workplaces

Objective 7.13Planning for the people who keep critical functions running.

  • Staff availability: plans assume that some people will be unavailable (illness, travel, affected by the same disaster), so critical roles have trained deputies.
  • Key person dependency: knowledge that sits with one person is documented and shared, and exercises sometimes remove a key person on purpose.
  • Work-area recovery: remote working, alternate offices or contracted recovery seats, with managed devices and secure connectivity.
  • Welfare: staff safety and wellbeing come first; long incidents need shifts, rest and support so decisions stay sound.
  • Succession: delegation of authority if leaders are unavailable is written down in advance.

At the retail group, a pandemic-style exercise showed that payroll could be run by only one person; a second was trained and the procedure documented the following month.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the first exercise
Situation
A company has just written its first business continuity plan and wants to test it.
Best response
Start with a tabletop exercise with the crisis team, then move to drills and functional exercises as the plan matures.
Why
A building-block approach progresses from discussion-based to operations-based exercises.
Scenario 2: security left out
Situation
A business continuity exercise plans to relocate staff to a temporary office with home routers and personal laptops.
Best response
Take part in planning so security requirements (managed devices, secure connectivity, access control) are built into the continuity arrangements.
Why
Security professionals participate so controls continue in continuity mode.
Scenario 3: blame in the debrief
Situation
After an exercise, managers want to name staff who made poor decisions.
Best response
Keep a no-fault approach: record gaps in plans, training and authority, and fix them through the improvement plan.
Why
Exercises test the organization; blame discourages honest participation.
Scenario 4: the cloud provider outage
Situation
An exercise shows that point of sale, ordering and the loyalty program all depend on one cloud provider region.
Best response
Record the concentration risk, assess options (multi-region, offline modes, alternate providers) and assign owners in the improvement plan.
Why
Exercises reveal dependencies and concentration risk.

Key takeaways

  • BC keeps critical functions running whatever the cause; broader than DR; ISO 22301 BCMS; inputs from the BIA and risk assessment.
  • Exercises (HSEEP): discussion-based (seminars, workshops, tabletops, games) and operations-based (drills, functional, full-scale); building-block progression; after-action report and improvement plan.
  • Running them: facilitator, players, controllers with injects, evaluators; plausible scenario, specific objectives, no-fault, hot wash.
  • Cyber scenarios: ransomware, breach, supplier compromise, destructive attack, loss of communications; business decisions and plan links.
  • Suppliers and improvement: supplier plans and joint exercises, concentration risk, owned actions, training, program cycle. Security keeps controls working in continuity mode.
