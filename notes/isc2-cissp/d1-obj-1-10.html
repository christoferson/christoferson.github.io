Overview

Objective 1.10

1.10 Understand and apply threat modeling concepts and methodologies.

Threat modeling feeds risk management (objective 1.9) and secure design (objective 3.1); its role in testing is covered in CC 5.5. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is designing a mobile app that lets customers scan and pay in store.

What threat modeling is

Objective 1.10Understanding the purpose of threat modeling and when it gives the most value.

NIST describes threat modeling as "a form of risk assessment that models aspects of the attack and defense sides of a logical entity" (SP 800-53). The Threat Modeling Manifesto puts it more plainly: "analyzing representations of a system to highlight concerns about security and privacy characteristics", organized around four questions:

  1. What are we working on?Model the system: components, data flows, trust boundaries.
  2. What can go wrong?Identify threats with a structured method.
  3. What are we going to do about it?Decide responses and design mitigations.
  4. Did we do a good enough job?Review and validate the model and the mitigations.

It is cheapest and most useful early, during design, when changes cost little; NIST's guidance on developer verification recommends "using threat modeling early" and repeating it "multiple times during development". It should be revisited when the design changes significantly, after incidents, and when new threats emerge. Approaches are often described by their focus:

  • Asset-centric: start from what is valuable and ask how it could be harmed (common in organizational risk methods such as OCTAVE).
  • Attacker-centric: start from likely adversaries, their goals and techniques (attack trees, ATT&CK).
  • System- or software-centric: start from the design and examine each component and flow (STRIDE on data flow diagrams).
  • Data-centric: focus on specific data and every place it is stored, processed and transmitted (NIST SP 800-154, still a 2016 draft that NIST said in 2025 it plans to finalize).

The threat modeling process

Objective 1.10Modeling a system so threats can be found systematically.

Most system-centric methods start with a data flow diagram (DFD) built with the architects and developers:

  • External entities: users, partners and outside systems the team does not control.
  • Processes: code that handles data, such as the app's payment service.
  • Data stores: databases, files, caches, queues, logs.
  • Data flows: how data moves between them, with protocols.
  • Trust boundaries: lines where the level of trust changes, such as between the internet and the API gateway, between services, or between the app and the phone's operating system. Most threats cluster where data crosses a boundary.

The team then walks each element and flow with the chosen method, records each threat with its affected element, rates it, chooses a response, and tracks mitigations into the backlog. The model, threats and decisions are kept as living documentation and revisited when the design changes.

Threat model summary: scan-and-pay app (retail group)
Scope
Mobile app, API gateway, basket and payment services, token vault, store exit gate check
Trust boundaries
Phone to internet; internet to API gateway; gateway to internal services; services to payment processor
Top threats
Tampered app altering prices (tampering); stolen session tokens (spoofing); replayed payment confirmations (spoofing, repudiation); scraping other customers' baskets (information disclosure)
Responses
Server-side pricing only; short-lived tokens bound to device; signed, single-use receipts checked at the exit gate; object-level authorization on every API call

STRIDE

Objective 1.10Using the most common threat classification and mapping it to the security property each threat violates.

STRIDE, created at Microsoft and used in its Threat Modeling Tool and Security Development Lifecycle, gives six threat categories, each the opposite of a security property:

ThreatViolatesExample and typical mitigation
SpoofingAuthenticationUsing stolen credentials; strong, phishing-resistant authentication
TamperingIntegrityAltering prices in a request; server-side validation, signatures, MACs
RepudiationNon-repudiationDenying a transaction; protected logs, digital signatures
Information disclosureConfidentialityReading other users' data; access control, encryption
Denial of serviceAvailabilityFlooding the API; rate limiting, scaling, DDoS protection
Elevation of privilegeAuthorizationGaining admin rights through a flaw; least privilege, input validation, patching

STRIDE is usually applied per element of the DFD: external entities are mainly exposed to spoofing, data stores to tampering, disclosure and denial of service, and data flows to tampering, disclosure and denial of service. Its strength is completeness and ease of learning; its weakness is that it finds threats without ranking them, so it is paired with a rating method.

Other methodologies

Objective 1.10Knowing what each common method is for, so the right one is chosen for the question.

  • PASTA (Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis): a risk-centric, multi-stage method that starts from business objectives, models the application and its attack surface, analyzes threats and vulnerabilities, simulates attacks and ends with risk and impact analysis. Good for linking threats to business impact.
  • Attack trees: an attacker's goal at the root, with branches for the ways to reach it (AND and OR nodes), annotated with cost, difficulty or likelihood. Good for analyzing one high-value target in depth.
  • LINDDUN (KU Leuven): a privacy threat framework built on STRIDE principles: Linking, Identifying, Non-repudiation, Detecting, Data disclosure, Unawareness (and unintervenability), and Non-compliance. Supports privacy by design; available as a lightweight card game (GO) and a systematic DFD-based version (PRO).
  • DREAD: rates threats on Damage, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected users and Discoverability, each 1–10, averaged. Simple, but criticized as subjective; Microsoft reportedly moved away from it internally, though some Microsoft guidance still teaches it.
  • VAST (Visual, Agile and Simple Threat modeling): designed to scale across many agile teams, with separate application and operational threat models.
  • OCTAVE (Carnegie Mellon SEI, with OCTAVE Allegro): organization-wide, asset-focused risk assessment for security strategy rather than design-level threat modeling.
  • MITRE ATT&CK: a knowledge base of real adversary tactics and techniques; used to make threat models realistic, check detection coverage and plan red team exercises. MITRE ATLAS does the same for attacks on AI systems.

Matching the method to the need. Design review of software: STRIDE on a DFD. Privacy: LINDDUN. Business-risk view: PASTA. One critical target: attack tree. Real-world adversary behavior and detection coverage: ATT&CK. Organization-wide asset risk: OCTAVE.

Rating and responding to threats

Objective 1.10Prioritizing the threats found and choosing what to do about each.

Threats are ranked by likelihood and impact (OWASP's approach), with DREAD scores, with CVSS-style severity for known vulnerabilities, or with the organization's own risk matrix so results feed the risk register directly. Then each threat gets one of the standard responses:

  • Mitigate: change the design or add controls (the most common).
  • Eliminate: remove the feature or component that creates the threat.
  • Transfer: rely on another party or component better placed to handle it, such as a payment provider's tokenization.
  • Accept: record the decision and owner when the risk is within tolerance.

Mitigations become requirements and tests: each high-rated threat should have a matching security requirement, a test or misuse case that proves the mitigation works (objective 6.2), and an owner. Unaddressed threats are visible risks, not forgotten ones.

Threat modeling AI systems

Objective 1.10Extending threat models to cover models, training data, prompts and AI agents.

AI systems add elements to the model and new threats at each boundary:

  • Training data and pipelines: poisoning and backdoors (integrity), leakage of personal or confidential data (confidentiality).
  • The model: extraction or theft of weights, membership inference, evasion with crafted inputs.
  • Prompts and outputs: prompt injection, including indirect injection through documents and web pages the model reads; disclosure of sensitive data in answers; harmful or wrong output.
  • Agents and tools: an AI agent with tool access can be manipulated into taking actions, so its permissions, approval steps and logging are part of the model.

MITRE ATLAS catalogs real techniques against AI systems in ATT&CK style, and NIST's adversarial machine learning taxonomy (AI 100-2) classifies attacks by goal; both help populate the "what can go wrong" step. At the retail group, the threat model for its customer service chatbot added a trust boundary around the order lookup tool, which the bot may call only for the authenticated customer's own orders.

Making threat modeling routine

Objective 1.10Building threat modeling into how teams work, so it happens without a special project.

  • Triggers: new systems, new external integrations, new data types, major architecture changes, and annual review for high-risk systems.
  • Lightweight sessions: a one-hour session with the team using a diagram and four questions (what are we building, what can go wrong, what are we going to do about it, did we do a good job) is often enough for a feature.
  • Outputs into the backlog: each mitigation becomes a ticket or requirement with an owner, so it is tracked like other work.
  • Templates and libraries: reusable threat lists for common patterns (web app, API, mobile, AI assistant) speed sessions up.
  • Facilitation: security champions or the application security team facilitate, while the people who build the system do the thinking.

Scenario check

PracticeChoosing threat modeling methods and responses in situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: when to model
Situation
The scan-and-pay app is two weeks from launch and has never been threat modeled. The project manager suggests a penetration test instead.
Best response
Run a focused threat model now on the highest-risk flows (payment and exit check) to guide the penetration test's scope, and make threat modeling part of design for future releases.
Why
Threat modeling is most valuable early, but even late it finds design flaws a test may not, and it tells testers where to look.
Scenario 2: the privacy question
Situation
A new loyalty feature tracks customers' movements around stores. Legal asks how to identify the privacy threats.
Best answer
Use LINDDUN on the feature's data flows to find linkability, identifiability, detectability and unawareness threats, alongside a privacy impact assessment.
Why
LINDDUN is designed for privacy threats; STRIDE focuses on security properties and can miss harms from authorized but intrusive processing.
Scenario 3: the price in the request
Situation
The threat model shows the app sends item prices to the server, which charges what it receives.
Best answer
This is a tampering threat across a trust boundary. Mitigate by looking up prices on the server and ignoring client-supplied prices, and add a misuse case test that sends altered prices.
Why
Data from outside a trust boundary cannot be trusted; integrity must be enforced on the trusted side.
Scenario 4: no time for threat modeling
Situation
A team says it has no time for threat modeling before a new partner API launches next month.
Best response
Run a short focused session on the API with the team, record the top threats and mitigations as backlog items, and schedule a fuller review later.
Why
Lightweight threat modeling fits delivery schedules and catches design flaws early.

Key takeaways

  • Threat modeling: a structured risk assessment of a system's attack and defense sides; four questions (what are we working on, what can go wrong, what will we do, did we do enough); early and repeated.
  • Focus: asset-, attacker-, system- or data-centric (SP 800-154 draft).
  • Process: DFD with external entities, processes, data stores, flows and trust boundaries; walk each element; rate; respond; track to requirements and tests.
  • STRIDE: spoofing (authentication), tampering (integrity), repudiation (non-repudiation), information disclosure (confidentiality), denial of service (availability), elevation of privilege (authorization).
  • Other methods: PASTA (risk-centric, business impact), attack trees (one goal), LINDDUN (privacy), DREAD (rating, subjective), VAST (agile scale), OCTAVE (organizational), ATT&CK and ATLAS (real techniques).
  • Respond: mitigate, eliminate, transfer, accept; each high threat gets a requirement, a test and an owner.
  • AI: model data, models, prompts and agents; poisoning, extraction, prompt injection, tool misuse; ATLAS and AI 100-2.
