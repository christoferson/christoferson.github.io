Overview

Objective 6.1

6.1 Design and validate assessment, test, and audit strategies. Sub-topics: internal (within organization control); external (outside organization control); third-party (outside enterprise control); location (on-premises, cloud, hybrid).

Testing basics are in CC 5.5; the test techniques themselves are on the 6.2 page; control assessment in the RMF on the 1.9 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. PCI DSS requires it to scan and penetration test the card environment, and its board wants independent assurance on security every year.

Designing the strategy

Objective 6.1Planning what to assess, test and audit, by whom, how often, and validating that the plan gives real assurance.

Three related activities give different kinds of assurance:

  • Testing: technical checks that a control works (a scan, a penetration test, a restore test).
  • Assessment: a broader evaluation of controls against requirements, using several methods. NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5 provides assessment procedures for every SP 800-53 control; its methods are commonly summarized as examine (documents and settings), interview (people) and test (exercise the control).
  • Audit: an independent, formal examination against a defined standard (PCI DSS, ISO/IEC 27001, SOC criteria), producing an opinion or report for management, regulators or customers.

A strategy starts from the organization's objectives, risks and obligations and sets: scope (systems, locations, providers), methods, frequency (continuous for scanning and monitoring, periodic for penetration tests and audits, after major changes), independence of the testers, reporting lines, and how findings are tracked to closure. Validating the strategy means checking that it covers the highest risks and every required control, that methods are deep enough to be trusted, and that results lead to fixes.

Management owns the risk; testers report. Testers and auditors identify and report weaknesses. Deciding whether to fix, accept or transfer the risk belongs to management. An auditor who also designed or runs the control they audit has lost independence.

Internal, external and third-party; location

Objective 6.1Choosing assessors by independence and access, and adapting tests to where systems run.

  • Internal (within the organization's control): internal audit and security teams. They know the environment and can test continuously, but are less independent; internal audit should report to the audit committee or board, not to IT.
  • External (outside the organization's control): independent auditors and testing firms the organization engages, such as a PCI qualified security assessor or a penetration testing company. More independent and credible to outsiders, but they need clear scope, rules of engagement and access.
  • Third-party (outside the enterprise's control): assessments of, or by, other parties, such as reviewing a supplier's attestation reports and certifications, sending questionnaires, or a customer or regulator assessing you. SOC reports are the usual evidence: SOC 1 is generally described as covering controls relevant to customers' financial reporting, SOC 2 the AICPA Trust Services Criteria (security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, privacy), Type 1 control design at a point in time, Type 2 operating effectiveness over a period.

Location changes what you can test and how:

  • On-premises: full access to systems and networks; physical testing is possible.
  • Cloud: you test your own part of the shared responsibility model under the provider's testing policy (some activities, such as denial-of-service testing, are usually forbidden), and rely on the provider's attestations for the rest.
  • Hybrid: test the connections and identity links between environments as well as each side, since attackers move along them.

Planning scope, frequency and methods

Objective 6.1Turning the strategy into a calendar of assessments that covers the highest risks.

  • Scope by risk: the most critical systems, sensitive data, internet-facing services and recent changes get the deepest and most frequent assessment; low-risk systems may rely on automated checks.
  • Mix of methods: continuous (vulnerability scanning, configuration compliance, control monitoring), periodic (penetration tests, red team exercises, internal audits), and event-driven (after major changes, incidents, acquisitions or new threats).
  • Obligations: some frequencies are set externally, such as PCI DSS internal and external scans and penetration tests of the cardholder data environment, certification audits, and regulatory examinations.
  • Coverage map: a matrix of systems and control families against assessment methods shows gaps and duplicates; combining assessments (one test satisfying several frameworks) saves effort.
  • Resources and independence: decide what internal teams do, what needs external assessors, and how to rotate external firms so fresh eyes look at the same systems.
  • Rules for every test: authorization, rules of engagement, safety limits for production and OT, communication plans, and handling of findings and test data.

Validating the strategy

Objective 6.1Checking that the testing program actually gives the assurance leadership relies on.

A strategy can look complete on paper and still miss what matters. Validation asks:

  • Do assessments cover the top risks in the risk register and the most critical assets from the BIA?
  • Are methods deep enough for the risk: would they have found the weaknesses in recent incidents and near misses?
  • Are findings fixed on time, and are the same findings reappearing (a sign that root causes are not addressed)?
  • Do red team or attack simulation results match what scans and audits claim (good scan results but undetected red teams mean detection is weak)?
  • Are assessors independent enough, and is their work quality-checked?

Internal audit or an external reviewer can assess the testing program itself, and results feed the next year's plan. Lessons from incidents are the strongest input: if an attacker used a path no test covered, the strategy changes.

Rules of engagement

Objective 6.1Agreeing in writing what a test may and may not do before it starts.

Every technical test, internal or external, needs written authorization from someone with authority over the systems, and rules of engagement (ROE) that both sides sign. Without them, a penetration test is indistinguishable from an attack, and the tester may be breaking the law. The ROE usually cover:

  • Scope: the IP ranges, domains, applications, accounts and locations in scope, and explicit exclusions (fragile systems, systems owned by others, production databases).
  • Timing: test windows, blackout periods (peak trading, month-end close), and whether the test is announced to operations staff or unannounced to test detection.
  • Permitted techniques: whether social engineering, physical entry, password cracking or exploitation of production systems is allowed; denial-of-service is normally excluded.
  • Safety and stop conditions: emergency contacts on both sides, what happens if a test causes an outage or finds evidence of a real compromise (stop and report immediately).
  • Data handling: testers prove access with flags or screenshots instead of copying real cardholder or personal data; test data and reports are encrypted and destroyed after an agreed period.
  • Third parties: written consent from any provider or partner whose systems are touched, and compliance with cloud provider testing policies.
  • Reporting: format, severity scale, delivery to named people only, and a debrief.

Testers carry an authorization letter (sometimes called a "get out of jail" letter) for physical and social engineering tests, naming a contact who can confirm the engagement if they are challenged or detained.

Assessment methods, depth and coverage

Objective 6.1Choosing how thoroughly each control is assessed.

NIST SP 800-53A describes three assessment methods that every assessment combines:

  • Examine: review documents, configurations, logs and records (policies, firewall rules, access review evidence).
  • Interview: discuss with the people who operate the control how it actually works.
  • Test: exercise the control under specified conditions and compare actual with expected behavior (try a login without MFA, restore a backup).

Each method can be applied with different depth (how rigorous and detailed) and coverage (how many items, how broad the sample), described as basic, focused or comprehensive. High-impact systems and controls that many others depend on deserve comprehensive depth and coverage; low-impact systems may get basic assessments. Interviews alone give the weakest assurance; testing gives the strongest.

Assessments can also be announced or unannounced (unannounced tests show whether operations notice), and use different levels of tester knowledge (black, gray or white box, objective 6.2). Ongoing assessment through continuous monitoring (SP 800-137) supplements, but does not replace, periodic in-depth assessment of controls that automated tools cannot check, such as procedures and physical security.

Assessing AI systems

Objective 6.1Adding AI models and applications to the assessment strategy.

  • Inventory first: know which AI models, assistants and AI features in purchased products are in use, and what data and actions they can reach.
  • Evaluations: test accuracy, bias, robustness and safety against defined criteria before release and after changes to models, prompts or data (the Measure function of the NIST AI RMF).
  • AI red teaming: adversarial testing for prompt injection, data leakage, jailbreaks and misuse of connected tools.
  • Conventional testing still applies: the surrounding application, APIs, identity, data stores and pipelines get normal penetration tests and reviews.
  • Supplier evidence: for third-party models and AI services, ask for evaluation results, data handling terms and incident processes.

AI assessments are scheduled by risk like any other system: an assistant that only drafts marketing text needs less than one that can issue refunds.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the auditor who built it
Situation
The security engineer who designed the new access control system is asked to audit it for the board.
Best response
Use an independent assessor (internal audit or an external firm); the engineer can support the audit with evidence.
Why
Assessors must be independent of what they assess for the results to be credible.
Scenario 2: everything once a year
Situation
All systems, from the payment platform to the cafeteria menu site, get the same annual penetration test.
Best response
Scope and frequency by risk: deeper and more frequent testing for critical, internet-facing and card systems; automated checks for low-risk ones.
Why
A risk-based strategy spends assessment effort where it reduces the most risk.
Scenario 3: the cloud penetration test
Situation
The team plans to run denial-of-service tests against a cloud-hosted service next week.
Best response
Check the provider's testing policy first (denial-of-service testing is usually prohibited or needs approval) and limit tests to what the customer is responsible for.
Why
Location changes what may be tested; cloud tests follow the provider's rules and shared responsibility.
Scenario 4: clean scans, silent breach
Situation
Vulnerability scans show few issues, yet a red team reached the domain controllers undetected.
Best response
Rebalance the strategy toward detection and response testing (purple teaming, attack simulations) and fix identity and monitoring gaps.
Why
Validation compares results across methods; scans test prevention, red teams test detection.
Scenario 5: the new AI assistant
Situation
A customer service AI assistant that can issue refunds is about to launch, and the test plan covers only the web application.
Best response
Add AI-specific testing (prompt injection, misuse of the refund tool, data leakage) and controls such as limits and human approval for large refunds.
Why
The assessment strategy must cover new kinds of systems and their specific risks.

Key takeaways

  • Strategy: tests, assessments (SP 800-53A) and audits give different assurance; set scope, methods, frequency, independence and follow-up. Testers report; management decides.
  • Assessors: internal (knowledge, continuity), external (independence), third-party (supplier attestations such as SOC 2 Type 2). Location: cloud testing follows provider policy; test hybrid links.
  • Planning: scope by risk; continuous, periodic and event-driven methods; external obligations; coverage map; rules of engagement for every test.
  • Validation: coverage of top risks, depth, remediation, recurring findings, cross-checking methods, independence, lessons from incidents.
