Overview

Objectives 6.1 and 6.2

6.1 Design and validate assessment, test, and audit strategies. 6.2 Conduct security controls testing.

Testing basics (scanning, penetration testing, rules of engagement, SAST and DAST) are in CC 5.5, and control assessment in the risk framework on the 1.9–1.10 page. This page is about planning a testing program and choosing the right test. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. PCI DSS requires it to scan and penetration test the card environment, and its board wants independent assurance on security every year.

Designing the strategy

Objective 6.1Planning what to assess, test and audit, by whom, how often, and validating that the plan gives real assurance.

Three related activities give different kinds of assurance:

  • Testing: technical checks that a control works (a scan, a penetration test, a restore test).
  • Assessment: a broader evaluation of controls against requirements, using several methods. NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5 provides assessment procedures for every SP 800-53 control; its methods are commonly summarized as examine (documents and settings), interview (people) and test (exercise the control).
  • Audit: an independent, formal examination against a defined standard (PCI DSS, ISO/IEC 27001, SOC criteria), producing an opinion or report for management, regulators or customers.

A strategy starts from the organization's objectives, risks and obligations and sets: scope (systems, locations, providers), methods, frequency (continuous for scanning and monitoring, periodic for penetration tests and audits, after major changes), independence of the testers, reporting lines, and how findings are tracked to closure. Validating the strategy means checking that it covers the highest risks and every required control, that methods are deep enough to be trusted, and that results lead to fixes.

Management owns the risk; testers report. Testers and auditors identify and report weaknesses. Deciding whether to fix, accept or transfer the risk belongs to management. An auditor who also designed or runs the control they audit has lost independence.

Internal, external and third-party; location

Objective 6.1Choosing assessors by independence and access, and adapting tests to where systems run.

  • Internal (within the organization's control): internal audit and security teams. They know the environment and can test continuously, but are less independent; internal audit should report to the audit committee or board, not to IT.
  • External (outside the organization's control): independent auditors and testing firms the organization engages, such as a PCI qualified security assessor or a penetration testing company. More independent and credible to outsiders, but they need clear scope, rules of engagement and access.
  • Third-party (outside the enterprise's control): assessments of, or by, other parties, such as reviewing a supplier's attestation reports and certifications, sending questionnaires, or a customer or regulator assessing you. SOC reports are the usual evidence: SOC 1 is generally described as covering controls relevant to customers' financial reporting, SOC 2 the AICPA Trust Services Criteria (security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, privacy), Type 1 control design at a point in time, Type 2 operating effectiveness over a period.

Location changes what you can test and how:

  • On-premises: full access to systems and networks; physical testing is possible.
  • Cloud: you test your own part of the shared responsibility model under the provider's testing policy (some activities, such as denial-of-service testing, are usually forbidden), and rely on the provider's attestations for the rest.
  • Hybrid: test the connections and identity links between environments as well as each side, since attackers move along them.

Vulnerability assessment and penetration testing

Objective 6.2Finding known weaknesses at scale, and proving through controlled attacks what an attacker could actually achieve.

Vulnerability assessment uses scanners to "identify hosts/host attributes and associated vulnerabilities" (NIST SP 800-115). Credentialed scans see far more than unauthenticated ones; scanning from both inside and outside shows different exposures. Scanners "can have a high false positive error rate", and assessors should set the risk level themselves rather than "simply accept the risk levels assigned by vulnerability scanners."

Penetration testing is "a test methodology in which assessors, typically working under specific constraints, attempt to circumvent or defeat the security features of a system" (SP 800-53). SP 800-53 CA-8 notes it "goes beyond automated vulnerability scanning".

  1. PlanningScope, objectives, written authorization and rules of engagement agreed before testing starts.
  2. DiscoveryReconnaissance, scanning and vulnerability analysis.
  3. AttackGaining access, escalating privileges and moving laterally, within the rules, to show impact.
  4. ReportingFindings, evidence, risk ratings and remediation advice; retest after fixes.

Knowledge levels: black box (no prior information, like an outside attacker), gray box (some, such as user credentials) and white box (full documentation and code, the most thorough per hour). Tests can also be announced or unannounced to the defenders, which tests detection as well as prevention.

No authorization, no test. Written permission from someone with authority over the systems, an agreed scope and rules of engagement come first. Testing a cloud or supplier system needs that owner's permission too; your own contract with them may not cover it.

Logs, synthetic transactions and compliance checks

Objective 6.2Using logs, scripted transactions and automated configuration checks as continuous evidence that controls work.

  • Log reviews: checking that the right events are logged, retained and reviewed, and reviewing them for unauthorized activity (failed and privileged logons, changes, access to sensitive data). Automated correlation in a SIEM does most of the work; people review the alerts and sample the rest. A log nobody reviews is not a control.
  • Synthetic transactions: scripted, artificial transactions run against a system on a schedule (log in, search, add to basket, pay) to check that it works, performs and enforces security, for example that a test account without rights is still refused. Unlike real user monitoring, which observes actual traffic, they work even when no one is using the system.
  • Benchmarks: measuring systems against a reference, either performance baselines or secure configuration benchmarks published by industry bodies and government (hardening guides).
  • Compliance checks: automated comparison of actual configurations against required baselines and policies, such as SCAP-based scanning, cloud posture management, and policy-as-code checks in deployment pipelines. They give continuous evidence for audits.

The retail group runs a synthetic checkout every five minutes in each region, which also tries to read another customer's order and alerts if it succeeds.

Code, misuse case, coverage and interface testing

Objective 6.2Testing software from its source code to its interfaces, including what users should not be able to do.

  • Code review and testing: manual peer review and static analysis (SAST, which analyzes "source, bytecode, or binary code" without running it), dynamic analysis (DAST, testing the running application by simulating attacks), interactive testing (IAST, an agent inside the running application), software composition analysis for third-party components, and fuzzing, which generates huge numbers of malformed inputs to find crashes. NIST's SSDF covers reviewing code (PW.7) and testing executable code (PW.8).
  • Misuse case testing: where use cases describe what a user should do, misuse (abuse) cases describe what an attacker might try, such as changing the price in a request, skipping a payment step or replaying a refund. Tests derived from them check that the system refuses.
  • Coverage analysis: measuring how much of the code or functionality the tests actually exercised (statement, branch or function coverage; NIST IR 8397 gives about 80 percent statement coverage as an example target), and how many requirements and threats have tests. Low coverage means untested paths where flaws hide.
  • Interface testing: testing where components meet. User interfaces for input validation and access control; network interfaces for exposed ports and protocol handling; APIs for authentication, authorization on every object (broken object-level authorization is a leading API flaw), input validation, rate limiting and error handling.

Positive vs negative testing. Functional tests prove the system does what it should. Security testing also needs negative tests (misuse cases, fuzzing, unauthorized API calls) that prove it refuses what it should not do.

Attack simulations

Objective 6.2Exercising people, processes and technology against realistic attacks to test detection and response, not just prevention.

  • Red team exercises: a team emulates a realistic adversary toward an objective, often over weeks and unannounced to defenders (the blue team). SP 800-53 CA-8(2) includes social engineering by "email, telephone, shoulder surfing, or personal conversations", and CA-8(3) physical penetration. CISA's published red team assessments show mature organizations failing to detect the testers and warn that "a 'known-bad' detection approach hampered detection."
  • Purple teaming: red and blue work together, attack by attack, tuning detections as they go.
  • Breach and attack simulation: tools that continuously run safe, automated attack techniques (often mapped to MITRE ATT&CK) to check that controls block and detect them.
  • Phishing simulations and tabletop exercises: testing staff behavior and incident decision-making.
  • AI red teaming: "a structured testing effort, often adopting adversarial methods, to find flaws and vulnerabilities in an AI system" (NIST AI 100-2), such as prompt injection, data leakage and harmful outputs.

Further reading

  • NIST SP 800-115, Technical Guide to Information Security Testing and Assessment (NIST).
  • NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5, Assessing Security and Privacy Controls (NIST, 2022).
  • CISA red team assessment, AA24-193A (CISA, 2024).

Key takeaways

  • Strategy: tests, assessments (SP 800-53A) and audits give different assurance; set scope, methods, frequency, independence and follow-up; validate coverage of top risks. Testers report; management decides.
  • Assessors: internal (knowledge, continuity), external (independence), third-party (supplier attestations such as SOC 2 Type 2). Location: cloud testing follows provider policy; test hybrid links.
  • Vulnerability assessment: credentialed, internal and external scans; verify false positives; set your own risk ratings. Penetration testing: authorization and ROE first; plan, discover, attack, report; black, gray, white box.
  • Logs, synthetic transactions, benchmarks, compliance checks: continuous evidence; synthetic transactions test even without real users.
  • Software: code review, SAST, DAST, IAST, SCA, fuzzing; misuse cases; coverage analysis; UI, network and API interface testing; negative testing.
  • Attack simulations: red, purple, BAS, phishing and tabletop exercises, AI red teaming; they test detection and response.
