1.12: Security awareness, education and training

Building a program that changes how people behave, not just what they have clicked through: the right content for each audience, engaging methods, regular updates as threats change, and honest measurement of whether it works.

Overview

Objective 1.12

1.12 Establish, maintain, and measure a security awareness, education, and training program. Sub-topics: methods and techniques (security champions, gamification); periodic content reviews for emerging technologies and trends (cryptocurrency, AI, blockchain); program effectiveness evaluation and stakeholder engagement.

The 2027 outline adds "measure" and "stakeholder engagement". Culture and awareness basics are in CC 2.3; personnel-specific threats such as MFA fatigue and duress are on the 7.14–7.15 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Store staff have about 15 minutes a month for training; the finance team is a top phishing target.

Building the program

Objective 1.12Establishing a learning program that reaches everyone at the depth their role needs.

Traditional teaching separates three levels: awareness (focusing attention on security so people recognize issues), training (teaching the skills a role needs) and education (deep understanding of why, for specialists). NIST's SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (September 2024), "Building a Cybersecurity and Privacy Learning Program", replaced the 2003 guide and dropped that three-part split because "these terms have different meanings and can lead to confusion"; it describes one learning program with awareness campaigns, awareness training for everyone, practical exercises and role-based training. Both views appear in exam material.

  • Everyone: literacy and awareness training (SP 800-53 AT-2) at onboarding and at least annually, plus ongoing campaigns. CISA's performance goals call for training "prior to accessing computer systems" and at least annually.
  • Role-based training (AT-3): deeper training for people whose roles carry more risk, such as administrators, developers, finance staff who approve payments, executives, and incident responders.
  • Specialists: certification, conferences and formal education for the security team.

A program needs what any program needs: a mandate from leadership, an owner, a budget, a plan by audience and topic, records of who completed what (AT-4), and a review cycle. NIST stresses culture: "The culture of the organization should emphasize, reinforce, and drive its desired behaviors toward cybersecurity and privacy," with leaders who support and take part, and HR practices that reinforce the norms.

Methods and techniques

Objective 1.12Choosing methods that engage people and change behavior.

CISA notes that "once-a-year training isn't enough". Effective programs mix methods:

  • Security champions: volunteers in each team (development, stores, finance) who get extra training, promote good practice locally and bring problems back. OWASP's Security Champions Guide lists ten principles, including a clear vision, management support, a dedicated captain, building a community, sharing knowledge, rewarding responsibility, investing in champions and anticipating personnel changes.
  • Gamification: points, leaderboards, badges and competitions (such as capture-the-flag events for developers or "spot the phish" challenges for staff) that make learning engaging. Reward reporting and learning, not just low click rates.
  • Phishing simulations: realistic test emails measure and build resistance. NIST says such exercises "should not be punitive, nor should any employee be called out for their response"; give immediate, private teaching moments, a simple report button, and legal review of the program.
  • Practical exercises: tabletop exercises for leaders, role-play for help desk and store staff (pretext calls), and cyber ranges for technical teams.
  • Micro-learning and nudges: short videos, posters, logon messages and just-in-time prompts, such as a warning banner on external email.
  • Social engineering content: SP 800-53 AT-2(3) covers phishing, pretexting, impersonation, baiting, quid pro quo and similar techniques; AT-2(2) covers recognizing insider threat indicators.

Make reporting the goal. A culture where people report a suspicious email quickly, even after clicking, protects more than one where they fear punishment and hide mistakes. CISA's phishing guidance asks people to report even after opening suspicious messages.

Keeping content current

Objective 1.12Reviewing training content regularly so it covers the threats and technologies people actually meet.

Content goes stale as attackers change tactics. Review it on a schedule (at least annually) and when triggered by incidents, new technology or new regulation, using incident data, threat intelligence and feedback. Emerging topics the outline names:

  • Artificial intelligence: what may and may not be entered into AI tools (the AI use policy), checking AI output, AI-written phishing that has no spelling mistakes to give it away, and deepfake voice and video used to impersonate executives, which calls for call-back verification of unusual payment or access requests.
  • Cryptocurrency: investment and romance scams and ransom demands. The FBI's IC3 warns crypto transactions "are irrevocable - meaning they cannot be reversed" and to "be wary of cryptocurrency recovery services, especially those charging an up-front fee"; reported losses to IC3 for all internet crime rose from $4.2 billion in 2020 to $16.6 billion in 2024.
  • Blockchain: NIST describes blockchains as "tamper evident and tamper resistant digital ledgers implemented in a distributed fashion"; staff who work with blockchain systems need to understand that the ledger's integrity does not protect the keys, wallets and applications around it.
  • Other trends: QR code phishing, MFA fatigue and push bombing, help desk impersonation, and new collaboration tools.

Measuring effectiveness

Objective 1.12Showing whether the program changes behavior and reduces risk, not just whether people completed it.

Completion rates show reach, not effect. SP 800-50 Rev. 1 suggests measuring behavior and outcomes: phishing click and report rates, the number and speed of incident reports, behavior change such as MFA adoption, test scores before, after and some months later, surveys of attitudes and sentiment, and program maturity from ad hoc to optimized. NIST also cautions that "a poorly designed phishing test might show a better success rate while giving less information about the preparedness of the workforce".

Awareness scorecard (retail group, quarterly)
Reach
Annual training completed: 97% (target 95%); role-based training for finance and admins: 100%
Behavior
Simulation report rate 64% (up from 38%); click rate 6% on comparable-difficulty tests
Speed
Median time from a real phishing email to first report: 4 minutes
Outcomes
Payment fraud attempts stopped by call-back checks: 3 this quarter
Sentiment
83% of staff say they know how to report and would not be blamed for doing so

Use the results: target extra training where rates are worst, retire content that does not change behavior, and report progress to leadership as part of the risk picture (objective 6.3).

Stakeholder engagement

Objective 1.12Involving the people who make a program succeed, from leadership to front-line staff.

  • Leadership: sponsor the program, take the training themselves and talk about security; CSF 2.0 expects leaders to foster a culture "that is risk-aware, ethical, and continually improving" (GV.RR-01).
  • HR: builds training into onboarding, role changes and performance processes; handles consequences fairly (GV.RR-04 covers security in HR practices).
  • Communications and marketing: help make campaigns clear and engaging.
  • Business units: shape content to their real work, provide time for training, and nominate champions.
  • Legal, privacy and works councils: review monitoring and phishing simulations.
  • Learners: give feedback; content that people find irrelevant or patronizing is ignored.

At the retail group, store managers helped rewrite store training as five-minute shift-briefing scripts, which raised completion and reporting more than the previous hour-long online course.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying program design reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the perfect completion rate
Situation
Training completion is 100%, yet a finance clerk paid a fake invoice after a convincing phone call from someone posing as the CFO.
Best response
Add role-based training and a mandatory call-back procedure for payment changes for finance staff, run realistic exercises, and measure behavior rather than completion.
Why
Completion measures reach, not effect; high-risk roles need targeted training and process controls.
Scenario 2: name and shame
Situation
A manager proposes publishing the names of staff who click simulated phishing links to motivate improvement.
Best response
Decline; give private, immediate feedback and extra training, and reward reporting instead.
Why
NIST says phishing exercises should not be punitive or call people out; fear reduces reporting, which is the behavior that limits damage.
Scenario 3: the new AI tools
Situation
Staff have started using public AI chatbots for work, and last year's training never mentions AI.
Best response
Update content now (an out-of-cycle review triggered by new technology) to cover the AI use policy, data that must not be entered, checking output, and AI-enabled scams.
Why
Periodic and event-driven content reviews keep training aligned with emerging technologies, as the outline expects.

Key takeaways

  • Levels: awareness (recognize), training (do a role), education (understand why); SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (2024) merges them into one learning program with campaigns, awareness training, exercises and role-based training.
  • Program: everyone at onboarding and at least annually (AT-2), role-based for high-risk roles (AT-3), records (AT-4), leadership mandate, owner, budget; culture that drives desired behavior.
  • Methods: security champions (OWASP ten principles), gamification, non-punitive phishing simulations, tabletops and role-play, micro-learning and nudges; reward reporting.
  • Content reviews: scheduled and event-driven; AI use and deepfakes, cryptocurrency scams (irreversible payments), blockchain basics, new social engineering techniques.
  • Measure behavior and outcomes (report rates, time to report, fraud stopped, retention tests, sentiment), not just completion; act on the results.
  • Stakeholders: leadership (GV.RR-01), HR (GV.RR-04), communications, business units, legal and works councils, learners' feedback.
