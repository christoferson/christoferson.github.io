Overview

Objective 7.10

7.10 Implement recovery strategies. Sub-topics: backup storage strategies (cloud storage, onsite, offsite, immutable, encrypted); recovery site strategies (cold vs warm vs hot, resource capacity agreements); multiple processing sites; system resilience, high availability (HA), quality of service (QoS), and fault tolerance.

Business impact analysis, RTO and RPO are on the 1.7 page; backup types and site definitions in CC 2.2; facility power on the 3.9 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its BIA set an RTO of 2 hours and an RPO of 5 minutes for card payments, and 24 hours for the loyalty program.

Backup storage strategies

Objective 7.10Storing backups where they survive the disasters and attacks they are meant to recover from.

  • Onsite: fastest restores, but lost with the site in a fire or flood, and reachable by an attacker on the network.
  • Offsite: a separate location far enough away not to share the same disaster (SP 800-53 CP-6 asks for a geographically distinct alternate storage site), chosen for accessibility, security, environment and cost.
  • Cloud storage: offsite by design and scalable; protect the account (separate credentials, MFA), choose regions deliberately, and watch for sync services that replicate deletions or encryption.
  • Immutable: write-once storage that cannot be changed or deleted for a retention period, even by administrators. It is the main defense against ransomware actors, who target backups first.
  • Encrypted: backups are copies of the most sensitive data, so encrypt them (CP-9(8)) and keep the keys separate and recoverable; an encrypted backup without its key is useless.

The #StopRansomware guide asks organizations to "maintain offline, encrypted backups of critical data, and regularly test the availability and integrity of backups in a disaster recovery scenario." A common rule of thumb is three copies, on two different media, one offsite (with one offline or immutable). Backups also need testing (CP-9(1)) and integrity checks before use: NIST's incident guidance says "the integrity of backups and other restoration assets is verified before using them."

Replication and RAID are not backup. High availability and replication copy corruption and deletions to the other side instantly; SP 800-34 warns that HA "cannot be a replacement for a solid backup strategy". You need point-in-time copies that an attacker or a mistake cannot reach.

Recovery sites and multiple processing sites

Objective 7.10Choosing alternate sites by recovery time and cost, and securing the agreements behind them.

  • Cold site: space with power and environmental systems but no computer equipment. Cheapest, slowest (weeks).
  • Warm site: "an environmentally conditioned work space that is partially equipped" (SP 800-34); hardware is present but data must be restored. Days.
  • Hot site: "a fully operational offsite data processing facility equipped with hardware and software"; current data or near-current data. Hours.
  • Mirrored site: fully redundant, with real-time mirroring; "virtually 100 percent availability", the most expensive.
  • Mobile site: transportable, self-contained units delivered after the disaster.
  • Cloud recovery: SP 800-53 CP-7 allows "failover to a cloud-based service provider"; infrastructure as code and replicated data can bring up a full environment on demand, so patterns such as pilot light (core services running, the rest started on demand) and warm standby (a scaled-down copy running) give faster recovery than a cold site at lower cost than a full hot site; restoring data still takes time.

Resource capacity agreements secure the capacity in advance: reciprocal agreements ("an agreement that allows two organizations to back up each other") need similar systems and spare capacity and are hard to enforce; commercial recovery sites may be oversubscribed in a regional disaster; cloud capacity can be reserved. Telecommunications need the same planning (CP-8: separate providers, no single points of failure).

Multiple processing sites run production in two or more locations at once (active-active), or keep one on standby (active-passive), so a site loss is a failover rather than a disaster. The retail group runs card payments active-active across two cloud regions with synchronous replication, and the loyalty program active-passive with hourly snapshots to a second region.

Resilience, high availability, QoS and fault tolerance

Objective 7.10Designing systems that keep working, or degrade gracefully, when components fail or demand spikes.

  • Resilience: the ability to anticipate, withstand, recover from and adapt to adverse conditions (the four goals in NIST SP 800-160 Vol. 2).
  • High availability: redundancy plus automatic failover, "the capability to switch over automatically ... to a redundant or standby system", through clustering, load balancing, and multiple zones or regions.
  • Fault tolerance: "a property of a system that allows proper operation even if components fail" (NISTIR 8202): RAID, dual power supplies, redundant network paths, N+1 components. Fault tolerance aims for no interruption at all; HA accepts a brief failover.
  • Quality of service (QoS): prioritizing critical traffic (payments, voice) over less important traffic, so the service still works under load or during an attack; rate limiting protects back ends.

Avoid hidden single points of failure: one DNS provider, one identity provider, one carrier, a shared storage array behind "redundant" servers, or one person who knows how to recover a system.

Matching strategies to recovery objectives

Objective 7.10Choosing backup frequency, site type and architecture from the RTO and RPO.

The BIA sets how long each process can be down (RTO) and how much data it can lose (RPO); recovery strategies are then chosen to meet them at the lowest reasonable cost:

  • RPO drives data protection: an RPO of 24 hours can be met with nightly backups; an RPO of minutes needs continuous replication or frequent log shipping; an RPO near zero needs synchronous replication to a second site.
  • RTO drives site and architecture: an RTO of weeks fits a cold site or rebuilding from backups; days a warm site; hours a hot site or cloud recovery with infrastructure as code; minutes or less active-active multiple processing sites.
  • Cost rises steeply as RTO and RPO shrink, so not every system gets the fastest option. Systems are grouped into recovery tiers with a standard strategy for each.
  • Dependencies set the real RTO: an application recovered in an hour is unusable if the identity service, DNS or network it needs takes a day.
  • Maximum tolerable downtime (MTD) is the outer limit: RTO plus the time to verify and resume work must stay within it.

At the retail group, card payments (RTO 2 hours, RPO 5 minutes) run active-active across regions; the loyalty program (24 hours) is restored from hourly snapshots in a second region; the internal wiki is rebuilt from nightly backups within a week.

Recovering the foundations

Objective 7.10Planning recovery of the shared services that everything else depends on.

  • Identity: directories and identity providers come first, since nobody can sign in to recover anything else without them; keep offline copies of directory backups and break-glass accounts that do not depend on the main directory.
  • Network and DNS: core routing, firewalls, VPN and DNS configurations backed up and restorable; a secondary DNS provider avoids one point of failure.
  • Keys, certificates and secrets: key management systems and HSM backups, certificate authority keys and secrets vaults must be recoverable, or encrypted backups are useless.
  • Infrastructure code and images: the repositories, pipelines and golden images used to rebuild systems, stored where a disaster or attacker cannot destroy them with production.
  • Documentation: recovery runbooks, network diagrams and contact lists available offline or outside the affected environment.
  • Clean rebuild ability: after a destructive attack, the organization may need to rebuild from trusted media rather than restore possibly infected systems, so trusted installation media and images are part of the strategy.

Some organizations define a "minimum viable company": the smallest set of systems and staff needed to keep critical operations running, recovered first after a major cyberattack.

Resilience patterns in the cloud

Objective 7.10Using cloud building blocks to meet recovery objectives.

  • Multiple availability zones: separate data centers within a region protect against a facility failure with low latency between them.
  • Multiple regions: protect against a regional outage or disaster, at the cost of data replication and complexity.
  • Backups in a separate account: immutable copies outside the production account survive account compromise.
  • Capacity and quotas: check that the recovery region has enough capacity and service limits to run production.
  • Dependency on provider services: identity, DNS and management services can be single points of failure even in multi-region designs.
  • Multi-cloud: rarely needed for whole applications, but sometimes used for critical services such as DNS or backups.

Shared responsibility applies to resilience: the provider runs resilient infrastructure, but the customer must design and test applications to use it.

Documenting the recovery strategy

Objective 7.10Recording strategy decisions so they can be reviewed, funded and tested.

  • For each recovery tier: the RTO and RPO, backup method and frequency, recovery site or region, and the systems in the tier.
  • The cost of each strategy and the risk accepted where the strategy does not fully meet the BIA.
  • Dependencies and the order of recovery.
  • Who approved the strategy and when it was last tested and reviewed.

A written strategy lets leadership see what they are paying for and what risk remains, and gives auditors a baseline to test against.

Backup types and rotation

Objective 7.10What each backup type copies, what a restore needs, and how media are rotated.

  • Full: copies all selected data and clears the archive bit (the file attribute marking changed files). Slowest to run, fastest to restore.
  • Incremental: copies data changed since the last backup of any kind and clears the archive bit. Fastest to run; a restore needs the last full backup plus every incremental since, in order.
  • Differential: copies data changed since the last full backup and does not clear the archive bit, so each differential grows. A restore needs the last full plus only the latest differential.
  • Synthetic full: a full backup built by combining the last full with later incrementals on the backup system, without reading production again.
  • Snapshots: point-in-time copies of volumes or virtual machines, quick to take and restore but usually stored with the original unless replicated or copied elsewhere.
  • Continuous data protection: captures every change, allowing recovery to almost any point in time (very small RPO).

Rotation schemes decide how long each set of media is kept: grandfather-father-son (GFS) keeps daily (son), weekly (father) and monthly (grandfather) backups, each overwritten on its own cycle; the Tower of Hanoi scheme uses media sets at different frequencies to give a longer history with fewer tapes. Media are labeled, tracked and retired after their rated number of uses.

RAID and off-site data methods

Objective 7.10Disk-level fault tolerance and ways of getting data to another site.

  • RAID 0: striping across disks for speed, no redundancy; one failure loses everything.
  • RAID 1: mirroring; each disk has a copy.
  • RAID 5: striping with distributed parity across at least three disks; survives one disk failure.
  • RAID 6: striping with double parity across at least four disks; survives two failures, useful for large disks with long rebuild times.
  • RAID 10 (1+0): mirrored pairs that are striped; fast and survives a failure in each pair.
  • Hot spares take over automatically when a disk fails; RAID protects against disk failure, not against deletion, corruption or ransomware.

Off-site data methods, from slowest to most current:

  • Electronic vaulting: backups transferred in bulk batches to an off-site location.
  • Remote journaling: transaction logs sent to the remote site frequently, so the database can be brought up to date by replaying them.
  • Remote mirroring and database shadowing: changes applied to a live copy at the remote site in real time (mirroring) or to several copies (shadowing), giving the smallest RPO at the highest cost.

Other agreements beside reciprocal ones: mutual assistance agreements (organizations agree to support each other) and service bureaus (contracted processing capacity from a provider).

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: replicated ransomware
Situation
Ransomware encrypts a database; the encryption is replicated instantly to the standby site.
Best response
Restore from point-in-time, immutable or offline backups, after verifying their integrity.
Why
Replication and HA copy corruption; only isolated point-in-time backups protect against it.
Scenario 2: the reciprocal agreement
Situation
Two companies agree to host each other's systems in a disaster; both have grown and neither has spare capacity.
Best response
Replace it with a commercial site, contracted cloud capacity or a second own site, and test it.
Why
Reciprocal agreements are cheap but hard to enforce and often lack capacity.
Scenario 3: an RTO of one hour
Situation
A system has an RTO of one hour and an RPO of 15 minutes, and the current strategy is nightly tape backups to an offsite store.
Best response
Move to replication to a hot site or cloud recovery environment, with frequent snapshots or log shipping, and test failover.
Why
RTO drives site choice and RPO drives backup frequency; nightly tapes meet neither.
Scenario 4: the forgotten dependency
Situation
In a test, the payment application is restored in 40 minutes but nobody can sign in because the identity service is still down.
Best response
Include identity, DNS, network and keys in recovery plans, recover them first, and test end to end.
Why
Dependencies set the real recovery time.
Scenario 5: the recovery region has no capacity
Situation
A failover test shows that the recovery region cannot start enough instances for production.
Best response
Reserve capacity or raise quotas in the recovery region, and include capacity checks in DR tests.
Why
Recovery strategies need capacity, not only data copies.

Key takeaways

  • Backups: onsite (fast), offsite (geographically distinct), cloud (protect the account), immutable (ransomware), encrypted (keys separate); test restores and verify integrity; replication and RAID are not backup.
  • Sites: cold, warm, hot, mirrored, mobile, cloud; reciprocal and commercial agreements have capacity risks; multiple processing sites active-active or active-passive.
  • Resilience: anticipate, withstand, recover, adapt; HA (failover), fault tolerance (no interruption), QoS (priority for critical traffic); find hidden single points of failure.
  • Matching: RPO drives data protection, RTO drives site and architecture, within MTD; recovery tiers; dependencies set the real RTO.
  • Foundations: identity first, network and DNS, keys and secrets, infrastructure code and images, offline runbooks, clean rebuild ability.
