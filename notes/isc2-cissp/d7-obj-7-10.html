7.10–7.13: Recovery, disaster recovery and continuity exercises

Choosing backup and site strategies that meet recovery objectives, building resilient systems, running disaster recovery when it is needed, and proving it all works through tests and exercises.

Overview

Objectives 7.10 to 7.13

7.10 Implement recovery strategies. 7.11 Implement Disaster Recovery (DR) processes. 7.12 Test disaster recovery plans (DRP). 7.13 Participate in Business Continuity (BC) planning exercises.

Business impact analysis, RTO and RPO are on the 1.7–1.8 page; backup types and site definitions in CC 2.2; facility power on the 3.8–3.10 page. This page covers the strategies and running and testing recovery. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its BIA set an RTO of 2 hours and an RPO of 5 minutes for card payments, and 24 hours for the loyalty program.

Backup storage strategies

Objective 7.10Storing backups where they survive the disasters and attacks they are meant to recover from.

  • Onsite: fastest restores, but lost with the site in a fire or flood, and reachable by an attacker on the network.
  • Offsite: a separate location far enough away not to share the same disaster (SP 800-53 CP-6 asks for a geographically distinct alternate storage site), chosen for accessibility, security, environment and cost.
  • Cloud storage: offsite by design and scalable; protect the account (separate credentials, MFA), choose regions deliberately, and watch for sync services that replicate deletions or encryption.
  • Immutable: write-once storage that cannot be changed or deleted for a retention period, even by administrators. It is the main defense against ransomware actors, who target backups first.
  • Encrypted: backups are copies of the most sensitive data, so encrypt them (CP-9(8)) and keep the keys separate and recoverable; an encrypted backup without its key is useless.

The #StopRansomware guide asks organizations to "maintain offline, encrypted backups of critical data, and regularly test the availability and integrity of backups in a disaster recovery scenario." A common rule of thumb is three copies, on two different media, one offsite (with one offline or immutable). Backups also need testing (CP-9(1)) and integrity checks before use: NIST's incident guidance says "the integrity of backups and other restoration assets is verified before using them."

Replication and RAID are not backup. High availability and replication copy corruption and deletions to the other side instantly; SP 800-34 warns that HA "cannot be a replacement for a solid backup strategy". You need point-in-time copies that an attacker or a mistake cannot reach.

Recovery sites and multiple processing sites

Objective 7.10Choosing alternate sites by recovery time and cost, and securing the agreements behind them.

  • Cold site: space with power and environmental systems but no computer equipment. Cheapest, slowest (weeks).
  • Warm site: "an environmentally conditioned work space that is partially equipped" (SP 800-34); hardware is present but data must be restored. Days.
  • Hot site: "a fully operational offsite data processing facility equipped with hardware and software"; current data or near-current data. Hours.
  • Mirrored site: fully redundant, with real-time mirroring; "virtually 100 percent availability", the most expensive.
  • Mobile site: transportable, self-contained units delivered after the disaster.
  • Cloud recovery: SP 800-53 CP-7 allows "failover to a cloud-based service provider"; infrastructure as code and replicated data can bring up a full environment on demand, turning a cold-site cost into a hot-site speed.

Resource capacity agreements secure the capacity in advance: reciprocal agreements ("an agreement that allows two organizations to back up each other") need similar systems and spare capacity and are hard to enforce; commercial recovery sites may be oversubscribed in a regional disaster; cloud capacity can be reserved. Telecommunications need the same planning (CP-8: separate providers, no single points of failure).

Multiple processing sites run production in two or more locations at once (active-active), or keep one on standby (active-passive), so a site loss is a failover rather than a disaster. The retail group runs card payments active-active across two cloud regions with synchronous replication, and the loyalty program active-passive with hourly snapshots to a second region.

Resilience, high availability, QoS and fault tolerance

Objective 7.10Designing systems that keep working, or degrade gracefully, when components fail or demand spikes.

  • Resilience: the ability to anticipate, withstand, recover from and adapt to adverse conditions (the four goals in NIST SP 800-160 Vol. 2).
  • High availability: redundancy plus automatic failover, "the capability to switch over automatically ... to a redundant or standby system", through clustering, load balancing, and multiple zones or regions.
  • Fault tolerance: "a property of a system that allows proper operation even if components fail" (NISTIR 8202): RAID, dual power supplies, redundant network paths, N+1 components. Fault tolerance aims for no interruption at all; HA accepts a brief failover.
  • Quality of service (QoS): prioritizing critical traffic (payments, voice) over less important traffic, so the service still works under load or during an attack; rate limiting protects back ends.

Avoid hidden single points of failure: one DNS provider, one identity provider, one carrier, a shared storage array behind "redundant" servers, or one person who knows how to recover a system.

The disaster recovery process

Objective 7.11Running recovery through clear roles, communications, assessment and restoration, and measuring it.

A disaster recovery plan is "a written plan for recovering one or more information systems at an alternate facility in response to a major hardware or software failure or destruction of facilities" (SP 800-34 Rev. 1). The outline's elements:

  1. ResponseDetect and declare the disaster against defined criteria, activate the plan and the DR team, and protect people first.
  2. PersonnelNamed roles with alternates (no single expert), contact lists kept offline, staff safety and welfare, and the authority to spend and decide.
  3. CommunicationsMethods that do not depend on the failed systems (phone trees, out-of-band messaging, satellite phones), and prioritization: emergency services and staff first, then leadership, then customers, regulators, suppliers and media through approved spokespeople.
  4. AssessmentDetermine the damage and expected outage, then choose to recover in place or fail over to the alternate site.
  5. RestorationRecover systems in BIA priority order to a known state (CP-10), verify data integrity, then later reconstitute and fail back to the primary site in a controlled way.
  6. Training and awarenessEveryone with a role knows it and has practiced it; all staff know how to get information during a disaster.
  7. Lessons learnedReview after every activation and test; update plans, contacts and runbooks.

Metrics show whether recovery capability is good enough:

  • Baseline: normal performance and recovery times measured beforehand, so you know what "recovered" means.
  • Mean time to resolve or recover (MTTR): the average time from failure to restored service; compare it with the RTO.
  • Mean time between failures (MTBF): the average operating time between failures of a component or system; a reliability measure that helps plan redundancy and replacement.
  • Achieved RTO and RPO in tests and real events, and the time since the last successful test of each plan.

Testing DR plans and BC exercises

Objectives 7.12 and 7.13Proving plans work through tests of increasing realism, and taking part in organization-wide continuity exercises.

SP 800-53 CP-4 lists "checklists, walk-through and tabletop exercises, simulations (parallel or full interrupt), and comprehensive exercises". In rising order of realism and disruption:

  1. Read-through (checklist)Each team reviews the plan for accuracy and completeness on its own.
  2. TabletopTeams meet and talk through a scenario and their decisions, without touching systems.
  3. WalkthroughA step-by-step rehearsal of the procedures with the people who would carry them out, sometimes at the recovery site.
  4. SimulationA realistic scenario is played out with actions taken, short of failing over production.
  5. Parallel testSystems are brought up at the alternate site and process real or copied workload, while production keeps running at the primary site.
  6. Full interruptionProduction is actually shut down at the primary site and run from the alternate site. The most realistic and the riskiest; needs senior approval and careful timing.

Communications are tested too: internal call trees, out-of-band channels, and external statements, holding messages and contact with regulators and key customers. Testing should match the system's criticality (NIST suggests tabletop for low-impact systems, functional tests including restores for moderate, full-scale tests including failover for high).

Business continuity exercises (objective 7.13) go beyond IT: business units practice working without systems, relocating staff, and making decisions under pressure. Security professionals take part to contribute security and IT recovery input, to make sure security controls survive in recovery mode (alternate sites and emergency access must still be secure), and to bring the lessons back into security plans. FEMA's HSEEP is a common exercise methodology.

Testing must not cause the disaster. A full interruption test that fails can become a real outage. Work up through less disruptive tests first, have a rollback plan, and get business approval. Questions asking for the "least disruptive" test that still exercises systems usually want parallel testing.

Key takeaways

  • Backups: onsite (fast), offsite (geographically distinct), cloud (protect the account), immutable (ransomware), encrypted (keys separate); test restores and verify integrity; replication and RAID are not backup.
  • Sites: cold, warm, hot, mirrored, mobile, cloud; reciprocal and commercial agreements have capacity risks; multiple processing sites active-active or active-passive.
  • Resilience: anticipate, withstand, recover, adapt; HA (failover), fault tolerance (no interruption), QoS (priority for critical traffic); find hidden single points of failure.
  • DR process: response, personnel, communications (out of band, prioritized), assessment, restoration in BIA order to a known state, training, lessons learned. Metrics: baseline, MTTR vs RTO, MTBF, achieved RTO and RPO.
  • Tests: read-through, tabletop, walkthrough, simulation, parallel, full interruption, in rising realism and risk; test communications; match tests to criticality.
  • BC exercises: business-wide; security keeps controls working in recovery mode and feeds lessons back.
