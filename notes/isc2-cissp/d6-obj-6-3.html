Overview

Objectives 6.3, 6.4 and 6.5

6.3 Collect security process data. 6.4 Analyze test/audit output and generate report. 6.5 Conduct or facilitate security audits.

Measures, metrics, KPIs and KRIs are explained in CC 2.4; the testing toolbox is on the 6.1–6.2 page. This page is about the evidence, what happens to findings, and audits. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its annual PCI DSS assessment is due in three months, and last year's penetration test left 14 findings open.

Collecting security process data

Objective 6.3Gathering technical and administrative evidence that security processes are operating, not just documented.

Process data is technical (system-generated: logs, scan results, configuration reports) or administrative (records of people and procedures: approvals, training records, review sign-offs). The outline's examples:

  • Account management: joiners, movers and leavers processed on time, leavers disabled within the target, orphaned and inactive accounts, privileged account counts, review completion.
  • Management review and approval: evidence that managers actually reviewed and approved access, changes, exceptions and policies (signed-off reviews, change board minutes), which auditors sample.
  • Key performance and risk indicators: a KPI is "a metric of progress toward intended results" and a KRI "a metric used to measure risk" (NIST SP 800-55, 2024). Set targets and tolerances, and use leading KRIs to warn before a tolerance is breached, such as critical vulnerabilities still open after 10 days against a 14-day tolerance (an example from NIST IR 8286C).
  • Backup verification data: job success rates, and more importantly restore tests, with time to restore and integrity checks. A backup never restored is not proven.
  • Training and awareness: completion rates, phishing simulation results and reporting rates, role-based training for privileged staff. NIST warns that "a poorly designed phishing test might show a better success rate while giving less information".
  • Disaster recovery and business continuity: plan reviews, exercise results, achieved recovery times against RTO and RPO, and open actions from exercises.

Good process data is collected automatically where possible, accurate, consistent over time and tied to a target, so trends can be seen. "Less is often more": a few meaningful measures beat dozens of counts.

Analyzing output and reporting

Objective 6.4Turning raw test and audit results into validated, prioritized findings and reports each audience can act on.

  1. ValidateRemove false positives and duplicates; confirm each finding with evidence.
  2. RateAssess risk in context: severity scores (such as CVSS), plus exploitability (is it on CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list?), exposure and the asset's value.
  3. Find root causesGroup findings by cause (a missing patch process, a weak build image) so the fix addresses the cause, not each symptom.
  4. ReportAn executive summary of risk and trends for leadership; detailed findings, evidence and remediation steps for technical owners; the scope, method and limitations so readers know what was not tested.
  5. TrackEach finding gets an owner, a due date by risk, and retesting to close it.

Test reports are sensitive: they are a map of the organization's weaknesses. Classify them, limit distribution, and protect them in transit and storage.

Remediation and exception handling

Objective 6.4Fixing findings by risk-based deadlines, and handling those that cannot be fixed through documented, time-limited exceptions.

Remediation follows the risk: critical and actively exploited issues within days, others within set service levels. Fixes are verified by retesting, and closed only with evidence. In the federal model, open weaknesses and their planned fixes are tracked in a plan of action and milestones (POA&M, SP 800-53 CA-5).

Exception handling covers findings that cannot be fixed on time or at all (a legacy system, a vendor dependency, a business need):

  • A written request stating the reason, the risk and any compensating controls.
  • Approval by the risk owner at the right level of authority, not by the team that wants the exception.
  • An expiry date and a review before renewal; exceptions without end dates become permanent risk.
  • A central register so the total accepted risk is visible.

Exceptions are risk acceptance. An exception is a formal decision to accept a risk, so it must be made by someone with authority to accept it, informed by the facts, and recorded. Silently leaving a finding open is not an exception; it is unmanaged risk.

Responsible disclosure

Objective 6.4Receiving and handling vulnerability reports from outsiders, and disclosing vulnerabilities you find in others' products responsibly.

Responsible (or coordinated) disclosure means a vulnerability is reported privately to the vendor or owner, who fixes it within a reasonable time before details are made public, so users can patch before attackers learn of it.

  • Vulnerability disclosure policy (VDP): tells researchers how to report, what is in scope, what testing is allowed, and that good-faith research will not lead to legal action. CISA's BOD 20-01 required US federal agencies to publish one.
  • Contact point: a security.txt file (RFC 9116) on the website tells researchers where to send reports.
  • Process: ISO/IEC 29147 covers receiving reports and publishing advisories; ISO/IEC 30111 covers handling them internally (triage, verification, fixing). Acknowledge quickly, keep the reporter informed, agree a disclosure date, credit them, and publish an advisory with a CVE identifier.
  • Bug bounties: a VDP plus payment for valid findings, run directly or through a platform.

The other direction matters too: when your testers find a flaw in a vendor's product, report it to the vendor (or a coordinator such as CISA or a CERT) under the same principles rather than publishing it.

Conducting and facilitating audits

Objective 6.5Running internal audits and supporting external and third-party ones, on premises and in the cloud.

  1. PlanAgree the objective, standard (criteria), scope, period and auditors; confirm independence.
  2. Gather evidenceDocuments, interviews, observation and testing; auditors sample populations (for example 25 leavers from the year) rather than check everything.
  3. EvaluateCompare evidence with the criteria and identify gaps.
  4. ReportFindings, risk, management responses and an opinion where required.
  5. Follow upTrack agreed actions to completion and verify them in the next audit.
  • Internal audits (within the organization's control): planned by internal audit by risk, reporting to the audit committee; security teams prepare evidence and respond to findings.
  • External audits (outside the organization's control): independent auditors for certifications and regulatory requirements (ISO/IEC 27001 certification, PCI DSS assessments, financial audits). Facilitating them means a single coordinator, evidence ready and organized, the right people available, and accurate answers; never hide or alter evidence.
  • Third-party audits (outside the enterprise's control): customers or regulators auditing you under contract or law, and you auditing suppliers through the right-to-audit clause, questionnaires or their attestation reports.
  • Location: on premises, auditors can inspect everything; in the cloud, they rely on the provider's attestations and certifications for its part and examine your configurations and logs for yours; in hybrid environments, scope must include the connections and identity links between the two.

Continuous control monitoring and compliance checks (objective 6.2) make audits easier: evidence is collected all year rather than in a rush before the auditors arrive.

Further reading

  • NIST SP 800-55 Vol. 1, Measurement Guide for Information Security (NIST, 2024).
  • Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog (CISA): an input to remediation priorities.
  • RFC 9116, A File Format to Aid in Security Vulnerability Disclosure (IETF).

Key takeaways

  • Process data: technical and administrative evidence on account management, management reviews, KPIs and KRIs (SP 800-55), backup restores, training, DR and BC exercises; automated, accurate, tied to targets.
  • Analysis and reports: validate, rate in context (KEV, exposure, value), find root causes, report by audience with scope and limits, track to closure; protect the reports.
  • Remediation by risk-based deadlines, verified by retest; POA&Ms (CA-5). Exceptions: written, compensating controls, approved by the risk owner, expiry date, central register.
  • Responsible disclosure: private report, fix, then publish; VDP with safe harbor (BOD 20-01), security.txt (RFC 9116), ISO/IEC 29147 and 30111, CVEs, bug bounties.
  • Audits: plan, gather evidence (sampling), evaluate, report, follow up; internal, external and third-party; cloud audits rely on provider attestations plus your own configurations.
