Overview

Objective 6.3

6.3 Collect security process data (e.g., technical, administrative). Sub-topics: account management; management review and approval; key performance and risk indicators; backup verification data; training and awareness; disaster recovery and business continuity.

Measures, metrics, KPIs and KRIs are explained in CC 2.4; analysis and reporting are on the 6.4 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its annual PCI DSS assessment is due in three months, and last year's penetration test left 14 findings open.

Collecting security process data

Objective 6.3Gathering technical and administrative evidence that security processes are operating, not just documented.

Process data is technical (system-generated: logs, scan results, configuration reports) or administrative (records of people and procedures: approvals, training records, review sign-offs). The outline's examples:

  • Account management: joiners, movers and leavers processed on time, leavers disabled within the target, orphaned and inactive accounts, privileged account counts, review completion.
  • Management review and approval: evidence that managers actually reviewed and approved access, changes, exceptions and policies (signed-off reviews, change board minutes), which auditors sample.
  • Key performance and risk indicators: a KPI is "a metric of progress toward intended results" and a KRI "a metric used to measure risk" (NIST SP 800-55, 2024). Set targets and tolerances, and use leading KRIs to warn before a tolerance is breached, such as critical vulnerabilities still open after 10 days against a 14-day tolerance (an example from NIST IR 8286C).
  • Backup verification data: job success rates, and more importantly restore tests, with time to restore and integrity checks. A backup never restored is not proven.
  • Training and awareness: completion rates, phishing simulation results and reporting rates, role-based training for privileged staff. NIST warns that "a poorly designed phishing test might show a better success rate while giving less information".
  • Disaster recovery and business continuity: plan reviews, exercise results, achieved recovery times against RTO and RPO, and open actions from exercises.

Good process data is collected automatically where possible, accurate, consistent over time and tied to a target, so trends can be seen. "Less is often more": a few meaningful measures beat dozens of counts.

Collecting and managing process data

Objective 6.3Gathering evidence that is reliable enough to base decisions and audits on.

  • Sources: identity and access systems, HR, ticketing and change systems, training platforms, backup tools, vulnerability scanners, SIEM, continuity exercise records and management sign-offs.
  • Automation: pull data directly from systems into a GRC or reporting platform on a schedule, instead of screenshots and spreadsheets assembled before audits.
  • Quality: accurate, complete for the population (all leavers, not a sample someone chose), time-stamped, consistent definitions, and protected from tampering.
  • Ownership: each data set has an owner responsible for its correctness, and definitions of each measure are written down (what counts as "remediated", "on time", "privileged").
  • Retention: keep evidence long enough for audit periods and trend analysis, under the retention schedule.

Administrative data often lives in email and documents; moving approvals and reviews into tracked workflows makes them measurable and auditable.

Using process data to improve

Objective 6.3Turning measurements into trends, decisions and actions.

Data has value only if it changes decisions. Good use of process data means:

  • Trends over snapshots: is time-to-deprovision improving quarter by quarter, and is patch latency rising after a reorganization?
  • Targets and tolerances: each KPI and KRI has a target and a threshold that triggers action, tied to risk appetite.
  • Audience: operational detail for teams, a small set of indicators for leadership, in business terms.
  • Root causes: repeated misses lead to process fixes (automation, ownership, training), not just reminders.
  • Honesty: measures that are easy to game (completion rates) are paired with outcome measures (behavior, incidents).

At the retail group, quarterly data showed restores were tested for only 60% of tier 1 systems; the steering committee made restore testing a target for each system owner, and coverage reached 95% within two quarters.

Account management and access data

Objective 6.3Measuring whether identities and access are being managed as the policy says.

Account management produces some of the most useful, and most audited, process data. Typical measures:

  • Time to deprovision: hours or days between a termination in HR and the removal of all access, with the share completed within the policy deadline.
  • Orphaned and dormant accounts: accounts with no current owner or no sign-in for a set period (for example 90 days).
  • Privileged accounts: number of standing administrator accounts, share protected by PAM, and use of just-in-time elevation.
  • Access certification: completion rate of reviews by deadline, and the share of access removed as a result (a review that never removes anything may be a rubber stamp).
  • Separation of duties: detected conflicts and how long they stay open.
  • Shared and service accounts: count, owners, and age of their credentials.

The evidence comes from comparing sources: the HR list of leavers against directory and application account lists, PAM logs against administrator group membership, and request tickets against actual changes. Mismatches are findings in their own right: an access change with no ticket means someone bypassed the process.

At the retail group, comparing the HR leaver list against the point-of-sale system found 38 active till accounts belonging to people who had left; the store access process was then linked to HR so till accounts close automatically.

Continuity and recovery evidence

Objective 6.3Collecting proof that the organization can actually recover.

  • Exercise records: tabletop, walkthrough, simulation, parallel and full interruption tests, with dates, participants, scenarios and results (Domain 7).
  • Achieved versus target: the recovery time and recovery point actually achieved in each test compared with the RTO and RPO from the BIA.
  • Plan currency: date each plan was last reviewed, and whether contact lists, dependencies and procedures were updated after changes.
  • After-action reports: issues found in exercises and real incidents, with owners and closure dates.
  • Dependency coverage: whether tests included suppliers, cloud services, identity systems and communication channels, not just the primary application.
  • Backup data: job success, coverage of in-scope systems, immutable or offline copies, and restore tests with integrity checks.

The strongest evidence is a timed restore or failover of a real system by the people who would do it in a crisis, using the written plan. A plan that has only been reviewed on paper gives low assurance.

Building a measurement program

Objective 6.3Choosing a small set of meaningful measures and running them consistently.

NIST SP 800-55 (2024, in two volumes) covers selecting measures and running an information security measurement program. Practical steps:

  1. Start from goalsPick measures that show progress on security objectives and top risks, not whatever tools happen to report.
  2. Define each measureName, formula, data source, frequency, owner, target and threshold, so it means the same thing every time.
  3. Prefer quantitative dataCounts, times and percentages from systems are more repeatable than opinions, though qualitative ratings still have a place in risk discussions.
  4. Collect and reportAutomate collection, store history, and report at the right level for each audience.
  5. Review the measuresRetire ones that no longer drive decisions and add ones for new risks.

Measures fall into a few practical kinds: implementation (is the control deployed, such as MFA coverage), effectiveness (does it work, such as phishing simulation report rate), efficiency (time and cost, such as mean time to remediate) and impact (business results, such as losses avoided or downtime). A balanced set uses all of them.

Management review and approval evidence

Objective 6.3Showing that leaders actually review security and make decisions.

ISO/IEC 27001 requires top management to review the ISMS at planned intervals, covering the status of previous actions, changes in issues and requirements, performance and audit results, nonconformities, risk assessment results and improvement opportunities. Similar reviews exist in most frameworks. Evidence includes:

  • Minutes and decisions of security steering committee and board risk committee meetings, with actions and owners.
  • Approvals: signed policies, approved risk acceptances, exception approvals, and access approvals recorded in workflows.
  • Sign-offs: system authorizations, change approvals and release sign-offs.
  • Follow-up: evidence that actions from previous reviews were completed.

Auditors look for decisions, not just meetings: a review that receives reports but never assigns actions or accepts risks gives weak evidence of governance.

Training and awareness data

Objective 6.3Measuring whether training changes behavior, not only whether it was completed.

  • Completion: the share of staff and of each role who completed required training on time, including new starters.
  • Behavior: phishing simulation click and report rates, time to report, and real phishing reports from staff.
  • Role-based training: completion of specialist training for developers, administrators and incident responders.
  • Incidents: incidents caused by human error and their trend after targeted training.

Report rate is often more useful than click rate: a rising share of staff reporting suspicious messages shows the human sensor network is working.

Sharing process data with auditors

Objective 6.3Giving auditors evidence they can rely on, quickly.

  • Provide system-generated reports with the query or filter used, so auditors can see the population is complete.
  • Give auditors read-only access to dashboards or the GRC platform where appropriate, instead of exporting spreadsheets.
  • Keep evidence for the whole audit period, with dates, not only the latest snapshot.
  • Explain gaps openly, with the remediation plan, rather than letting auditors discover them.

Well-organized process data turns an audit from weeks of evidence gathering into a review of controls already being measured.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the screenshot audit
Situation
Before each audit, staff spend two weeks collecting screenshots of access reviews and backup jobs.
Best response
Automate evidence collection from the systems into a GRC platform, with defined measures and owners.
Why
Automated, continuous evidence is more reliable and cheaper than assembling it before audits.
Scenario 2: the green dashboard
Situation
All backup jobs show "success", but a ransomware test restore failed.
Best response
Measure restore tests (time to restore, integrity checks) as well as job success, and test regularly.
Why
Backup verification data must prove recoverability, not just that jobs ran.
Scenario 3: training at 100%
Situation
Training completion is 100%, yet phishing simulation report rates are falling.
Best response
Report behavior and outcome measures alongside completion, and investigate why reporting is falling (process, culture, tooling).
Why
Completion shows reach, not effect; pair easy-to-game measures with outcome measures.
Scenario 4: who approved it
Situation
Auditors ask for evidence that managers approved access changes; approvals were given by chat messages.
Best response
Move approvals into the access request workflow so they are recorded and auditable; for the past period, collect what evidence exists and disclose gaps.
Why
Management review and approval must leave evidence.
Scenario 5: the steering committee
Situation
The security steering committee receives monthly reports but the minutes show no decisions or actions for a year.
Best response
Restructure the agenda around decisions (risk acceptances, priorities, budget) and record actions with owners and follow-up.
Why
Management review evidence must show decisions and follow-through.

Further reading

  • NIST SP 800-55 Vol. 1, Measurement Guide for Information Security (NIST, 2024).
  • NIST IR 8286C Rev. 1, Staging Cybersecurity Risks for Enterprise Risk Management (NIST, 2025).

Key takeaways

  • Process data: technical and administrative evidence on account management, management reviews, KPIs and KRIs (SP 800-55), backup restores, training, DR and BC exercises.
  • Collection: automated from source systems, complete populations, defined measures with owners, protected and retained.
  • Use: trends, targets and tolerances, audience-appropriate reporting, root-cause fixes, outcome measures alongside activity measures.
