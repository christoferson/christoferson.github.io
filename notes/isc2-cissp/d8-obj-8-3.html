Overview

Objective 8.3

8.3 Assess the effectiveness of software security. Sub-topics: auditing and logging of changes; risk analysis and mitigation.

The development process is on the 8.1 page and the pipeline on the 8.2 page; code testing on the 6.2 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its application security team tracks findings from pipeline scans, penetration tests and a bug bounty program for the e-commerce platform.

Assessing software security effectiveness

Objective 8.3Checking that software security controls work, through change records and ongoing risk analysis.

  • Auditing and logging of changes: every change to code, configuration and deployed software should be traceable: who changed what, when, why, who reviewed and approved it, and which build was deployed. Repository history, pull request reviews, pipeline logs and deployment records together give that trail. Auditors sample changes to confirm that reviews and approvals actually happened and that nobody bypassed the pipeline to change production directly.
  • Risk analysis and mitigation: reassess software risk when the threat landscape, the code or its use changes, using threat models, test and scan results, vulnerability reports and incidents. Mitigate by fixing code, adding compensating controls (WAF rules, feature flags to disable a function) or accepting residual risk formally.
  • Measures of effectiveness: vulnerability density and trends, time to fix by severity, the share of findings caught before production, recurring weakness types (a sign that training or a framework is missing), and coverage of testing and threat modeling.

Auditing changes in practice

Objective 8.3Making the change trail complete, protected and actually checked.

  • What to record: commits with verified authors, pull request reviews and approvals, pipeline runs with their inputs and results, artifact versions and signatures, deployments with who triggered them and where, and configuration changes in production.
  • Link the records: a deployed build should trace back to its commits, reviews, test results and change ticket, and forward to the systems it runs on.
  • Protect the logs: send repository, pipeline and deployment logs to the central log platform, out of reach of the developers and administrators whose actions they record.
  • Detect bypasses: alert on direct pushes to protected branches, disabled branch protection, pipeline definition changes, deployments outside the pipeline and changes made directly in production consoles.
  • Review: auditors and the security team sample changes to confirm reviews and approvals happened; automated checks compare what runs in production with what the pipeline built.

Change auditing also supports incident response: when a vulnerability or backdoor is found, the trail shows when it was introduced, by whom, and which releases contain it.

Software risk analysis

Objective 8.3Keeping a current view of the risk each application carries.

  • Application inventory: every application and API with its owner, data classification, exposure (internet-facing or internal), technology stack and business criticality.
  • Risk rating: applications are tiered by risk, which sets how often they are threat modeled, tested and reviewed.
  • Threat models kept current: updated when architecture, data flows or threats change, not filed after the first design review.
  • Evidence from many sources: pipeline scan results, penetration tests, bug bounty and VDP reports, incidents, production monitoring and threat intelligence about the frameworks used.
  • Risk register entries: significant application risks are recorded with owners and treatment decisions, alongside other enterprise risks (objective 1.9).

The retail group rates its 180 applications in three tiers. Tier 1 (payments, e-commerce, identity) is threat modeled for every major change and penetration tested twice a year; tier 3 internal tools rely on pipeline scanning and a yearly review.

Mitigation options

Objective 8.3Choosing how to treat a software risk when a weakness is found.

  • Fix the code: the preferred option, with a test added so the flaw does not return.
  • Upgrade or replace a component: for vulnerable libraries or frameworks.
  • Compensating controls: a WAF rule or API gateway policy (virtual patching), tighter access, extra monitoring, or input filtering at the edge while the fix is built.
  • Feature flags and kill switches: turn off a vulnerable feature without a full release.
  • Retire: remove unused features, endpoints and old API versions entirely.
  • Accept: a documented, time-limited risk acceptance by the risk owner when the risk is low or the cost of fixing is out of proportion.

Whatever the choice, the fix is verified (retest or rescan), and the root cause is examined: a class of flaw that keeps appearing calls for a framework change, a new pipeline rule or training, not only individual fixes.

Independent reviews and reporting

Objective 8.3Getting an outside view of whether the software security program works.

  • Penetration tests and code audits by people outside the development team, focused on high-risk applications and major changes.
  • Bug bounty and VDP data: the number and severity of external reports show what internal testing misses (objective 6.4).
  • Maturity reassessment: repeating the SAMM or similar assessment yearly shows whether practices improved (objective 8.1).
  • Architecture reviews: periodic reviews of critical systems against current threats and standards.
  • Reporting: leadership sees risk by application tier, trends in findings and time to fix, open exceptions, and progress against maturity targets, in business terms.

Comparing sources is revealing: if external researchers keep finding authorization flaws that scanners never report, the testing program needs manual authorization tests, and developers need better patterns for access checks.

Measuring software security

Objective 8.3Choosing measures that show whether software is getting more secure.

  • Escape rate: the share of vulnerabilities found after release (by testers, researchers or attackers) rather than in the pipeline; falling escape rates show that shift left is working.
  • Time to remediate by severity and application tier, compared with policy deadlines.
  • Open risk: count and age of open findings and exceptions, especially in tier 1 applications.
  • Weakness trends: the most common CWE types over time, which point to training and framework gaps.
  • Coverage: the share of applications with threat models, pipeline scanning, SBOMs and recent penetration tests.
  • Dependency health: the share of components with known exploitable vulnerabilities, and how far behind current versions they are.

Raw finding counts are misleading on their own: more findings may mean better testing rather than worse code. Pair counts with severity, exposure and trends, and report them per application owner so that accountability is clear.

Software security in production

Objective 8.3Watching how software behaves once it is live, where real attacks happen.

  • Application logging: security-relevant events (sign-ins, authorization failures, input validation failures, admin actions, high-value transactions) logged with enough context and sent to the SIEM.
  • Runtime protection: WAFs and API gateways at the edge, and runtime application self-protection where used, block and report attacks.
  • Anomaly detection: unusual patterns such as many failed authorization checks from one account or bulk data downloads through an API.
  • Feedback to developers: attacks seen in production feed threat models, test cases and backlog items.

Production evidence is the most direct measure of effectiveness: if attackers repeatedly probe a function and fail, controls are working; if fraud teams find abuse that no security tool flagged, the software is missing detection or business logic checks.

Threat models as an effectiveness check

Objective 8.3Using threat models to see whether controls cover the threats that matter.

  • For each threat in the model, record the control that addresses it and the test or evidence that shows the control works.
  • Threats with no control, or controls with no evidence, are gaps to fix or accept.
  • Compare findings from testing and incidents against the model: a finding the model did not anticipate means the model needs updating.
  • Review models for high-risk applications at least yearly and after major changes.

Linking threats, controls and evidence turns the threat model from a design document into a living measure of how well an application is protected.

Secure by design commitments

Objective 8.3Measuring a software producer against public secure-by-design goals.

CISA's Secure by Design pledge asks software manufacturers to show measurable progress on goals such as increasing MFA use, eliminating default passwords, reducing entire classes of vulnerability, increasing customers' installation of patches, publishing a vulnerability disclosure policy, issuing CVEs accurately, and giving customers better evidence of intrusions. Organizations that build software can use the same goals as effectiveness measures, and buyers can ask suppliers to report on them.

Release security reviews

Objective 8.3Deciding, release by release, whether residual risk is acceptable.

  • Check that required activities happened: threat model updated, scans passed or exceptions approved, tests for security requirements passed.
  • List open findings by severity with owners and dates.
  • Confirm that SBOM, signing and release notes are produced.
  • Record the release decision and who accepted any residual risk.

For frequent releases these checks are automated as pipeline gates, with human review reserved for high-risk changes or exceptions.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the bypassed pipeline
Situation
An engineer fixed a production bug by editing code directly on the server, outside the pipeline.
Best response
Record it as an emergency change, bring the fix into the repository through review, redeploy through the pipeline, and alert on direct production changes in future.
Why
All changes must be traceable; bypasses break the audit trail and may hide malicious changes.
Scenario 2: the stale threat model
Situation
A payment service added a new partner integration, but its threat model dates from three years ago.
Best response
Update the threat model for the new data flows and test the integration before release.
Why
Risk analysis must be kept current as software changes.
Scenario 3: no fix yet
Situation
A critical injection flaw is found in the online store, and the fix needs two weeks of work.
Best response
Apply compensating controls now (WAF rule, disable the feature with a flag), fix the code, verify, and remove the temporary control afterwards.
Why
Mitigation can reduce risk while the permanent fix is developed.
Scenario 4: the same flaw again
Situation
Penetration tests find cross-site scripting in a different application every quarter.
Best response
Address the root cause: standardize on auto-escaping templates, add SAST rules, and train developers.
Why
Recurring weakness types show that the process, not just the code, needs fixing.
Scenario 5: the uncovered threat
Situation
A threat model lists account takeover as a top threat, but no control or test addresses it for the mobile app.
Best response
Add controls (MFA, rate limits, anomaly detection), tests that verify them, and track the gap until closed.
Why
Mapping threats to controls and evidence shows where security is not effective.

Key takeaways

  • Effectiveness: audit trails of every change (who, what, why, reviewed, deployed); ongoing risk analysis and mitigation; measures such as time to fix and findings caught before production.
  • Change auditing: complete linked records from commit to deployment, protected logs, alerts on bypasses, sampling by auditors.
  • Risk analysis: application inventory and tiers, current threat models, evidence from scans, tests, bounties and incidents, risk register entries.
  • Mitigation: fix, upgrade, compensating controls, feature flags, retire, or time-limited acceptance; verify and address root causes.
  • Independent review: penetration tests and audits, external reports, maturity reassessment, architecture reviews, business-level reporting.
