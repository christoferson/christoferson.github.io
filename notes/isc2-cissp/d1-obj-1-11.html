1.11–1.12: Supply chain risk and awareness programs

Two ways risk enters through trusted channels: the products and services an organization buys, which can be tampered with, faked or compromised upstream, and its own people, who need a program that keeps their knowledge current and is measured by what they actually do.

Overview

Objectives 1.11 and 1.12

1.11 Apply Supply Chain Risk Management (SCRM) concepts. 1.12 Establish, maintain, and measure a security awareness, education, and training program.

Supplier contracts and SLAs are on the 1.7–1.8 page; the basics of awareness programs, social engineering and phishing tests are in CC 2.3. This page covers the supply chain threats and technical mitigations named in the outline, and running an awareness program at CISSP level. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It buys payment terminals, servers and network gear from several manufacturers, and its developers use hundreds of open source libraries.

Supply chain risks

Objective 1.11Recognizing how products and services can be compromised before they reach the organization, and the harm that follows.

Supply chain risk management (NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1) covers "identifying, assessing, and mitigating cybersecurity risks throughout the supply chain." The outline names four kinds of risk from what an organization acquires:

  • Tampering: a product is altered in manufacture, transit or repair, for example malicious firmware loaded onto a device or a software update modified at the source.
  • Counterfeits: fake or recycled components sold as genuine, which may be unreliable or deliberately weakened.
  • Implants: hidden hardware or code added to provide covert access. Few are publicly confirmed, but the possibility drives inspection and trusted-supplier programs.
  • Reputational: a supplier's breach or misconduct becomes the customer's headline, and customers blame the brand they dealt with.

Software supply chain attacks show how one compromised supplier reaches thousands of customers. CISA's records include SolarWinds (2020), where backdoored versions of the Orion management software reached government networks and an emergency directive ordered agencies to disconnect it; 3CX (2023), where a widely used conferencing app "was trojanized"; and XZ Utils (2024, CVE-2024-3094), where malicious code was hidden in two releases of an open source compression library used by Linux systems.

Assessing and controlling suppliers

Objective 1.11Reducing supply chain risk through supplier assessment and monitoring, minimum requirements, service levels and checks on what is delivered.

  • Third-party assessment and monitoring: tier suppliers by criticality, assess them before contract (questionnaires, certifications, SOC 2 reports, on-site reviews) and monitor them during the relationship (CSF GV.SC-07).
  • Minimum security requirements written into contracts, and passed down to subcontractors (SP 800-53 SR-3(3)).
  • Service level requirements: availability, support, patch delivery times and incident notification (objective 1.8).
  • Buying from authorized sources and an anti-counterfeit program: SR-11 requires policies "to detect and prevent counterfeit components from entering the system" and reporting of counterfeits found.
  • Tamper protection and inspection: SR-9 asks for "a tamper protection program", and SR-10 for inspecting systems or components "to detect tampering", especially after they leave controlled areas (for example a laptop returned from travel or repair).

The retail group buys payment terminals only from approved vendors and distributors, checks tamper-evident seals and serial numbers on delivery, and has store staff inspect terminals daily for added overlays or wires.

Hardware roots of trust and PUFs

Objective 1.11Using trusted hardware to prove a device is genuine and its firmware has not been changed.

NIST defines roots of trust as "highly reliable hardware, firmware, and software components that perform specific, critical security functions", and a hardware root of trust as "an inherently trusted combination of hardware and firmware that maintains the integrity of information." Because everything above it is checked against it, the root of trust must be protected in silicon. Uses include:

  • Secure and measured boot: each stage checks the next before running it, so tampered firmware is detected.
  • Firmware resiliency: NIST SP 800-193 sets three goals: protecting firmware against unauthorized changes, detecting changes that occur, and recovering "rapidly and securely".
  • Attestation: a device proves its identity and measured state to a remote verifier before it is trusted on the network.

Common implementations are the Trusted Platform Module (TPM), which the Trusted Computing Group describes as a "hardware-based root of trust that securely generates and stores cryptographic keys", and open designs such as Caliptra, an open-source root-of-trust block for data center chips whose version 2.1 adds post-quantum algorithms.

A physically unclonable function (PUF) uses tiny, uncontrollable variations from manufacturing to give each chip a unique, repeatable response to a challenge. A device key can be derived from that response each time it is needed instead of being stored, so it is very hard to extract or copy, and a counterfeit chip cannot reproduce it. NIST's glossary has no entry for PUFs; treat this as the general concept.

Software bills of materials

Objective 1.11Using an SBOM to know what is inside software, and to answer quickly whether a new vulnerability affects it.

A software bill of materials (SBOM) is a machine-readable list of the components and dependencies in a piece of software. CISA, NSA, FBI and international partners published the "2026 Minimum Elements for a Software Bill of Materials" in July 2026, replacing the 2021 US Commerce Department list; it adds fields such as component hashes, licenses, the tool used to generate the SBOM and the author's signature, and notes that AI systems and SaaS "may require additional elements". A G7 guide for AI SBOMs followed in May 2026. A simplified entry in a common format:

One SBOM component (simplified, CycloneDX-style JSON) 
{
  "type": "library",
  "name": "zlib",
  "version": "1.3.1",
  "purl": "pkg:generic/zlib@1.3.1",
  "licenses": [{ "license": { "id": "Zlib" } }],
  "hashes": [{ "alg": "SHA-256", "content": "9f2c...e71a" }]
}

When the XZ Utils backdoor was announced, organizations with SBOMs could search them in minutes for versions 5.6.0 and 5.6.1. A VEX statement is "a form of a security advisory that indicates whether a product or products are affected by a known vulnerability", which saves customers from chasing components that are present but not exploitable.

Federal SBOM rules changed in 2026. OMB memo M-26-05 (January 2026) rescinded the earlier memos that required software suppliers to sign a secure development attestation for federal agencies. Agencies now take a risk-based approach and "may" ask for attestations or SBOMs in contracts, so SBOMs are a buyer's choice rather than a blanket federal mandate.

Building the awareness program

Objective 1.12Designing a program that reaches everyone at the right depth, using methods such as security champions and gamification.

Programs are traditionally described in three levels: awareness (everyone recognizes threats and knows what to do), training (role-based skills, such as secure coding for developers or fraud checks for finance) and education (deeper understanding for security staff, often through degrees and certifications). NIST's current guide (SP 800-50 Rev. 1, 2024) replaced that continuum with a single cybersecurity and privacy learning program, but the three levels remain common in exam material (CC 2.3).

Methods that change behavior, not just tick a box:

  • Security champions: staff in each team, often developers, who take on a security role and act as the link to the security team. OWASP's Security Champions Guide sets out ten principles, including securing management support, nominating a dedicated captain, creating a community, rewarding responsibility and investing in the champions.
  • Gamification: points, leaderboards, capture-the-flag events and team challenges that make practice engaging. It works best when it rewards reporting and good behavior rather than shaming mistakes.
  • Simulations and exercises: phishing simulations, tabletop exercises and role-play, run without punishing individuals.
  • Short, frequent content in the flow of work, rather than one long annual course.

The retail group has one security champion in every development squad and every regional store team; champions meet monthly, get advanced training and are recognized in performance reviews.

Keeping content current

Objective 1.12Reviewing content on a schedule so it covers new technologies, new attacks and new ways staff work.

Content goes stale as fast as threats change, so programs review it periodically and after major events. Current topics:

  • AI: what may and may not be entered into AI tools, AI-written phishing with perfect grammar, voice clones and deepfake video calls (CC 2.3).
  • Cryptocurrency: crypto payments are attractive to criminals because they "are irrevocable - meaning they cannot be reversed" (FBI IC3), and staff and customers should be warned about investment scams and fake "recovery services, especially those charging an up-front fee."
  • Blockchain: NIST describes blockchains as "tamper evident and tamper resistant digital ledgers implemented in a distributed fashion" (IR 8202); staff working with blockchain projects need to know that the ledger's integrity does not protect lost or stolen keys.

The retail group's content owner reviews the program every quarter against new threat intelligence and incidents, and updates the AI module whenever the approved tools list changes.

Measuring effectiveness

Objective 1.12Proving the program changes behavior, and keeping leaders and other stakeholders involved.

Completion rates show reach, not effect. Better measures (CC 2.3, CC 2.4) are behavioral: phishing report rates and time to report, click rates adjusted for test difficulty, incidents reported by staff, policy exceptions, and results from audits and tabletop exercises. SP 800-50 Rev. 1 suggests a senior leadership committee that meets regularly with the program's managers, and stakeholders such as HR, legal, communications and business unit heads help set priorities and deliver messages.

Key takeaways

  • Supply chain risks: tampering, counterfeits, implants, reputational damage; software supply chain attacks (SolarWinds, 3CX, XZ Utils) reach thousands of customers through one supplier.
  • Mitigations: tier, assess and monitor suppliers; minimum requirements and flow-down; SLAs; authorized sources and anti-counterfeit (SR-11); tamper protection and inspection (SR-9, SR-10).
  • Roots of trust in hardware (TPM, Caliptra) anchor secure boot, firmware resiliency (SP 800-193: protect, detect, recover) and attestation. PUFs derive device-unique keys from manufacturing variation, so keys are not stored and chips cannot be cloned.
  • SBOMs list components (CISA 2026 minimum elements, AI SBOM guide 2026); VEX says whether a vulnerability affects a product. Federal attestation and SBOM requirements became optional, risk-based (M-26-05).
  • Program: awareness, training, education (NIST now uses one learning program); security champions (OWASP's ten principles), gamification, simulations, short frequent content.
  • Review content regularly for AI, cryptocurrency, blockchain and new attacks. Measure behavior and outcomes, not completions; involve senior leadership and stakeholders.
