7.8: Patch and vulnerability management

Overview

Objective 7.8

7.8 Implement and support patch and vulnerability based management. The objective has no sub-bullets.

Scanning as a testing technique is on the 6.2 page, configuration baselines on the 7.3 page, and the change process that patches follow on the 7.9 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its scanners report about 40,000 open findings; the team can fix perhaps 2,000 a month.

The vulnerability and patch management cycle

Objective 7.8Running a continuous cycle that finds, prioritizes, fixes and verifies vulnerabilities across every asset.

NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4 (2022), subtitled Preventive Maintenance for Technology, treats patching as routine maintenance, a normal cost of running technology, and defines patch management as "the process of identifying, prioritizing, acquiring, installing, and verifying the installation of patches". SP 800-53 SI-2 (flaw remediation) requires organizations to "identify, report, and correct system flaws", test updates "for effectiveness and potential side effects", install security updates within a defined time, and fold remediation into configuration management. RA-5 requires scanning, with privileged (credentialed) access where appropriate (RA-5(5)).

  1. InventoryKnow every asset and its software, including cloud, containers, firmware and network devices; you cannot patch what you do not know about.
  2. IdentifyScan regularly and when new vulnerabilities are announced; subscribe to vendor advisories; use SBOMs to find affected components.
  3. PrioritizeBy risk, not by count (below).
  4. RemediatePatch, upgrade, reconfigure, or apply a mitigation (disable the feature, block the port, add a WAF rule) until a fix is possible.
  5. VerifyRescan or check that the fix is installed and effective (SI-2(2) automates this status check).
  6. ReportTrack time to remediate, overdue items and exceptions; feed results into risk management.

Prioritizing by risk

Objective 7.8Combining severity, likelihood of exploitation and business context to fix what matters first.

  • CVSS (version 4.0, published November 2023 by FIRST): a severity score from 0 to 10 based on how a vulnerability can be exploited and its impact. Severity alone is not risk: most high-scoring vulnerabilities are never exploited.
  • EPSS (FIRST): "estimates the probability that a published CVE will be exploited in the wild in the next 30 days."
  • CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog (KEV): vulnerabilities with evidence of active exploitation; anything on it is a top priority.
  • SSVC (Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization): "a vulnerability prioritization methodology that helps an analyst decide on vulnerability response actions", using a decision tree over exploitation, exposure, automatability and impact. Outcomes: Track (standard timelines), Track* (monitor closely), Attend (sooner than standard), Act ("as soon as possible", with leadership involved).
  • Business context: the asset's exposure (internet-facing or internal), its criticality and the data it holds, and compensating controls already in place.

BOD 22-01 was revoked in June 2026. CISA's BOD 22-01 (2021) set fixed federal deadlines for KEV vulnerabilities (two weeks, or six months for older CVEs). On June 10, 2026 it was replaced by BOD 26-04, "Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk", which keeps the KEV catalog but sets deadlines informed by SSVC, from as short as three days to "fix on system upgrade". Older study materials still quote the two-week rule.

The retail group ranks its 40,000 findings by KEV status, internet exposure, EPSS and asset criticality. About 600 land in its "act now" bucket; the rest follow monthly patch cycles or are accepted with an exception.

Making patching work

Objective 7.8Patching quickly and safely through automation, testing, staged rollout and planned exceptions.

  • Automate (SI-2(4), SI-2(5)): central patch tools and automatic updates for endpoints and common software; rebuild rather than patch where infrastructure is immutable (new images, redeployed containers).
  • Test and stage: test on representative systems, then roll out in rings (IT staff, a pilot group of stores, then everyone), with a rollback plan. Testing must be fast enough that it does not become the delay.
  • Maintenance windows: agreed in advance with the business, so routine patches do not need individual negotiation; emergency patches follow the emergency change process.
  • Hard-to-patch systems: OT, medical and embedded devices, and end-of-support systems, get compensating controls (isolation, virtual patching, monitoring) and an exception with an expiry and a replacement plan.
  • Remove the old (SI-2(6)): uninstall previous versions and unused software, which removes whole classes of future vulnerabilities.
  • Verify the source: obtain patches only from the vendor, check signatures, and remember that update mechanisms are themselves a supply chain target.

Running the program

Objective 7.8Assigning responsibilities, deadlines and measures so vulnerabilities actually get fixed.

  • Roles: the security team runs scanning, intelligence and reporting; asset and system owners are accountable for remediation; IT operations and development teams do the work; risk owners approve exceptions.
  • Remediation deadlines: set in policy by risk level, for example actively exploited and internet-facing within days, critical within weeks, others in the normal monthly cycle; deadlines are measured from detection or publication.
  • Exceptions: when a deadline cannot be met, a written exception with compensating controls, approval by the risk owner and an expiry date (objective 6.4).
  • Measures: mean time to remediate by risk level, the share fixed within deadline, the number and age of overdue findings, scan coverage of the inventory, and the number of open exceptions.
  • Reporting: dashboards for owners, trends and top risks for leadership, with overdue critical items escalated by name.

SP 800-40 Rev. 4 suggests grouping assets into maintenance groups with common platforms and risk, each with its own patching approach and schedule, so that decisions are made once per group rather than per system. At the retail group, the 9,000 tills form one maintenance group patched by ring over two weeks; payment servers form another with a stricter schedule and extra testing.

Zero-days and emergency vulnerabilities

Objective 7.8Responding when a critical vulnerability is being exploited and no patch exists yet.

  1. Assess exposureUse the inventory and SBOMs to find every affected system, starting with internet-facing ones.
  2. MitigateApply vendor workarounds, disable the vulnerable feature, restrict access, add WAF or IPS rules, or take the system offline if the risk is high enough.
  3. HuntLook for signs that the vulnerability was already exploited, using indicators from the vendor and CISA; if it was, handle it as an incident.
  4. PatchWhen a fix is released, deploy it through the emergency change process, then verify.
  5. Follow upCheck later advisories, since first patches are sometimes incomplete, and review how quickly the organization could locate affected systems.

Patching does not evict an attacker. If a system was compromised before the patch, the attacker may already have credentials, web shells or other persistence. Exploited vulnerabilities need investigation as well as a patch.

Vulnerabilities in third-party, cloud and SaaS services

Objective 7.8Managing vulnerabilities in systems you do not patch yourself.

  • SaaS: the provider patches the application, but you still manage configuration, identities, integrations and data sharing; follow the provider's security notices.
  • IaaS and PaaS: you patch operating systems and applications you deploy (IaaS) or your code and its dependencies (PaaS and serverless); the provider patches the platform under them.
  • Managed services and appliances: contracts set patch timelines and notification; ask for evidence of patch status for critical services.
  • Open source dependencies: software composition analysis flags vulnerable libraries in your applications; fixing them means updating, rebuilding and redeploying (objective 8.4).
  • End of support: software and devices that no longer receive patches are tracked with retirement dates, isolated, and replaced; extended support contracts buy time but cost more each year.

Assets that patching often misses

Objective 7.8Finding the systems that fall outside the normal patch cycle.

  • Firmware: BIOS and UEFI, storage controllers, baseboard management controllers and network cards.
  • Network and security appliances: firewalls, VPN gateways and load balancers, often internet-facing and heavily targeted.
  • Container base images: images that were scanned at build but not rebuilt since, so they carry old libraries.
  • Browser extensions and desktop add-ins, and third-party applications that update themselves inconsistently.
  • IoT and building systems: cameras, printers, badge readers and HVAC controllers.
  • Shadow IT: cloud resources and SaaS created outside the normal process.

Comparing several discovery sources against the patch tool's coverage is the usual way to find them (objective 7.3).

Working with the business on patching

Objective 7.8Getting maintenance time and support from system owners.

  • Agree standing maintenance windows with each business owner once, instead of negotiating every patch.
  • Explain risk in business terms: which services an exploited vulnerability could stop, and what recent attacks used similar flaws.
  • Report owners' remediation performance on the security scorecard, with overdue critical items escalated by name.
  • Make patching cheaper: automation, redundancy so servers can be patched one at a time, and rebuild-and-replace for immutable systems.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: patch by score
Situation
The team patches strictly in order of CVSS base score, and an actively exploited medium-severity flaw on an internet-facing server waits for months.
Best response
Prioritize by risk: KEV status and exploitation evidence, exposure and asset criticality, then severity.
Why
CVSS measures severity, not likelihood or business impact.
Scenario 2: the untested patch
Situation
An urgent operating system patch is pushed to all 9,000 tills at once and breaks card payments in every store.
Best response
Roll out in rings with testing on representative systems and a rollback plan, even for urgent patches.
Why
Staged rollout limits the impact of a faulty patch.
Scenario 3: the unpatchable controller
Situation
A warehouse control system runs an operating system that is no longer supported and cannot be upgraded until the line is replaced next year.
Best response
Isolate it, restrict and monitor access, apply virtual patching where possible, and record a time-limited exception with the replacement plan.
Why
Compensating controls and a documented exception manage risk that cannot be patched away.
Scenario 4: patched but owned
Situation
A critical VPN vulnerability was patched two days after the advisory; the advisory says exploitation started a week before.
Best response
Hunt for signs of earlier compromise using the published indicators, and treat any found as an incident (reset credentials, rebuild if needed).
Why
Patching closes the hole but does not remove an attacker already inside.
Scenario 5: the forgotten appliance
Situation
Attackers exploit a VPN appliance that was not in the patch tool and had not been updated for a year.
Best response
Add network and security appliances to the inventory and patch process with an owner, hunt for compromise on the appliance, and monitor vendor advisories for edge devices closely.
Why
Edge appliances are high-value targets and often fall outside normal patching.

Key takeaways

  • Cycle (SP 800-40 Rev. 4, SI-2, RA-5): inventory, identify, prioritize, remediate or mitigate, verify, report; patching is preventive maintenance.
  • Prioritize by risk: CVSS v4.0 (severity), EPSS (likelihood in 30 days), KEV (known exploitation), SSVC (Track, Track*, Attend, Act), plus exposure and criticality. BOD 26-04 (June 2026) replaced BOD 22-01.
  • Operations: automate, test and roll out in rings with rollback, agreed windows, compensating controls and exceptions for hard-to-patch systems, remove old versions, verify patch sources.
  • Program: owners accountable, deadlines by risk, exceptions with expiry, maintenance groups, measures such as time to remediate and overdue items.
  • Zero-days: find exposure, mitigate, hunt for prior exploitation, emergency patch, follow up. Third parties: responsibility by service model, SCA for dependencies, end-of-support tracking.
