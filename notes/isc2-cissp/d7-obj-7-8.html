7.8–7.9: Patch, vulnerability and change management

Finding and fixing weaknesses in order of real risk, patching as routine maintenance rather than firefighting, and making sure every change to production is assessed, approved, tested and reversible.

Overview

Objectives 7.8 and 7.9

7.8 Implement and support patch and vulnerability based management. 7.9 Understand and participate in change management processes.

Scanning as a testing technique is on the 6.1–6.2 page, and configuration baselines on the 7.3–7.5 page. Neither objective has sub-bullets. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its scanners report about 40,000 open findings; the team can fix perhaps 2,000 a month.

The vulnerability and patch management cycle

Objective 7.8Running a continuous cycle that finds, prioritizes, fixes and verifies vulnerabilities across every asset.

NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4 (2022), subtitled Preventive Maintenance for Technology, treats patching as routine maintenance, a normal cost of running technology, and defines patch management as "the process of identifying, prioritizing, acquiring, installing, and verifying the installation of patches". SP 800-53 SI-2 (flaw remediation) requires organizations to "identify, report, and correct system flaws", test updates "for effectiveness and potential side effects", install security updates within a defined time, and fold remediation into configuration management. RA-5 requires scanning, with privileged (credentialed) access where appropriate (RA-5(5)).

  1. InventoryKnow every asset and its software, including cloud, containers, firmware and network devices; you cannot patch what you do not know about.
  2. IdentifyScan regularly and when new vulnerabilities are announced; subscribe to vendor advisories; use SBOMs to find affected components.
  3. PrioritizeBy risk, not by count (below).
  4. RemediatePatch, upgrade, reconfigure, or apply a mitigation (disable the feature, block the port, add a WAF rule) until a fix is possible.
  5. VerifyRescan or check that the fix is installed and effective (SI-2(2) automates this status check).
  6. ReportTrack time to remediate, overdue items and exceptions; feed results into risk management.

Prioritizing by risk

Objective 7.8Combining severity, likelihood of exploitation and business context to fix what matters first.

  • CVSS (version 4.0, published November 2023 by FIRST): a severity score from 0 to 10 based on how a vulnerability can be exploited and its impact. Severity alone is not risk: most high-scoring vulnerabilities are never exploited.
  • EPSS (FIRST): "estimates the probability that a published CVE will be exploited in the wild in the next 30 days."
  • CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog (KEV): vulnerabilities with evidence of active exploitation; anything on it is a top priority.
  • SSVC (Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization): "a vulnerability prioritization methodology that helps an analyst decide on vulnerability response actions", using a decision tree over exploitation, exposure, automatability and impact. Outcomes: Track (standard timelines), Track* (monitor closely), Attend (sooner than standard), Act ("as soon as possible", with leadership involved).
  • Business context: the asset's exposure (internet-facing or internal), its criticality and the data it holds, and compensating controls already in place.

BOD 22-01 was revoked in June 2026. CISA's BOD 22-01 (2021) set fixed federal deadlines for KEV vulnerabilities (two weeks, or six months for older CVEs). On June 10, 2026 it was replaced by BOD 26-04, "Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk", which keeps the KEV catalog but sets deadlines informed by SSVC, from as short as three days to "fix on system upgrade". Older study materials still quote the two-week rule.

The retail group ranks its 40,000 findings by KEV status, internet exposure, EPSS and asset criticality. About 600 land in its "act now" bucket; the rest follow monthly patch cycles or are accepted with an exception.

Making patching work

Objective 7.8Patching quickly and safely through automation, testing, staged rollout and planned exceptions.

  • Automate (SI-2(4), SI-2(5)): central patch tools and automatic updates for endpoints and common software; rebuild rather than patch where infrastructure is immutable (new images, redeployed containers).
  • Test and stage: test on representative systems, then roll out in rings (IT staff, a pilot group of stores, then everyone), with a rollback plan. Testing must be fast enough that it does not become the delay.
  • Maintenance windows: agreed in advance with the business, so routine patches do not need individual negotiation; emergency patches follow the emergency change process.
  • Hard-to-patch systems: OT, medical and embedded devices, and end-of-support systems, get compensating controls (isolation, virtual patching, monitoring) and an exception with an expiry and a replacement plan.
  • Remove the old (SI-2(6)): uninstall previous versions and unused software, which removes whole classes of future vulnerabilities.
  • Verify the source: obtain patches only from the vendor, check signatures, and remember that update mechanisms are themselves a supply chain target.

Change management

Objective 7.9Assessing, approving, testing, implementing and recording changes so they do not introduce outages or weaknesses.

SP 800-53 CM-3 (configuration change control) requires organizations to define which changes are controlled, review and approve or reject them with security and privacy impact analysis, document decisions, implement only approved changes, keep records, monitor change activity, and coordinate it through a change control board: "a group of qualified people with responsibility for the process of regulating and approving changes" (SP 800-128).

  1. RequestDescribe the change, its reason, the systems affected, and the implementation and rollback plans.
  2. Assess impact"Analyze changes to the system to determine potential security and privacy impacts prior to change implementation" (CM-4), plus operational risk and dependencies.
  3. ApproveBy the change board or delegated approvers, according to risk; routine changes can be pre-approved as standard changes.
  4. Test"Test, validate, and document changes ... before finalizing" (CM-3(2)), in a separate test environment (CM-4(1)).
  5. ImplementIn the agreed window, by authorized people only (CM-5 restricts who can make changes), with communication to those affected.
  6. Review and recordConfirm success, update baselines, documentation and the CMDB, and close the record; back out if verification fails.
  • Emergency changes: for urgent fixes and incident response, a faster path with a smaller approval group, but still documented and reviewed by the board afterwards.
  • Change types: standard (pre-approved, low risk, repeatable), normal (assessed and approved each time), emergency.
  • Unauthorized changes: detected through configuration monitoring and file integrity checks (objective 7.3) and treated as possible incidents.
  • Security's role: security staff take part in change review, so security impact is considered before a change, not discovered after it.

Pipelines are change management too. In DevOps, the change process moves into the pipeline: peer review of code and infrastructure changes, automated tests and security scans as gates, and deployment logs as the record. The controls are the same; they are enforced by tools rather than meetings.

Key takeaways

  • Cycle (SP 800-40 Rev. 4, SI-2, RA-5): inventory, identify, prioritize, remediate or mitigate, verify, report; patching is preventive maintenance.
  • Prioritize by risk: CVSS v4.0 (severity), EPSS (likelihood in 30 days), KEV (known exploitation), SSVC (Track, Track*, Attend, Act), plus exposure and criticality.
  • BOD 26-04 (June 2026) replaced BOD 22-01's fixed two-week rule with SSVC-informed deadlines, from three days to fix on upgrade.
  • Operations: automate, test and roll out in rings with rollback, agreed windows, compensating controls and exceptions for hard-to-patch systems, remove old versions, verify patch sources.
  • Change management (CM-3, CM-4, CM-5): request, impact analysis, approval by a change control board, test, implement by authorized staff, review and record; standard, normal and emergency changes; unauthorized changes are incidents; pipelines enforce the same controls.
