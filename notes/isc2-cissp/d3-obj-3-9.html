3.9: Site and facility security controls

The specific controls that protect equipment, media, evidence and people inside a facility: secure rooms, environmental protection, fire safety and resilient power, and keeping them working.

Overview

Objective 3.9

3.9 Design site and facility security controls. Sub-topics: wiring closets and intermediate distribution frames; server rooms and data centers; media storage facilities; evidence storage; restricted and work area security; utilities and HVAC; environmental issues (natural disasters, man-made); fire prevention, detection and suppression; power (redundant, backup).

Site selection and layout principles are on the 3.8 page; backup sites and recovery on the 7.10–7.13 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its own data center sits on a business park near a river; every store has a small network cabinet in the back office.

Securing rooms and storage areas

Objective 3.9Matching controls to each kind of sensitive space.

  • Wiring closets and intermediate distribution frames (IDFs): the cabinets and rooms where floor or store cabling meets network equipment. Often forgotten and shared with cleaning supplies, they give an attacker direct access to the network. Keep them locked, logged and monitored, and never use them for storage. SP 800-53 PE-4 (access control for transmission) and PE-9 (power equipment and cabling) protect cabling and power lines.
  • Server rooms and data centers: a single controlled entry with multi-factor access (badge plus PIN or biometric), anti-tailgating (mantrap or turnstile), visitor escort and logs, video, no windows, true floor-to-ceiling walls (so no one can climb over through a false ceiling), locked racks, and environmental and fire controls (below). SP 800-53 PE-18 (location of system components) covers placing equipment where hazards and unauthorized access are least likely to reach it.
  • Media storage facilities: backup tapes, disks and archives stored at their classification level, with environmental protection, access logs, check-in and check-out, and an offsite copy far enough away not to share the same disaster.
  • Evidence storage: locked, access-controlled storage with a full chain of custody: "a process that tracks the movement of evidence through its collection, safeguarding, and analysis lifecycle" (NIST SP 800-72), recording each person who handled it, when and why. Gaps can make evidence unusable (objective 1.5).
  • Restricted and work areas: badge-controlled zones with clear desk and clear screen rules, privacy screens, secure printing (PE-5 covers output devices), shredding bins, and controls on photography and personal devices where card or design data is visible.

Utilities, HVAC and environmental threats

Objective 3.9Keeping equipment within safe conditions and protecting it from natural and human-made hazards.

Heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) keeps equipment cool and dry enough. SP 800-53 PE-14 requires maintaining temperature, humidity and similar conditions at acceptable levels and monitoring them; industry guidance (ASHRAE) defines the recommended ranges. Too hot shortens equipment life; too humid causes condensation and corrosion; too dry causes static discharge. Data centers use hot and cold aisles, redundant cooling units and positive air pressure (so smoke and dust are pushed out, not drawn in). HVAC systems are also connected building systems that need patching and network isolation like any OT.

Water is a common cause of damage: PE-15 requires master shutoff or isolation valves that are accessible, working and known to key staff; add leak sensors under raised floors and avoid water pipes over server rooms.

Environmental threats are natural (floods, earthquakes, storms, extreme heat, wildfire, pandemics) or human-made (fire, explosions, chemical spills, civil unrest, sabotage, and failures of nearby infrastructure). Responses combine site selection, building design (raised floors, flood barriers, seismic racks), monitoring, and continuity plans with geographically separate alternate sites (objective 7.10).

Because its data center is near a river, the retail group put the equipment hall on an upper floor, installed leak detection, and keeps its recovery site in a different region.

Fire prevention, detection and suppression

Objective 3.9Preventing fires, detecting them early and putting them out without destroying the equipment.

SP 800-53 PE-13 requires fire detection and suppression systems "supported by an independent energy source", with automatic activation and notification for higher-impact systems. NFPA 75 is the US standard for protecting IT equipment and NFPA 2001 covers clean agent systems.

  • Prevention: no combustible storage in equipment rooms, fire-rated walls and doors, cable management, regular inspections, and training.
  • Detection: smoke detectors (ionization or photoelectric), very early aspirating smoke detection that samples the air, heat detectors (fixed temperature or rate of rise), and flame detectors.
  • Fire classes (US, OSHA): Class A is "ordinary combustible materials" such as paper and wood; B is flammable liquids and gases; C is "energized electrical equipment"; D is combustible metals. Commercial kitchen oils are commonly classed as K. Using water on a class C fire risks electrocution.
  • Water sprinklers: wet pipe (water always in the pipes; simple and reliable, but leaks and accidental discharge harm equipment), dry pipe (pipes hold air until a head opens; for areas that freeze), pre-action (fills the pipes only after a detector triggers, then discharges when a head opens; the usual choice for data centers) and deluge (all heads open at once; for high-hazard areas, not data centers).
  • Gas suppression: clean agents and inert gases put out fires without water damage. Halon is no longer produced: US production and import of new halons ended on January 1, 1994 because it damages the ozone layer. Gas systems need warning alarms and time for people to leave before discharge.

People first. Every fire and evacuation question puts human safety above equipment and data: doors on escape routes must open, alarms must warn before gas discharge, and an emergency power shutoff (PE-10) must be reachable but protected from accidental use.

Power

Objective 3.9Keeping systems running through power problems with redundant supplies and backup power.

Power problems range from momentary to prolonged: spikes and surges (short and longer bursts of high voltage), sags and brownouts (short and longer drops in voltage), and faults and blackouts (short and longer total losses). Noise and interference can also corrupt data. Controls build up in layers:

  1. ConditioningSurge protection and power conditioners smooth out spikes, sags and noise.
  2. Redundant supplyTwo utility feeds from different substations, and dual power supplies in servers fed from separate circuits.
  3. UPSAn uninterruptible power supply "allows connected devices to run for at least a short time when the primary power source is lost" (NIST), long enough for an orderly shutdown or for generators to start (PE-11).
  4. GeneratorsFor longer outages, with fuel stored on site, delivery contracts and regular load testing.

The Uptime Institute's data center tiers summarize how much redundancy a facility has: Tier I is basic capacity; Tier II adds redundant capacity components for power and cooling; Tier III is concurrently maintainable (any component can be serviced without stopping IT); Tier IV is fault tolerant (no single failure disrupts IT). The retail group's data center is built to Tier III; its stores run their tills on a small UPS long enough to finish transactions and close down cleanly.

Monitoring, testing and maintaining facility controls

Objective 3.9Keeping physical and environmental controls working through monitoring, tests and maintenance.

Facility controls fail quietly unless someone checks them:

  • Environmental monitoring (PE-14 requires monitoring environmental control levels): temperature, humidity, water leaks, smoke and power quality sensors feeding alerts to a monitored console, with thresholds and named responders.
  • Testing: UPS and generator load tests on a schedule, fire detection and suppression inspections by qualified contractors, leak detector tests, and failover tests for cooling and power paths.
  • Maintenance: service contracts for HVAC, UPS batteries, generators and fire systems; maintenance by escorted, authorized contractors, logged like any privileged access.
  • Access review: who holds badges and keys for each zone, reviewed with logical access reviews; key and combination changes when holders leave.
  • Integration with incident response: environmental alarms trigger documented procedures (shut down equipment safely, move workloads to another site, call the fire service), and physical events are logged as incidents.

Building management and environmental systems are themselves networked OT; they need patching, segmentation from the corporate network, and changed default passwords (objective 3.5).

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the sprinkler choice
Situation
The new data center design specifies wet-pipe sprinklers throughout the equipment hall to save cost.
Best response
Use pre-action sprinklers (and consider clean-agent suppression for the most critical rooms), with early smoke detection.
Why
Pre-action systems fill only after detection, reducing water damage from leaks or accidental discharge.
Scenario 2: the generator that never ran
Situation
During a power cut, the UPS carried the load but the generator failed to start; batteries ran out after 20 minutes.
Best response
Add scheduled generator load testing, fuel checks and maintenance contracts, and alerts on generator status; review UPS runtime against start-up time.
Why
Backup power only works if tested; UPS runtime must cover the time to bring generators online.
Scenario 3: the shared wiring closet
Situation
An audit finds a store's wiring closet unlocked and used to store cleaning supplies.
Best response
Lock it, restrict access to authorized staff, remove the supplies, disable unused ports, and add the closet to periodic checks.
Why
Wiring closets give direct network access and are often forgotten; PE-4 and PE-9 protect cabling and equipment.
Scenario 4: the evidence locker
Situation
A seized laptop from an investigation is kept in a manager's desk drawer overnight.
Best response
Move it to a secured evidence store with logged access and record every transfer in the chain of custody.
Why
Gaps in custody or insecure storage can make evidence unusable (objective 1.5).

Key takeaways

  • Spaces: lock and monitor wiring closets and IDFs; data centers with MFA entry, anti-tailgating, full-height walls; media storage with offsite copies; evidence with chain of custody; restricted areas with clear desk and secure printing.
  • Environment: HVAC keeps temperature and humidity in range (PE-14); positive pressure; water shutoff valves and leak sensors (PE-15); plan for natural and human-made hazards.
  • Fire: prevent, detect (smoke, aspirating, heat, flame), suppress; classes A, B, C, D (and K); pre-action sprinklers or clean agents for data centers; halon production ended in 1994; people before equipment.
  • Power: conditioning, dual feeds and power supplies, UPS, generators; Uptime Tiers I-IV (basic, redundant components, concurrently maintainable, fault tolerant).
  • Keep them working: environmental monitoring and alerts, scheduled tests of UPS, generators and fire systems, maintained contracts, access reviews, links to incident response; building systems are OT.
