2.4–2.6: Data life cycle, asset management and data controls

Managing data from collection to destruction with clear roles and retention rules, retiring assets safely at the end of their life, and choosing the right controls for data in each state by tailoring a baseline and adding protection tools.

Overview

Objectives 2.4, 2.5 and 2.6

2.4 Manage data lifecycle. 2.5 Ensure appropriate asset management (e.g., End of Life (EOL), End of Support (EOS)). 2.6 Determine data security controls and compliance requirements.

The previous page set classification and handling; this one manages data and assets over time and chooses their controls. End-of-life basics are in CC 5.4 and sanitization in CC 5.1. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its loyalty program holds data on 12 million customers, many in the EU.

Data management policy and the life cycle

Objective 2.4Setting a data management policy that governs data at every stage from collection to destruction.

A data management policy sets the rules for the whole life cycle: who owns which data, how it is classified, where it may be stored, how long it is kept, and how it is destroyed. It points to the classification, handling and retention standards and is approved by senior management. The life cycle is often drawn as:

  1. Collect or createOnly what is needed, with a lawful basis and a classification at creation.
  2. StoreIn approved locations, protected for its class.
  3. Use and shareBy authorized people, for its purpose.
  4. MaintainKept accurate, backed up and reviewed.
  5. ArchiveMoved to long-term storage when no longer active, still protected.
  6. DestroySecurely, when the retention period ends and no legal hold applies.

Data roles

Objective 2.4Knowing who is responsible for data in organizational terms and in privacy law.

  • Data owner: the business manager accountable for a data set; sets its classification and approves access.
  • Data custodian: usually IT; carries out the owner's decisions day to day (storage, backups, access settings, encryption).
  • Data steward: looks after data quality and correct use within the rules.
  • Controller (GDPR Art. 4): the body that, "alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data." The controller "shall be responsible for, and be able to demonstrate compliance" with the GDPR principles (Art. 5(2), accountability).
  • Processor (Art. 4): a body that "processes personal data on behalf of the controller". Controllers may use only processors giving "sufficient guarantees" of appropriate measures, under a binding contract that sets the subject, duration, purpose, data types and the processor's obligations (Art. 28).
  • Data subject: the identified or identifiable person the personal data is about.
  • Users: people who use the data within their authorization and follow the handling rules.

Organizational roles and legal roles are different axes. The retail group is the controller of its loyalty data (it decides why and how it is processed), and inside it the head of e-commerce is the data owner. Its cloud database provider and email marketing service are processors. Using a processor does not hand over the controller's accountability.

Collection, location and maintenance

Objective 2.4Collecting only what is needed, keeping data where the law allows, and keeping it accurate.

GDPR's principles (Art. 5(1)) shape the early stages: lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; and integrity and confidentiality. In practice:

  • Collection: collect only the fields needed for a stated purpose, tell people what is collected and why, and record the lawful basis. Data never collected cannot be breached.
  • Location: data residency is where data is physically stored and processed; data localization laws require some data to stay in a country (China's PIPL in objective 1.4); data sovereignty means the data is subject to the laws where it sits. Cloud regions, backups and support access all count.
  • Maintenance: keep data accurate and up to date, correct errors on request, back it up, and review classifications and access regularly.

The retail group stopped collecting customers' dates of birth for the loyalty program, keeping only a "over 18" flag, and pinned the EU loyalty database and its backups to EU cloud regions.

Retention, remanence and destruction

Objective 2.4Keeping data exactly as long as required, then destroying it so nothing recoverable remains.

A retention schedule lists each record type, its minimum retention period (set by law, regulation or contract, such as tax or employment records) and its maximum. GDPR's storage limitation principle says personal data must be "kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary." Keeping data too long increases breach impact and legal exposure; destroying it too early breaks the law. A legal hold overrides the schedule for data relevant to litigation or an investigation (objective 1.5).

Remanence is "residual information remaining on storage media after clearing" (CNSSI 4009). It is why deleting files or formatting a drive is not enough, why SSDs need purge or destroy rather than overwriting, and why cloud data is usually ended by cryptographic erase (destroying the keys). Destruction follows NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 (clear, purge, destroy) and is recorded with a certificate (objective 2.2). Remember backups, archives, logs, test copies and copies held by processors, which must be destroyed too.

Asset retention and retirement

Objective 2.5Managing assets through end of life and end of support, and retiring them without leaving data or risk behind.

An asset management policy covers the asset life cycle: acquisition from approved sources, inventory and ownership, maintenance, and retirement. Its CISSP-level points:

  • End of life and end of support: once a vendor stops issuing security updates, the asset carries growing, unfixable risk. Plan replacement before the date; if it must stay, isolate it and record the accepted risk (SP 800-53 SA-22; CC 5.4).
  • Asset retention: some assets must be kept after they leave service, for example a laptop under legal hold or equipment needed to read archived data, so retirement checks for holds and dependencies first.
  • Retirement: migrate or archive the data, sanitize or destroy the storage, remove the asset from the inventory, revoke its accounts, certificates and licenses, and update network and monitoring configurations. Disposal follows environmental rules, through a certified provider.

When the retail group retired 2,000 old tills, it kept three units (and the software to read their transaction archives) for the seven-year financial retention period, and had the rest shredded by a certified recycler with a certificate per serial number.

Data states and selecting controls

Objective 2.6Selecting controls for data at rest, in transit and in use, by starting from a baseline and tailoring it to the system and its obligations.

Controls differ by data state: at rest (encryption, access control, backups), in transit (TLS, VPN, integrity checks) and in use (access control, DLP on endpoints, confidential computing). Requirements come from the classification, from laws and contracts (GDPR, PCI DSS, HIPAA) and from the organization's standards.

Rather than choosing controls one by one, organizations start from a control baseline: "the set of controls that are applicable to information or an information system to meet legal, regulatory, or policy requirements" (NIST). NIST SP 800-53B provides low, moderate and high security baselines and a privacy baseline. The baseline is then tailored, which NIST defines as modifying baselines by:

  1. Common controlsIdentifying and designating controls inherited from elsewhere (a data center's physical security, a cloud provider's controls).
  2. ScopingApplying scoping considerations to decide which baseline controls apply, for example by technology, physical infrastructure, public access or the system's security objectives.
  3. Compensating controlsSelecting alternatives that give "equivalent or comparable protection" where a baseline control cannot be used.
  4. ParametersAssigning values to organization-defined parameters (how often, how long, which roles).
  5. SupplementingAdding controls or enhancements the risk calls for.
  6. SpecificationProviding extra detail for implementation.

Scoping vs tailoring. Scoping is one step inside tailoring: removing or adjusting baseline controls that do not apply to this system. Tailoring is the whole process, including adding controls. Industry frameworks are tailored the same way: PCI DSS applies to the cardholder data environment, so segmenting card systems shrinks its scope.

Data protection methods

Objective 2.6Knowing what DRM, DLP, CASB and CNAPP tools do and when each fits.

NIST's glossary lists these terms without full definitions, so the descriptions below are the common industry meanings:

  • Digital rights management (DRM; for business documents often called information rights management): persistent controls that travel with a file, such as who may open it, whether it can be copied, printed or forwarded, and when access expires. Protection continues after the file leaves the organization.
  • Data loss prevention (DLP): identifies sensitive data and monitors or blocks it in use on endpoints, in motion on networks and email, and at rest in storage (CC 5.1).
  • Cloud access security broker (CASB): a policy enforcement point between users and cloud services, working through APIs or as a proxy. It discovers unsanctioned cloud use (shadow IT), applies DLP and access policies to SaaS data, and detects risky activity.
  • Cloud-native application protection platform (CNAPP): a combined platform for securing cloud workloads and their configuration, usually bundling cloud security posture management (misconfigurations), workload protection, cloud identity and entitlement management, and scanning of infrastructure-as-code and containers before deployment.

The retail group applies DRM to board papers and product designs shared with suppliers, DLP on endpoints and email for card numbers and customer exports, a CASB to control which SaaS apps hold company data, and a CNAPP across both cloud providers to catch exposed storage and over-privileged roles.

Key takeaways

  • Data management policy governs the life cycle: collect or create, store, use and share, maintain, archive, destroy.
  • Roles: owner (accountable, classifies), custodian (implements), steward (quality), users; GDPR controller (decides purposes and means, accountable), processor (acts on the controller's behalf under an Art. 28 contract), data subject.
  • Collection and location: minimise, state the purpose; residency, localization and sovereignty; keep data accurate.
  • Retention: schedule with minimum and maximum periods; storage limitation; legal holds override. Remanence is residual data after clearing; destroy with SP 800-88 Rev. 2, including backups and processors' copies.
  • Assets: plan for EOL/EOS, retain assets under hold or needed for archives, retire by migrating data, sanitizing, removing from inventory and revoking credentials.
  • Controls: by data state; start from a baseline (SP 800-53B), then tailor: common controls, scoping, compensating controls, parameters, supplementing, specification. Scoping is part of tailoring.
  • Tools: DRM (controls travel with the file), DLP (detect and block sensitive data), CASB (control cloud app use), CNAPP (secure cloud workloads and configuration).
