2.4: Managing the data life cycle

Managing data from collection to destruction: the policy that governs it, who is responsible at each stage in organizational and legal terms, where data may live, how long it is kept, and how it is removed so nothing recoverable remains.

Overview

Objective 2.4

2.4 Manage data lifecycle. Sub-topics: data management policy; data roles (owners, controllers, custodians, processors, users/subjects); data collection; data location; data maintenance; data retention; data remanence; data destruction.

Classification and handling are on the 2.1 and 2.2 pages; asset retirement on the 2.5 page; controls by data state on the 2.6 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its loyalty program holds data on 12 million customers, many in the EU.

Data management policy and the life cycle

Objective 2.4Setting a policy that governs data at every stage from creation to destruction.

A data management policy sets the rules for the whole life cycle: who owns which data, how it is classified, where it may be stored and processed, who may use it for what, how long it is kept, and how it is destroyed. It points to the classification, handling and retention standards, assigns roles, and is approved by senior management. It is the bridge between privacy law (what the organization must do with personal data) and security (how it protects data). The life cycle is often drawn as:

  1. Collect or createOnly what is needed, with a lawful basis and a classification at creation.
  2. StoreIn approved locations, protected for its class.
  3. Use and shareBy authorized people and systems, for its stated purpose.
  4. MaintainKept accurate, backed up and reviewed.
  5. ArchiveMoved to long-term storage when no longer active, still protected and still findable.
  6. DestroySecurely, when the retention period ends and no legal hold applies.

Security controls change along the way: strong access control while data is active, cheaper but still encrypted storage when archived, and verified destruction at the end. Every copy follows the same life cycle, including backups, exports, test copies and data held by suppliers.

Data roles

Objective 2.4Knowing who is responsible for data in organizational terms and in privacy law.

Two vocabularies describe data roles. The organizational one:

  • Data owner: the business manager accountable for a data set; classifies it, approves access, accepts its risks and sets retention within policy.
  • Data custodian: usually IT or a platform team; carries out the owner's decisions day to day (storage, backups, access settings, encryption).
  • Data steward: looks after data quality, definitions and correct use within the rules.
  • Users: use the data within their authorization and follow the handling rules.

The privacy law one, from the GDPR:

  • Data subject: the "identified or identifiable natural person" the personal data relates to (Art. 4(1)), such as a loyalty member.
  • Controller: the body that, "alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data" (Art. 4(7)). The controller "shall be responsible for, and be able to demonstrate compliance" with the GDPR principles (Art. 5(2), accountability).
  • Processor: a body that "processes personal data on behalf of the controller" (Art. 4(8)). Controllers may use only processors giving "sufficient guarantees" of appropriate measures, under a binding contract that sets the subject matter, duration, nature and purpose, types of data, categories of data subjects, and the controller's rights and the processor's obligations (Art. 28).

Organizational and legal roles are different axes. The retail group is the controller of its loyalty data (it decides why and how it is processed), and inside it the head of e-commerce is the data owner. Its cloud database provider and email marketing service are processors. Using a processor does not hand over the controller's accountability, and a processor that starts deciding its own purposes for the data becomes a controller for that processing.

Collection, location and maintenance

Objective 2.4Collecting only what is needed, keeping data where the law allows, and keeping it accurate.

GDPR's principles (Art. 5(1)) shape the early stages: lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; and integrity and confidentiality.

  • Collection: collect only the fields needed for a stated purpose, tell people what is collected and why, record the lawful basis, and classify at the point of collection. Data never collected cannot be breached, so minimization is also a security control.
  • Location: data residency is where data is physically stored and processed; data localization laws require some data to stay in a country (China's PIPL, objective 1.4); data sovereignty means the data is subject to the laws where it sits. These are common industry terms; NIST has no glossary definitions for them. Cloud regions, backups, disaster recovery sites, support staff with remote access and AI services that process prompts elsewhere all count as location.
  • Maintenance: keep data accurate and up to date, correct errors on request, back it up, reconcile copies, and review classifications and access regularly. Inaccurate data is an integrity failure and, for personal data, a breach of the accuracy principle.

The retail group stopped collecting customers' dates of birth for the loyalty program, keeping only an "over 18" flag, and pinned the EU loyalty database, its backups and its analytics copies to EU cloud regions.

Retention, remanence and destruction

Objective 2.4Keeping data exactly as long as required, then destroying it so nothing recoverable remains.

A retention schedule lists each record type, its minimum retention period (set by law, regulation or contract, such as tax, employment or financial records) and its maximum, plus who owns it and how it is disposed of. GDPR's storage limitation principle says personal data must be "kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary", with limited exceptions for archiving, research and statistics. Keeping data too long increases breach impact, discovery costs and legal exposure; destroying it too early breaks the law.

  • Legal hold overrides the schedule for data relevant to litigation or an investigation (objective 1.5); systems must be able to suspend deletion selectively.
  • Automation: retention labels and lifecycle policies in email, storage and databases delete or archive data on schedule, which is more reliable than manual clean-ups.

Remanence is "residual information remaining on storage media after clearing" (CNSSI 4009). It is why deleting files or formatting a drive is not enough, why SSDs need purge or destroy rather than overwriting, and why cloud data is usually ended by cryptographic erase (destroying the keys). Destruction follows NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 (clear, purge, destroy) and is recorded with a certificate (objective 2.2).

Destruction covers every copy. When a retention period ends, destroy the data in production, backups, archives, logs, analytics copies, test environments and processors' systems. Contracts should require processors to delete or return data and confirm it in writing.

Data subject rights and the life cycle

Objective 2.4Designing the life cycle so the organization can answer people's requests about their data, and can trace where data came from and went.

Privacy laws give individuals rights that reach into every stage of the life cycle. Under the GDPR they include the rights of access, rectification, erasure, restriction of processing, data portability and objection; CCPA/CPRA gives California consumers rights to know, delete, correct and opt out of sale or sharing (objective 1.4). To honor them on time, the organization must be able to:

  • Find all of a person's data across systems, including backups, analytics copies and processors, which depends on an accurate data inventory and data maps.
  • Verify identity before releasing or deleting data, so the rights process itself does not become a way to steal data; requests from impostors are a known fraud pattern.
  • Act consistently: correct or delete in every copy, tell processors to do the same, and record what was done.
  • Know the exceptions: legal retention duties or legal holds can override erasure, and the response must explain that.

Data lineage records where data came from, how it was transformed and where it went: from the store till to the data warehouse to a marketing model. It supports rights requests, breach scoping (which downstream copies were affected?), data quality, and AI governance (which data trained this model?). Records management applies the retention schedule to formal records, with clear rules for what counts as a record, where the authoritative copy lives, and who may dispose of it.

Security and privacy meet here. Strong data maps and lineage make breach response faster and more accurate, and minimization and timely deletion mean there is less to protect, less to search and less to notify about. At the retail group, the privacy team answers about 300 access and deletion requests a month through a workflow that queries the loyalty database, the email platform and the analytics warehouse, then sends deletion instructions to the two processors and records their confirmations.

De-identification methods

Objective 2.4Reducing identifiability while keeping data useful.

  • Anonymization: irreversibly removing the ability to identify individuals; truly anonymous data falls outside the GDPR, but true anonymization is hard to achieve.
  • Pseudonymization: replacing identifiers with pseudonyms, with the key to re-identify kept separately; the data is still personal data under the GDPR, but the risk is lower.
  • Tokenization: replacing a sensitive value (such as a card number) with a token, with the mapping kept in a secure vault; reduces the systems in scope for PCI DSS.
  • Masking: hiding parts of values, either permanently in copies (static masking, for test data) or on display (dynamic masking).
  • Generalization and aggregation: replacing exact values with ranges or totals.

Re-identification risk: combining de-identified data with other data sets can identify people again, so de-identification is assessed against what others could link it with. Under the GDPR, joint controllers (Art. 26) decide purposes together and must agree their responsibilities, and processors may use sub-processors only with the controller's authorization (Art. 28).

Scenario check

PracticeApplying life cycle reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: keep everything
Situation
Marketing wants to keep all loyalty data forever "in case it is useful for AI later".
Best answer
Apply the retention schedule: keep personal data only as long as needed for its stated purposes; anonymize or aggregate what is needed for long-term analysis; delete the rest.
Why
GDPR storage limitation and purpose limitation; excess data increases breach impact and legal exposure.
Scenario 2: the processor's new idea
Situation
The email marketing provider proposes using the retailer's customer lists to train its own recommendation product for other clients.
Best answer
Refuse unless there is a lawful basis and a new agreement; the contract should limit the processor to processing on documented instructions.
Why
A processor acts on the controller's behalf (Art. 28); using the data for its own purposes would make it a controller without a lawful basis.
Scenario 3: the hold and the schedule
Situation
Seven-year-old transaction records are due for automatic deletion next week, but legal counsel has issued a hold for a pricing dispute covering that period.
Best answer
Suspend deletion for the records covered by the hold; resume normal deletion when counsel lifts it.
Why
Legal holds override retention schedules; destroying held data can bring court sanctions.
Scenario 4: the erasure request
Situation
A customer asks to have all their data erased. Their order history must be kept for tax purposes for seven years.
Best response
Erase what is not needed (marketing profile, preferences, analytics copies, data at processors), keep the order records under the legal retention duty with access restricted, and explain this in the response.
Why
Erasure rights have exceptions for legal obligations; the response must cover every copy and be honest about what is kept and why.

Key takeaways

  • Data management policy governs the life cycle: collect or create, store, use and share, maintain, archive, destroy; every copy follows it.
  • Roles: owner (accountable, classifies), custodian (implements), steward (quality), users; GDPR controller (decides purposes and means, accountable under Art. 5(2)), processor (acts on the controller's behalf under an Art. 28 contract), data subject. Different axes.
  • Collection: minimize, state the purpose, record the lawful basis, classify at creation. Location: residency, localization, sovereignty, including backups, support access and AI services. Maintenance: accuracy, backups, reviews.
  • Retention: schedule with minimum and maximum periods; storage limitation; legal holds override; automate.
  • Remanence is residual data after clearing; destroy with SP 800-88 Rev. 2 across every copy, including processors'.
