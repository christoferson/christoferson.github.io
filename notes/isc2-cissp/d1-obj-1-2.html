1.2: Security concepts and the five pillars

The five properties every later domain protects: what each means in law and in NIST's vocabulary, how it is lost, which controls protect it, how they trade off against each other, and how to tell authenticity, integrity and non-repudiation apart under exam pressure.

Overview

Objective 1.2

1.2 Understand and apply security concepts. Sub-topic: confidentiality, integrity, availability, authenticity and non-repudiation (the five pillars of information security).

The CIA triad, AAA and authentication factors are introduced in CC 1.1. This page takes the CISSP view: precise definitions, the controls that deliver each property, and the reasoning to choose between them. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. A retail group with about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services for customers and staff.

The five pillars at a glance

Objective 1.2Knowing the five properties and why the outline lists two beyond the CIA triad.

The outline names five properties, which ISC2 labels the five pillars of information security:

PillarAssures thatTypical controls
ConfidentialityInformation is disclosed only to those authorizedAccess control, encryption, classification, data loss prevention
IntegrityInformation and systems are not changed without authorization, or changes are detectedHashes, change control, integrity monitoring, write-once storage
AvailabilitySystems and data are usable when neededRedundancy, backups, capacity planning, DoS protection
AuthenticityData or a message is genuine and came from its claimed sourceStrong authentication, message authentication codes, signatures
Non-repudiationAn originator cannot credibly deny an action, provably to a third partyDigital signatures with protected private keys, protected audit logs

Why five rather than three? In US federal law (FISMA), integrity is defined as guarding against improper modification or destruction and "includes ensuring information non-repudiation and authenticity". Listing them separately, as the CISSP outline does, forces the distinction that matters in practice: a control can protect content from change without proving who sent it, and can prove who sent it to the recipient without being able to prove it to anyone else.

Confidentiality

Objective 1.2Preventing unauthorized disclosure of information in every state it can be in.

FISMA defines confidentiality as "preserving authorized restrictions on information access and disclosure, including means for protecting personal privacy and proprietary information"; FIPS 199 adds that "a loss of confidentiality is the unauthorized disclosure of information." The privacy phrase is the link between security and privacy, but privacy is broader: personal data can be misused by people who are fully authorized (objective 3.1 covers privacy by design).

How confidentiality is lost: theft by attackers, insiders browsing data out of curiosity, misdirected email, misconfigured cloud storage, shoulder surfing, eavesdropping on networks, social engineering, data left on disposed media, and inference from data that looks harmless on its own (objective 3.5).

Controls, by data state:

  • At rest: access control and least privilege; encryption of disks, databases and backups (SP 800-53 SC-28); classification and labeling so handling rules apply.
  • In transit: TLS, IPsec and SSH; SC-8 requires protecting the confidentiality and integrity of transmitted information because "unprotected communication paths are exposed to the possibility of interception and modification."
  • In use ("in process" in NIST's wording): application-level access control, masking of sensitive fields on screen, endpoint DLP, and confidential computing for the most sensitive processing.

Administrative controls matter as much as technical ones: need-to-know, non-disclosure agreements, clear desk rules, and training. At the retail group, call center staff see only the last four digits of a customer's card; full numbers are tokenized and only the payment platform can reverse the token.

Integrity

Objective 1.2Preventing or detecting unauthorized change to data and systems, whether by error or attack.

FISMA: "guarding against improper information modification or destruction"; FIPS 199: "a loss of integrity is the unauthorized modification or destruction of information." NIST's cryptographic definition is narrower: the property "whereby data has not been altered in an unauthorized manner since it was created, transmitted, or stored." Integrity covers both data integrity (values are correct and unaltered) and system integrity (the system performs as intended, free from unauthorized manipulation). It also covers honest mistakes: SP 800-53 SI-7 notes that unauthorized changes "can occur due to errors or malicious activity."

Controls:

  • Prevent: least privilege and separation of duties; input validation; configuration change control (CM-3 requires reviewing and approving changes "with explicit consideration for security and privacy impact analyses"); write-once-read-many storage for logs and records.
  • Detect: integrity verification tools (SI-7) such as file integrity monitoring and secure boot measurements; database constraints and reconciliation; audit logs of changes.
  • Correct: backups and versioning to restore known-good data.

Three cryptographic tools give increasing assurance, and questions test which one fits:

  • Hash (SHA-2, SHA-3): a fixed-length "fingerprint" of the data. It detects change, but has no key, so anyone who alters the data can also recompute the hash. Useful only if the hash itself is protected or published separately.
  • MAC or HMAC: a checksum computed with a shared secret key. It detects change and shows the data came from a holder of the key.
  • Digital signature: computed with the sender's private key and verified with the public key. It detects change, authenticates the origin and supports non-repudiation.

Integrity models formalize the rules: Biba ("no read down, no write up") and Clark-Wilson (well-formed transactions and separation of duties) are on the 3.1–3.2 page.

Availability

Objective 1.2Keeping systems and data usable by authorized people when they need them.

FISMA: "ensuring timely and reliable access to and use of information"; FIPS 199: a loss of availability is "the disruption of access to or use of information or an information system." Availability is lost to hardware and software failure, power and network outages, human error, natural disasters, ransomware, and denial-of-service attacks. CISA's joint DDoS guidance groups attacks as volumetric ("attacks aiming to consume available bandwidth"), protocol ("attacks which exploit vulnerabilities in network protocols") and application attacks; SP 800-53 SC-5 adds that denial of service can also come from "a lack of planning to support organizational needs with respect to capacity and bandwidth."

Controls: redundancy and failover (no single points of failure); backups at a frequency "consistent with recovery time and recovery point objectives" (CP-9); capacity planning and monitoring; DDoS protection and rate limiting; patching and change control (a failed change is a common outage cause); and tested continuity and recovery plans (objectives 7.10 to 7.13).

Availability is a security property. Ransomware is first an availability attack (and, with data theft, a confidentiality attack). A security control that takes a critical service down, such as an over-aggressive IPS rule, also harms security. Good answers protect availability alongside confidentiality.

Authenticity

Objective 1.2Assuring that data, messages and parties are genuine and come from where they claim.

NIST defines authenticity in two senses. In its cryptographic and identity guides it is "the property that data originated from its purported source." In its risk and control guides it is "the property of being genuine and being able to be verified and trusted; confidence in the validity of a transmission", extended to the message and its originator. Authenticity therefore applies to data (this file is the real price list), to messages (this payment instruction really came from the store), and to parties (this is really our bank's website).

How authenticity is lost: spoofed email senders, look-alike domains and fake websites, forged documents, counterfeit hardware, deepfake voice and video, and man-in-the-middle attacks that substitute content.

Controls: strong (ideally phishing-resistant) authentication of users and devices; mutual TLS and certificate validation for systems; MACs and digital signatures on messages and software; email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC); code signing and signed updates; provenance checks in the supply chain; and out-of-band verification of unusual requests.

Authenticity is not the same as integrity. A message can be intact but fake (an attacker's genuine, unaltered message pretending to be from the CEO), or authentic but altered in transit. Questions that ask "how does the receiver know it came from the store?" are about authenticity; "how does the receiver know it was not changed?" are about integrity.

Non-repudiation

Objective 1.2Producing evidence that a party performed an action, strong enough to convince a third party.

SP 800-53 defines non-repudiation as "protection against an individual who falsely denies having performed a certain action"; its control AU-10 requires organizations to "provide irrefutable evidence" that an individual or process performed defined actions, such as creating information, sending or receiving a message, or approving it. FIPS 186-5 describes it as assurance of integrity and origin that a third party can verify as coming from the holder of the private key.

How it is delivered: AU-10 names "digital signatures and digital message receipts". A digital signature, when "properly implemented", gives origin authentication, data integrity and "signatory non-repudiation" (FIPS 186-5). The words "properly implemented" carry weight: several NIST sources say signatures give non-repudiation only with a supporting infrastructure and policy. In practice that means:

  • the private key is under the signer's sole control, ideally in a smart card, security key or HSM, never shared or copied;
  • the signer's identity was verified before the certificate was issued (PKI, objective 3.6);
  • certificates are checked for revocation, and signatures are time-stamped so they can be verified after the certificate expires;
  • supporting logs are accurate and protected (AU-8 time stamps, AU-9 protection of audit information).

A MAC gives authenticity but not non-repudiation. NIST SP 800-63-4 says it plainly: "MACs provide authenticity and integrity protection but not non-repudiation protection." Sender and receiver share the key, so either could have made the tag, and a judge cannot tell which. Only an asymmetric digital signature with a key only the signer holds supports non-repudiation. A shared administrator account defeats non-repudiation for the same reason: you cannot prove which person used it.

Logs support non-repudiation for actions inside systems: unique user IDs, accurate time, and logs that the person being logged cannot alter. At the retail group, payment messages between stores and the processing center carry MACs (authenticity and integrity inside the company), while instructions to the bank to release large settlements are digitally signed by named officers using smart cards (non-repudiation).

Impact levels and trade-offs

Objective 1.2Rating how much each property matters for a system, and balancing them when they conflict.

FIPS 199 rates the potential impact of losing confidentiality, integrity or availability as low (a "limited" adverse effect), moderate ("serious") or high ("severe or catastrophic", which can include loss of life). Each information type gets a rating for each objective, written as a triple, for example {(confidentiality, high), (integrity, high), (availability, moderate)} for card data. Two details often tested:

  • A system takes the high-water mark: the highest rating for each objective across all the information it holds (objective 2.1).
  • "Not applicable" can be used only for confidentiality (public information has no confidentiality impact); every system has some integrity and availability impact.

The pillars trade off against each other and against usability. Encryption protects confidentiality but can hurt availability if keys are lost; strict change control protects integrity but slows urgent fixes; wide replication improves availability but creates more copies to protect. NIST SP 800-12 describes the goal as "the optimal balance between protecting the information or system and utilizing available resources."

The priority also depends on context. NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 notes that IT security programs typically focus on confidentiality, integrity and availability "in that order", while an operational technology environment "prioritizes safety, followed by availability, integrity, and confidentiality" (objective 3.5). For the retail group, the payment platform puts integrity and confidentiality first; the distribution centers' conveyor controllers put safety and availability first.

DAD is a memory aid, not a NIST term. Many study guides pair the triad with its opposites: disclosure, alteration and destruction (or denial). NIST's own words are unauthorized disclosure, modification or destruction, and disruption of access.

The pillars in AI systems

Objective 1.2Applying the same five properties to AI models, their data and their outputs.

NIST's adversarial machine learning taxonomy (AI 100-2, 2025) classifies attacker goals in triad terms: availability breakdown, integrity violation, privacy compromise, and, for generative AI, misuse enablement. Mapping them:

  • Confidentiality: model extraction, training data extraction and membership inference; the AML field tends to call these "privacy" attacks. Leaking restricted data into public AI tools is also a confidentiality failure.
  • Integrity: data poisoning ("an adversary controls a subset of the training data") and model poisoning, evasion with crafted inputs, and prompt injection that changes an AI agent's behavior. NIST warns that poisoning "can cause availability or integrity violations."
  • Availability: overwhelming AI services, including energy-latency attacks that make each query expensive.
  • Authenticity: deepfakes and synthetic content that impersonate people or sources; content provenance and watermarking aim to restore it.
  • Non-repudiation: logging prompts, outputs, model versions and the human who approved an AI-assisted decision, so actions can be attributed later.

Scenario check

PracticeChoosing the property and the control that a situation calls for.

Scenario 1: the disputed order
Situation
A supplier claims it never sent an order confirmation that committed it to a low price. The message was protected with an HMAC using a key both companies share.
Best answer
The HMAC shows the message was not altered and came from a key holder, but cannot prove which company created it. To prevent this in future, require digital signatures with keys that only each party holds.
Why
Non-repudiation needs an asymmetric signature and sole control of the private key; a shared-key MAC gives authenticity and integrity only.
Scenario 2: the published price file
Situation
Stores download a nightly price file from a web server and check its SHA-256 hash, which is published in the same folder. An attacker who reached the server replaced both.
Best answer
Sign the file (or the hash) with the head office's private key, and have stores verify the signature with the public key they already hold.
Why
A plain hash has no key, so anyone who can change the data can change the hash. A signature adds authenticity, which the stores need, not just change detection.
Scenario 3: the shared admin account
Situation
A database was deleted using the shared "dbadmin" account, which five administrators use. Logs show the account and the time, but no one admits doing it.
Best answer
Replace the shared account with individual named accounts (with privileged access management), so each action is tied to one person, and protect the logs from the administrators.
Why
Accountability and non-repudiation require unique identities; a shared credential means no individual can be held responsible. Restoring from backup fixes availability, but not the root cause.

Key takeaways

  • Five pillars: confidentiality, integrity, availability, authenticity and non-repudiation. FISMA folds authenticity and non-repudiation into integrity; the CISSP outline separates them.
  • Confidentiality: no unauthorized disclosure, in every data state (SC-28 at rest, SC-8 in transit, in-use controls); privacy is broader.
  • Integrity: no unauthorized or undetected change to data or systems, from attack or error; prevent, detect (SI-7), correct. Hash detects change; MAC adds a shared-key origin; signature adds non-repudiation.
  • Availability: timely, reliable access; redundancy, backups tied to RTO and RPO, capacity, DDoS protection, tested recovery.
  • Authenticity: genuine and from the claimed source (data, messages, parties); strong authentication, signatures, email authentication, code signing.
  • Non-repudiation (AU-10): evidence a third party can verify; digital signatures with sole control of private keys, PKI, time stamps, protected logs, no shared accounts. MACs do not give it.
  • FIPS 199: low, moderate, high; high-water mark; "not applicable" only for confidentiality. Pillars trade off; OT puts safety and availability first.
