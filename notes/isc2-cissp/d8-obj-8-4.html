8.4: Assessing the security impact of acquired software

Judging the risk of software you buy, download or subscribe to, before and after you start relying on it.

Overview

Objective 8.4

8.4 Assess security impact of acquired software. Sub-topics: COTS; open source (SCA); third-party; managed services (enterprise applications); cloud services (SaaS, IaaS, PaaS, FaaS).

Supply chain risk management is on the 1.11 page; cloud service models in CC 4.3; supplier audits on the 6.5 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is choosing a new warehouse management system and relies on about 40 SaaS applications.

Acquired software

Objective 8.4Assessing the security of software you did not write, which is most of what an organization runs.

  • Commercial off-the-shelf (COTS): no source code access, so rely on the vendor's evidence: security development practices (an SSDF-based attestation where offered), independent certifications and test reports, vulnerability disclosure and patch history, support lifetime, and an SBOM. Test it in your configuration, harden it, and write security requirements into the contract (objective 3.3).
  • Open source: code is visible but nobody is contractually responsible. Software composition analysis (SCA) identifies "open-source and third-party components in use in an application, their known security vulnerabilities" and licenses. Also check project health (active maintainers, release frequency, security policy), pin and verify versions, and plan how you would patch or replace an abandoned component.
  • Third-party software (contractor-built or partner code, plug-ins, SDKs): require secure development practices and testing in the contract, review or test the code you receive, and limit what plug-ins and SDKs can access.
  • Managed services (such as enterprise applications run by a provider): assess the provider's controls through attestation reports, questionnaires and right-to-audit clauses; agree incident notification, data location and exit terms; manage your own configuration and user access.
  • Cloud services: SaaS, PaaS, IaaS and FaaS move different parts of the responsibility to the provider (CC 4.3). For SaaS you mostly assess the provider and configure identity, data sharing and logging; for IaaS and PaaS you secure much more yourself; for FaaS, your code, dependencies and permissions.

CISA's Secure by Demand guide gives buyers questions to ask before, during and after procurement, such as SSO and phishing-resistant MFA at no extra cost, no default passwords, and logs included in the base product. The retail group sent those questions to all three warehouse system finalists and dropped one that charged extra for SSO and audit logs.

Buying software does not transfer the risk. The vendor writes the code, but the organization is still accountable for the data and the systems it runs on. Assess before purchase, configure securely, patch, and monitor, as for software you build yourself.

Assessing a supplier before purchase

Objective 8.4Gathering evidence about software you are about to buy.

  • Security questionnaires, based on a standard set where possible, scaled to the data and access involved.
  • Attestations and certifications: SOC 2 Type 2 reports, ISO/IEC 27001 certificates, PCI attestations, or government authorizations, checked for scope and exceptions (objective 6.5).
  • Development evidence: SSDF-aligned practices, penetration test summaries, an SBOM, and a published vulnerability disclosure policy with a history of timely fixes.
  • Product security features: SSO and MFA, role-based access, audit logs, encryption, API security and secure defaults, included in the base price.
  • Hands-on testing: install or configure a trial in your environment and test it, especially for on-premises software that will hold sensitive data.
  • Company factors: financial stability, ownership and location (which may matter for legal access to data), and support model.

The depth of assessment follows the risk: a design tool with no sensitive data needs a short review, while the warehouse management system that connects to stores, suppliers and payment data gets the full assessment.

Contract terms for software and services

Objective 8.4Writing security expectations into the agreement, where they can be enforced.

  • Security requirements: the controls the supplier must maintain, often by reference to a standard.
  • Vulnerability handling: how quickly the supplier notifies customers of vulnerabilities and provides fixes, by severity.
  • Incident notification: deadlines and content of breach notices, and cooperation in investigations.
  • Data: location, use limits (including use for training AI models), return and deletion at the end of the contract.
  • Assurance: right to audit or regular attestation reports, and notice of material changes such as new subprocessors.
  • Support life: end-of-support dates and notice periods, so replacement can be planned.
  • Exit: data export formats, transition help and, for critical on-premises software from small vendors, source code escrow.

Open source governance

Objective 8.4Using open source deliberately rather than by accident.

  • Policy: which licenses are acceptable, who approves new components, and what checks apply.
  • Project health: maintainers, release frequency, response to security reports, and a security policy; tools such as the OpenSSF Scorecard automate some of these checks.
  • Inventory: SBOMs for every application, so a newly disclosed vulnerability can be located quickly.
  • Controlled intake: components come through an internal registry, pinned and verified (objective 8.2).
  • Contingency: for critical components, a plan if the project is abandoned or compromised: fork and maintain internally, or replace.
  • Contributing back: fixing and reporting issues upstream helps keep the components the organization depends on healthy.

Monitoring acquired software over time

Objective 8.4Keeping assurance current after the contract is signed.

  • Advisories and patches: subscribe to supplier security notices and patch acquired software through the normal vulnerability management process (objective 7.8).
  • SaaS configuration: check tenant settings (sharing, external access, MFA, logging, integrations) regularly, manually or with SaaS security posture management tools.
  • Integrations and tokens: review which third-party apps and API tokens have access to SaaS data, and remove unused ones; connected apps are a common breach path.
  • Reassessment: repeat supplier assessments on a cycle set by risk, and after incidents, acquisitions or major product changes.
  • End of life: track support end dates and plan replacement before software stops receiving fixes.

At the retail group, a yearly review of SaaS integrations found 26 connected apps with access to the shared file platform; 11 were unused and removed.

Third-party code and managed services in depth

Objective 8.4Handling code written by others for you, and applications run for you.

  • Contracted development: require the supplier to follow your secure coding standard and SSDF practices, deliver source code and an SBOM, fix vulnerabilities found within agreed times, and allow your review and testing before acceptance.
  • Plug-ins, extensions and SDKs: they run inside your application or platform with its permissions; approve them, limit their access, and keep them updated. Mobile SDKs can collect personal data, so privacy review is part of approval.
  • Managed enterprise applications: the provider runs and patches the application, but you control users, roles, integrations and data; agree who administers what, how changes are made, and how quickly security issues are fixed.
  • Integration risk: every connection between acquired software and your systems (APIs, file transfers, service accounts) needs least privilege, monitoring and an owner.

At the retail group, the delivery partners' SDK in the mobile app was found to collect device location in the background; the contract was amended and the SDK replaced by a server-side integration.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the vendor with no evidence
Situation
A small vendor of a critical warehouse application has no attestation report and declines a penetration test.
Best response
Assess by other means (questionnaire, design review, testing the product in your environment), add contract terms for security and vulnerability handling, and add compensating controls such as isolation.
Why
Lack of evidence raises risk; buyers gather what evidence they can and limit exposure.
Scenario 2: SaaS means no responsibility
Situation
The business assumes that because the CRM is SaaS, the provider handles all of its security.
Best response
Configure identity, MFA, sharing and logging in the tenant, review integrations, and confirm responsibilities in the contract.
Why
In SaaS the customer still owns identity, configuration and data decisions.
Scenario 3: the abandoned library
Situation
A core open source library used by the mobile app has had no maintainer for two years and has an unfixed vulnerability.
Best response
Plan replacement or an internal fork, mitigate the vulnerability now, and add project health checks to the intake policy.
Why
Open source risk includes the health of the project, not just known CVEs.
Scenario 4: AI training on our data
Situation
A SaaS provider's updated terms allow customer data to be used to train its AI models.
Best response
Review with legal and privacy, opt out or negotiate terms, and assess whether the data classification allows continued use.
Why
Contract terms on data use are part of acquired software risk.

Key takeaways

  • Acquired software: COTS (vendor evidence, SBOM, contract terms), open source (SCA, project health), third-party code (contract and review), managed services (attestations, exit terms), cloud (responsibility by model). Buying does not transfer accountability.
  • Before purchase: questionnaires, attestations checked for scope, development evidence, product security features, hands-on testing, company factors; depth by risk.
  • Contracts: security requirements, vulnerability and incident notification, data location and use, assurance, support life, exit and escrow.
  • Open source: policy, project health, SBOM inventory, controlled intake, contingency, contributing back.
  • Over time: advisories and patching, SaaS configuration and integration reviews, reassessment, end-of-life tracking.
