Overview

Objectives 7.3, 7.4 and 7.5

7.3 Perform Configuration Management (CM). 7.4 Apply foundational security operations concepts. 7.5 Apply resource protection.

These objectives apply principles taught elsewhere to daily operations: least privilege and separation of duties in CC 3.2 and on the 5.5–5.6 page; handling, sanitization and encryption on the 2.1–2.3 page. This page focuses on what operations teams do. Change management itself is on the 7.8–7.9 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its 300 store servers, 9,000 tills and cloud estate are built from code and checked for drift every night.

Configuration management

Objective 7.3Provisioning systems from approved baselines, detecting drift, and automating both.

Security-focused configuration management (NIST SP 800-128) keeps systems in known, approved, secure states. The SP 800-53 CM family supplies the controls: CM-2 baseline configuration, CM-3 configuration change control, CM-6 configuration settings, CM-7 least functionality and CM-8 component inventory.

  1. BaselineDefine the approved configuration for each system type, starting from a recognized secure configuration guide or benchmark (NIST's National Checklist Program, SP 800-70, lists many), tailored and documented.
  2. ProvisionBuild every new system from the baseline: hardened golden images, templates, or infrastructure as code, so new systems are secure by default and identical.
  3. Control changesChanges to baselines go through change control, are tested, and update the baseline version.
  4. MonitorCompare running systems with the baseline continuously; drift (unapproved changes) is corrected or investigated as a possible incident.
  5. InventoryKeep a current inventory of components and their configurations, in a configuration management database (CMDB) or from automated discovery.

Automation makes this scale: configuration management tools enforce the desired state, infrastructure-as-code templates are reviewed and scanned before deployment, and immutable infrastructure replaces rather than patches running servers. The code repository then becomes a critical asset: protect it, require reviews for changes, and sign what is deployed.

Baseline vs change management. Configuration management defines and maintains the approved state of systems; change management is the process that approves changes to it. A question about an unapproved setting found on a server is about configuration monitoring; a question about approving a new setting is about change management.

Foundational security operations concepts

Objective 7.4Applying defense in depth, least privilege, separation of duties, privileged account controls and job rotation in daily operations.

  • Defense in depth: multiple coordinated layers of controls, technical, operational and administrative, so no single failure leads to compromise. In operations: hardened hosts, segmentation, monitoring, backups and trained staff, each covering the others' gaps.
  • Need-to-know and least privilege: least privilege limits what someone can do to "the minimum necessary to accomplish assigned tasks"; need-to-know limits which information they may see even within their clearance or role. Operations staff get only the tools and systems their shift requires.
  • Separation of duties and responsibilities: "no user should be given enough privileges to misuse the system on their own". Examples in operations: whoever writes code does not deploy it to production alone; security administrators do not also administer the audit logs; requesting and approving access are different people. Two-person control (dual authorization) covers the most critical actions.
  • Privileged account management: an inventory of every privileged account, separate admin accounts, no standing privilege (just-in-time elevation), credentials vaulted and rotated, sessions recorded, and break-glass accounts sealed, monitored and tested (objective 5.5).
  • Job rotation: moving people through roles periodically. It reduces dependence on one person, gives cross-training, and exposes fraud or errors that the previous holder was hiding. Mandatory vacations serve a similar detective purpose: someone else does the job for a while.

Collusion. Separation of duties stops one person acting alone, but two people can collude. Job rotation and mandatory vacations make long-running collusion harder to sustain and more likely to be found.

Media management and protection

Objective 7.5Tracking and protecting storage media from issue to disposal.

Media management covers every medium that holds organizational data: disks and SSDs, backup tapes, USB drives, mobile devices, paper, and cloud storage volumes and snapshots. The SP 800-53 MP family sets the controls: access (MP-2), marking (MP-3), storage (MP-4), transport (MP-5), sanitization (MP-6) and use (MP-7, such as restricting or blocking removable media).

  • Inventory and tracking: label media with their classification, log check-out and return, account for backup tapes in transit and at offsite storage.
  • Archiving: long-term storage under the retention schedule, in a format and on media that will still be readable when needed, protected against loss and environmental damage, with periodic test reads.
  • Encryption: encrypt removable media, backups and archives, with keys managed separately so a lost tape or drive is not a breach.
  • Disposal and reuse: sanitize by the method the classification and destination require (NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2: clear for reuse inside the organization, purge or destroy for media leaving it), with a record for each item (objective 2.2).

The retail group blocks USB storage on all store and office devices except for a few approved, encrypted drives, and its backup provider collects tapes in locked cases with each case's serial number logged at both ends.

Protecting data in each state

Objective 7.5Matching protection to whether data is stored, moving or being processed.

  • At rest: full-disk, database and file encryption, access control, backups protected at the same level as the original, and integrity protection such as write-once storage for logs and backups.
  • In transit: TLS, IPsec or SSH on every network path, including internal ones; certificate validation; no fallback to cleartext protocols.
  • In use: access control and least privilege in applications, masking sensitive fields on screen, endpoint DLP, memory protection, and confidential computing (trusted execution environments) for the most sensitive processing (objective 3.4).

Operations teams own much of this day to day: checking that encryption is actually on, that certificates are valid, that backups are encrypted and restorable, and that sensitive data has not appeared somewhere it should not be (discovery scans).

Key takeaways

  • Configuration management (SP 800-128; CM-2, -3, -6, -7, -8): baseline from secure configuration guides, provision from it (images, infrastructure as code), control changes, monitor drift, keep the inventory; automate and protect the code.
  • Defense in depth: coordinated technical, operational and administrative layers.
  • Need-to-know limits information; least privilege limits actions. Separation of duties stops one person acting alone; two-person control for critical actions.
  • Privileged accounts: inventory, separate accounts, JIT, vaulting, session recording, monitored break-glass. Job rotation and mandatory vacations detect fraud and counter collusion.
  • Media (MP family): label, track, store and transport securely, restrict removable media, archive readably, encrypt, sanitize to SP 800-88 Rev. 2 with records.
  • Data states: at rest (encryption, access control, WORM), in transit (TLS, IPsec, SSH everywhere), in use (least privilege, masking, DLP, confidential computing).
