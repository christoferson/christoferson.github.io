Overview

Objective 4.1

4.1 Apply secure design principles in network architectures.

Network basics (the OSI and TCP/IP layers, TCP and UDP, ports, IPv4 and IPv6, NAT, VPNs, firewalls and IDS, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth) are in CC 4.1, and segmentation and zero trust in CC 4.2. This page covers the architecture decisions on top. Network devices themselves are on the 4.2 page and secure channels on 4.3. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its stores connect over SD-WAN, with 5G backup links and in-store Wi-Fi for staff, devices and customers.

Models, addressing and multilayer protocols

Objective 4.1Using the layer models to place controls and attacks, and understanding how addressing and layering create risk.

The OSI model has seven layers (physical, data link, network, transport, session, presentation, application); the TCP/IP model has four (link, internet, transport, application). CISSP questions use them to place things: a switch and ARP spoofing at layer 2, routers and IPsec at layer 3, TCP, UDP and port scanning at layer 4, TLS often described at layers 5 to 6, and web application firewalls at layer 7.

IP traffic is delivered in four ways:

  • Unicast: one sender to one receiver.
  • Broadcast: to every host on the local network (IPv4 only). RFC 4291: "There are no broadcast addresses in IPv6, their function being superseded by multicast addresses."
  • Multicast: to a group of hosts that joined it, such as video streams or routing updates.
  • Anycast: one address announced from many locations, delivered to the "nearest" one by routing. DNS root servers and CDNs use it; it spreads load and absorbs denial-of-service traffic.

IPv6 brings a much larger address space, mandatory support for extension headers and stateless address autoconfiguration. Its security impact: firewalls, IDS and logging must handle IPv6 as well as IPv4, or attackers use the unwatched protocol; and IPv6 tunnels through IPv4 networks can bypass controls.

Multilayer protocols carry one protocol inside another (encapsulation). That is how VPNs and overlays work, but it also means a firewall that inspects only the outer layer may miss what is inside, and it enables covert channels (data hidden in DNS or ICMP) and VLAN hopping through double tagging. Industrial protocols such as Modbus and DNP3 were designed for serial lines without authentication; carried over TCP/IP, they become reachable from anywhere the network reaches.

Secure and converged protocols

Objective 4.1Choosing protocols that protect traffic, and securing protocols that merge storage, voice and compute onto IP networks.

  • IPsec (layer 3): AH gives integrity and authentication without confidentiality; ESP adds encryption. Transport mode protects the payload between two hosts; tunnel mode wraps the whole packet, typically between gateways. IKEv2 negotiates the keys.
  • SSH: encrypted remote administration and file transfer (SFTP), replacing Telnet, rlogin and FTP. Protect host keys and prefer key-based or certificate authentication.
  • SSL/TLS: all versions of SSL and TLS 1.0 and 1.1 are deprecated; use TLS 1.2 or 1.3 (TLS 1.3 is now RFC 9846). TLS also protects many other protocols (HTTPS, LDAPS, SMTP with STARTTLS).

Converged protocols carry traffic that once had its own network over standard Ethernet and IP:

  • iSCSI: "a transport protocol for SCSI that works on top of TCP" (RFC 7143), so storage travels over the IP network. Put it on a dedicated, isolated storage network, use CHAP authentication, and use IPsec where it crosses untrusted links.
  • VoIP: SIP sets up calls (use SIPS, which requires TLS) and RTP carries the voice; SRTP adds confidentiality, message authentication and replay protection. Risks include eavesdropping, toll fraud and denial of service, so voice usually gets its own VLAN and quality-of-service priority.
  • InfiniBand over Ethernet (RoCE, RDMA over Converged Ethernet): very low-latency direct memory access between servers, used in HPC and AI clusters. It assumes a trusted network, so keep it in isolated fabrics.
  • Compute Express Link (CXL): "an industry-supported Cache-Coherent Interconnect for Processors, Memory Expansion and Accelerators" (CXL 4.0 was released in November 2025). It lets servers share memory pools, so memory in a shared pool needs isolation and encryption between tenants.

Transport architecture, performance and traffic flows

Objective 4.1Understanding how networks are built and measured, and which directions traffic flows in.

  • Topologies: bus, ring, star (a single central failure point), mesh (full or partial, resilient but costly) and hybrid; data centers typically use a spine-leaf design.
  • Planes: the data plane forwards traffic; the control plane decides where it goes (routing protocols, spanning tree); the management plane configures and monitors the devices (SSH, SNMP, APIs). Protect the control plane (route authentication, rate limiting) and isolate the management plane, because whoever controls them controls the network.
  • Switching: store-and-forward switches receive the whole frame and check it for errors before forwarding; cut-through switches start forwarding after reading the destination address, so latency is lower but corrupt frames pass through.
  • Performance metrics: bandwidth (capacity), throughput (what actually gets through), latency (delay), jitter (variation in delay, which hurts voice and video) and signal-to-noise ratio (signal strength relative to interference, which matters for wireless). Security tools add latency, and sudden changes in these metrics can signal an attack.
  • Traffic flows: north-south traffic enters or leaves the data center or network; east-west traffic moves between systems inside it. Traditional perimeter firewalls watch north-south traffic; lateral movement by attackers is east-west, which is why internal segmentation matters.

Physical, logical and micro-segmentation

Objective 4.1Dividing the network so that a compromise in one place cannot spread freely.

  • Physical segmentation: separate hardware and cabling. In-band management uses the production network; out-of-band management uses a separate network (or console and cellular links), so devices can be managed even when production is down or compromised. An air gap has no network connection at all, though data still crosses on removable media, which must be controlled.
  • Logical segmentation on shared hardware:
    • VLANs separate layer 2 broadcast domains (a 12-bit ID, about 4,000 VLANs); traffic between them goes through a router or firewall.
    • VPNs create protected virtual networks over shared ones.
    • Virtual routing and forwarding (VRF): one router keeps "a number of separate forwarding tables" (RFC 4364), so overlapping networks stay separate at layer 3.
    • Virtual domains: one firewall split into several independent virtual firewalls, each with its own policy and administrators.
  • Micro-segmentation: controls around each workload rather than each network. It uses overlays and encapsulation (VXLAN, with a 24-bit ID for about 16 million segments), distributed firewalls enforced on every host or hypervisor, routers and IDS/IPS inside the network, and identity-based policy. It is one of the three approaches to zero trust in NIST SP 800-207, in which "network location alone does not imply trust".

The retail group separates its payment network from everything else (shrinking PCI DSS scope), manages network devices out of band, and uses micro-segmentation in the data center so that a compromised web server cannot reach the database servers of other applications.

Edge, wireless, cellular and CDNs

Objective 4.1Securing where the network meets other networks, and the radio links that extend it.

  • Edge networks: ingress filtering drops incoming traffic that should not arrive (spoofed internal or private source addresses); egress filtering limits what may leave, which blocks malware callbacks and data theft and stops the organization's addresses being used in spoofed attacks. Peering links the organization's network directly with providers or partners; it needs route filtering and authentication so false routes are not accepted.
  • Wi-Fi: WPA3 (SAE for personal, 802.1X with EAP for enterprise), separate SSIDs and VLANs for staff, devices and guests, and wireless IDS for rogue access points and evil twins.
  • Bluetooth: NIST SP 800-121 Rev. 2 recommends strong security modes, keeping devices undiscoverable and turning Bluetooth off when not needed.
  • Zigbee: a low-power "self-organizing, self-healing mesh" for sensors and building automation, with AES-128 encryption; protect the network key and the joining process.
  • Satellite: high latency and links run by a provider. CISA advisory AA22-076A (March 2022; its update attributes the activity to "Russian state-sponsored malicious cyber actors") recommends MFA, no default credentials, least privilege, reviewing trust with providers, and independent encryption on leased links.
  • Cellular (4G and 5G): 5G is largely software-defined and cloud-based. CISA's guidance names supply chain risk, limited vendor diversity, legacy 4G weaknesses and a larger attack surface. Private 5G and network slicing (separate virtual networks on shared infrastructure) need isolation between slices.
  • Content delivery networks (CDNs): distributed caches that serve content from near the user, routed there by DNS-based steering or anycast. They absorb denial-of-service traffic and often include a WAF, but they terminate TLS, so the CDN provider sees the traffic, and the origin servers must accept connections only from the CDN.

Software-defined and cloud networks

Objective 4.1Securing networks that are configured by software and APIs rather than by hand.

  • Software-defined networking (SDN): a central controller holds the control plane and programs the switches through APIs. Policy becomes consistent and automatable, but the controller and its APIs become prime targets: authenticate and authorize every API call, protect the controller, and log changes.
  • SD-WAN: software-defined control of wide-area links (broadband, MPLS, 4G and 5G), with central policy, encrypted tunnels and path selection. NIST SP 800-215 covers this enterprise network landscape alongside SASE.
  • Network functions virtualization (NFV): firewalls, routers and load balancers run as software on standard servers (ETSI defines the framework). The hypervisor and orchestration then need the same protection as the functions.
  • Virtual private cloud (VPC): a logically isolated network in a public cloud, with its own subnets, route tables, security groups and network access lists. Misconfigured routes, public subnets and over-broad peering are the usual weaknesses.
  • Monitoring and management: network observability (flow records, packet capture, telemetry and logs together), traffic shaping (prioritizing and limiting traffic), capacity management, and fault detection and handling (alerts, redundancy and automatic failover). Good visibility of normal traffic is what makes anomalies stand out.
  • Software-defined security: security policy expressed as code and applied automatically wherever workloads run, such as security groups defined in infrastructure-as-code, policy pushed by an SDN controller, or zero trust policy engines. Changes go through version control and review like any code.

Software-defined means API-defined. In SDN, SD-WAN, NFV and VPCs, whoever can call the management API can rewrite the network. Treat those APIs and the identities that use them as the most privileged part of the network.

Network attacks and design countermeasures

Objective 4.1Recognizing common attacks at each layer and the architectural choices that defeat them.

  • Layer 2: ARP spoofing (poisoning address mappings to intercept traffic), MAC flooding of switches, VLAN hopping by double tagging or switch spoofing, rogue DHCP servers. Countermeasures: dynamic ARP inspection, DHCP snooping, port security, disabling trunk negotiation on access ports, and not using the native VLAN for user traffic.
  • Layer 3: IP spoofing, route injection and BGP hijacking (announcing someone else's address space), and ICMP abuse. Countermeasures: ingress and egress filtering, route filtering and authentication with peers, and resource public key infrastructure for route origin validation.
  • DNS: cache poisoning, hijacked registrar accounts, DNS tunneling for data exfiltration. Countermeasures: DNSSEC for origin authentication, registrar locks and MFA, monitoring DNS query patterns, encrypted DNS to trusted resolvers.
  • Transport and session: SYN floods, session hijacking, TLS downgrade and stripping. Countermeasures: SYN cookies and DDoS protection, TLS everywhere with strict transport security, certificate validation.
  • Denial of service: volumetric, protocol and application floods; countered with capacity, upstream scrubbing, CDNs, rate limiting and anycast.
  • Wireless: evil twins, deauthentication attacks, weak pre-shared keys; countered with WPA3, 802.1X with certificate validation, and wireless intrusion detection.

OSI and TCP/IP in depth

Objective 4.1Layers, data units, devices and the TCP handshake.

  • Layer 7 Application (data): HTTP, DNS, SMTP, SNMP; application firewalls and proxies.
  • Layer 6 Presentation (data): formats, encoding, compression, encryption (TLS is often placed here or at layer 5).
  • Layer 5 Session (data): setting up and managing sessions; RPC, NetBIOS; circuit-level gateways.
  • Layer 4 Transport (segments for TCP, datagrams for UDP): ports, reliability, flow control.
  • Layer 3 Network (packets): IP addressing and routing, ICMP, IPsec; routers and layer 3 switches.
  • Layer 2 Data link (frames): MAC addresses, ARP, VLANs, 802.1X; switches and bridges.
  • Layer 1 Physical (bits): cables, radio, signals; hubs and repeaters.

The TCP/IP model groups these into four layers: application (OSI 5 to 7), transport, internet and link (network access). Each layer encapsulates the data from the layer above with its own header on the way down, and removes it on the way up.

TCP is connection-oriented and reliable: the three-way handshake is SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK; flags include SYN, ACK, FIN (orderly close), RST (abort), PSH and URG. SYN floods abuse the handshake by leaving it half-open. UDP is connectionless, with no handshake or delivery guarantee, used for DNS queries, streaming, VoIP and many tunnels.

Each switch port is its own collision domain; a broadcast domain ends at a router or VLAN boundary, which is why VLANs and routing limit the reach of broadcast-based attacks such as ARP spoofing.

Common ports and secure replacements

Objective 4.1Well-known ports the exam expects, paired with their protected versions.

  • FTP TCP 20/21 (cleartext): replace with SFTP (over SSH, 22) or FTPS (implicit 990).
  • SSH TCP 22: secure remote shell and file transfer. Telnet TCP 23 (cleartext): replace with SSH.
  • SMTP TCP 25 (server to server), 587 (submission with STARTTLS), 465 (implicit TLS).
  • DNS UDP/TCP 53: add DNSSEC for integrity; DNS over TLS (853) or HTTPS (443) for privacy.
  • DHCP UDP 67/68; TFTP UDP 69 (no authentication).
  • HTTP TCP 80: replace with HTTPS TCP 443.
  • Kerberos 88; NTP UDP 123 (Network Time Security adds authentication).
  • POP3 TCP 110 (secure 995); IMAP TCP 143 (secure 993).
  • SNMP UDP 161/162: use SNMPv3 with authentication and encryption instead of v1 and v2c community strings.
  • LDAP 389: use LDAPS (636) or signed and sealed binds.
  • SMB TCP 445: never exposed to the internet; SMB signing and encryption internally.
  • Syslog UDP 514: use syslog over TLS (6514) for integrity and confidentiality.
  • RDP TCP 3389: never directly exposed; use a gateway with MFA.
  • RADIUS UDP 1812/1813; TACACS+ TCP 49; IPsec NAT traversal UDP 4500, IKE UDP 500.

Wireless security in depth

Objective 4.1Wi-Fi security generations, authentication methods and wireless attacks.

  • WEP: RC4 with a 24-bit IV; broken within minutes; never use.
  • WPA: an interim fix using TKIP; deprecated.
  • WPA2: AES with CCMP. Personal mode uses a pre-shared key (open to offline guessing if the passphrase is weak); Enterprise mode uses 802.1X with a RADIUS server.
  • WPA3: Personal mode replaces the PSK handshake with SAE (simultaneous authentication of equals), which resists offline dictionary attacks and gives forward secrecy; Enterprise offers a 192-bit mode; management frame protection is required; Enhanced Open (OWE) encrypts open networks.
  • EAP methods: EAP-TLS (certificates on client and server, strongest), PEAP and EAP-TTLS (a TLS tunnel protecting an inner password method; clients must validate the server certificate), EAP-FAST; LEAP is obsolete.
  • Attacks: rogue access points and evil twins (fake networks with a trusted name), deauthentication floods to force reconnections, WPS PIN brute force (disable WPS), KRACK (key reinstallation against the WPA2 handshake, fixed by patches), and jamming.
  • Bluetooth: bluejacking (unsolicited messages), bluesnarfing (stealing data), bluebugging (taking control); keep devices non-discoverable and patched.
  • RFID and NFC: short-range, but open to skimming, cloning and relay attacks; protect badges and payment cards with shielding and cryptographic tags.

IPsec and IPv6 in depth

Objective 4.1Details of IPsec negotiation and of IPv6 that affect security.

  • Security associations (SAs): one-way agreements on algorithms and keys, identified by a security parameter index (SPI); a two-way connection needs two.
  • IKE: negotiates SAs and keys. IKEv1 used phase 1 (main or aggressive mode, building a protected channel) and phase 2 (quick mode, creating the IPsec SAs); IKEv2 is simpler and current.
  • AH vs ESP: AH authenticates the whole packet including the outer IP header, so it breaks when NAT changes addresses; ESP provides encryption with optional integrity, and works through NAT using NAT traversal (UDP 4500).

IPv6:

  • 128-bit addresses written in hexadecimal; no broadcast (multicast and anycast instead); global unicast (2000::/3), link-local (fe80::/10) and unique local (fc00::/7) addresses.
  • Neighbor Discovery replaces ARP; rogue router advertisements can redirect traffic, so switches use RA Guard.
  • ICMPv6 is essential (neighbor discovery, path MTU), so it cannot be blocked wholesale; filter by type.
  • Hosts may configure addresses automatically (SLAAC); privacy extensions change addresses over time, complicating logging.
  • Transition: dual stack, tunnels such as 6to4 and Teredo (which can bypass IPv4 security controls if unmanaged), and NAT64. Networks that "do not use IPv6" often have it enabled on hosts by default.

Cellular threats: fake base stations (IMSI catchers) and forced downgrades to older generations, SIM swapping to take over phone numbers, and SMS interception; 5G conceals the subscriber identity (SUCI) to reduce tracking.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the flat store network
Situation
A store's tills, staff laptops, cameras and guest Wi-Fi all share one network.
Best response
Segment into separate VLANs or zones with firewall rules between them, putting the tills in an isolated payment segment.
Why
Segmentation limits lateral movement and shrinks PCI DSS scope; guest and IoT devices must never reach payment systems.
Scenario 2: the management interfaces
Situation
Network devices are administered over the same network that carries user traffic, using Telnet.
Best response
Move management to an out-of-band network, replace Telnet with SSH, and use AAA (TACACS+) with MFA for administrators.
Why
The management plane controls the network; isolate and encrypt it.
Scenario 3: east-west blind spot
Situation
An attacker moved between servers in the data center for weeks without triggering any alert.
Best response
Add east-west visibility (flow logs, internal IDS sensors) and micro-segmentation that allows only required flows between workloads.
Why
Perimeter controls watch north-south traffic; lateral movement is east-west.
Scenario 4: the leaked routes
Situation
The company's public address range briefly appears announced by an unknown network.
Best response
Work with carriers to filter the announcements, publish route origin authorizations, and monitor routing for future hijacks.
Why
BGP hijacking diverts traffic; route filtering and origin validation are the defenses.

Key takeaways

  • Models: OSI 7 layers, TCP/IP 4; use them to place devices, protocols and attacks. Addressing: unicast, broadcast (IPv4 only), multicast, anycast; secure IPv6 as fully as IPv4.
  • Multilayer protocols enable VPNs and overlays but also hide traffic and covert channels. Secure protocols: IPsec (AH, ESP; transport, tunnel), SSH, TLS 1.2 or 1.3.
  • Converged protocols: iSCSI (isolate, CHAP, IPsec), VoIP (SIPS, SRTP, voice VLAN), RoCE and CXL (isolated fabrics, tenant isolation).
  • Architecture: topologies; data, control and management planes (protect and isolate the latter two); store-and-forward vs cut-through; bandwidth, throughput, latency, jitter, SNR; north-south vs east-west.
  • Segmentation: physical (in-band vs out-of-band, air gap), logical (VLAN, VPN, VRF, virtual domains), micro-segmentation (overlays, distributed firewalls, zero trust).
  • Edges and radio: ingress and egress filtering, peering with route filtering; WPA3, Bluetooth, Zigbee, satellite (AA22-076A), 5G slicing; CDNs absorb attacks but see the traffic.
  • Software-defined: SDN, SD-WAN, NFV, VPC, observability and software-defined security; protect the controllers and APIs.
  • Attacks and countermeasures: ARP spoofing and VLAN hopping (DAI, DHCP snooping, trunk control), spoofing and BGP hijacking (filtering, route origin validation), DNS attacks (DNSSEC, registrar locks), DoS (scrubbing, CDN, anycast), wireless attacks (WPA3, 802.1X).
