Overview

Objective 6.5

6.5 Conduct or facilitate security audits. Sub-topics: internal (within organization control); external (outside organization control); third-party (outside enterprise control); location (on-premises, cloud, hybrid).

Audit strategy is on the 6.1 page; reporting and remediation on the 6.4 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its annual PCI DSS assessment is due in three months, and last year's penetration test left 14 findings open.

Conducting and facilitating audits

Objective 6.5Running internal audits and supporting external and third-party ones, on premises and in the cloud.

  1. PlanAgree the objective, standard (criteria), scope, period and auditors; confirm independence.
  2. Gather evidenceDocuments, interviews, observation and testing; auditors sample populations (for example 25 leavers from the year) rather than check everything.
  3. EvaluateCompare evidence with the criteria and identify gaps.
  4. ReportFindings, risk, management responses and an opinion where required.
  5. Follow upTrack agreed actions to completion and verify them in the next audit.
  • Internal audits (within the organization's control): planned by internal audit by risk, reporting to the audit committee; security teams prepare evidence and respond to findings.
  • External audits (outside the organization's control): independent auditors for certifications and regulatory requirements (ISO/IEC 27001 certification, PCI DSS assessments, financial audits). Facilitating them means a single coordinator, evidence ready and organized, the right people available, and accurate answers; never hide or alter evidence.
  • Third-party audits (outside the enterprise's control): customers or regulators auditing you under contract or law, and you auditing suppliers through the right-to-audit clause, questionnaires or their attestation reports.
  • Location: on premises, auditors can inspect everything; in the cloud, they rely on the provider's attestations and certifications for its part and examine your configurations and logs for yours; in hybrid environments, scope must include the connections and identity links between the two.

Continuous control monitoring and compliance checks (objective 6.2) make audits easier: evidence is collected all year rather than in a rush before the auditors arrive.

Preparing for and supporting an audit

Objective 6.5Making audits efficient and accurate from the auditee side.

  1. Agree scope and criteriaConfirm systems, locations, period and standard with the auditor; check that scope matches what customers or regulators expect.
  2. Name a coordinatorOne point of contact who tracks requests, schedules interviews and keeps evidence organized.
  3. Prepare evidenceRespond to the auditor's request list with complete, dated evidence from source systems; populations must be complete so auditors can sample fairly.
  4. Brief intervieweesStaff answer accurately and within their knowledge; they say "I will find out" rather than guess.
  5. Track findings earlyDiscuss draft findings promptly, correct factual errors, and agree management responses with owners and dates.

Never alter or hide evidence. Misleading an auditor or regulator turns a control weakness into an integrity failure, and can carry legal consequences. Disclose known gaps and the plan to fix them.

Audits of cloud services and third parties

Objective 6.5Getting assurance about providers you cannot audit directly.

  • Attestation reports: providers share SOC 2 reports, ISO/IEC 27001 certificates, PCI attestations and government authorizations such as FedRAMP, usually under NDA. Check the scope (services and regions), the period covered, exceptions noted, and the controls the customer must operate for the provider's controls to work.
  • Gaps between reports: if a report's period ended months ago, ask what has changed since; some providers issue letters covering the gap.
  • Right to audit: contracts for critical suppliers may grant audit rights, though large cloud providers usually offer reports and documentation instead of individual audits.
  • Your side of shared responsibility: auditors will still examine your configurations, identities, logs and data protection in the cloud, so the same evidence discipline applies.
  • Fourth parties: reports may exclude a provider's own subcontractors; understand which critical services those cover.

After the audit

Objective 6.5Turning audit findings into lasting improvements.

  • Management responses: each finding gets an agreed action, owner and date, or a documented risk acceptance.
  • Tracking: findings go into the same register as other remediation, reported to the audit committee until closed.
  • Verification: internal audit or the next external audit checks that actions were completed and effective.
  • Learning: recurring themes across audits feed the security strategy and the next assessment plan (objective 6.1).

Audit standards and certification cycles

Objective 6.5Knowing the main audit and attestation regimes and how often they happen.

  • ISO/IEC 27001 certification: an accredited certification body audits the information security management system in two stages (stage 1 reviews documentation and readiness, stage 2 tests implementation), then performs surveillance audits, usually yearly, with recertification every three years. ISO 19011 gives guidelines for auditing management systems, and organizations must also run their own internal audits.
  • SOC reports (AICPA): SOC 1 covers controls relevant to customers' financial reporting; SOC 2 reports on controls against the Trust Services Criteria (security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, privacy) for a restricted audience; SOC 3 is a general-use summary. A Type 1 report covers the design of controls at a point in time; a Type 2 report also tests operating effectiveness over a period, usually 6 to 12 months.
  • PCI DSS: depending on transaction volume and payment brand rules, merchants and service providers are assessed by a Qualified Security Assessor (QSA) who produces a Report on Compliance (ROC), or complete a Self-Assessment Questionnaire (SAQ); both end with an Attestation of Compliance (AOC).
  • Regulatory examinations: sector regulators and data protection authorities can examine or inspect, often with legal powers to demand evidence.

The retail group maps its controls once to a common control set, so evidence collected for the ISO surveillance audit also serves the PCI assessment and the customers who ask for its SOC 2 report.

Audit evidence and sampling

Objective 6.5How auditors decide whether evidence is enough.

Auditors need sufficient (enough) and appropriate (relevant and reliable) evidence. Reliability increases when evidence comes from independent sources, is system-generated rather than prepared by the auditee, and is obtained directly by the auditor.

  • Inquiry: asking staff; weakest on its own and always corroborated.
  • Observation: watching a control performed, such as a visitor sign-in.
  • Inspection: examining records, configurations and documents.
  • Reperformance: the auditor performs the control again (recalculates, re-runs a query, tries an access) to confirm the result.

Design testing asks whether a control would meet its objective if operated as described (often through a walkthrough of one transaction); operating effectiveness testing asks whether it actually worked throughout the period, using a sample. Samples can be statistical (random selection, so results can be projected to the population with stated confidence) or judgmental (chosen by the auditor, for example high-risk items). Either way, the auditor draws the sample from a complete population supplied by the auditee, which is why completeness of populations matters so much.

Internal audit and the three lines

Objective 6.5Where internal audit fits in governance and why its independence matters.

The Institute of Internal Auditors' Three Lines Model (2020) describes roles rather than rigid layers: management owns and manages risk (first-line roles such as operations and IT, second-line roles such as security, risk and compliance functions that set policy, advise and monitor), and internal audit gives independent, objective assurance (the third line) to the governing body, usually through an audit committee.

  • Independence: the head of internal audit reports functionally to the audit committee, so findings cannot be suppressed by the managers being audited.
  • Objectivity: auditors do not audit work they did or designed recently, and do not take on management responsibilities.
  • Risk-based audit plan: approved by the audit committee, covering the highest risks, including cybersecurity, over a cycle of years.
  • Coordination: internal audit can rely on the work of others (external auditors, the security team's testing) where it has assessed its quality, avoiding duplicate effort.

Security professionals usually facilitate audits rather than conduct them: they help scope, provide evidence and respond to findings, while keeping their own self-assessments clearly separate from independent audit work.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the auditor's sample
Situation
The auditor asks for the list of all leavers in the year so they can pick a sample. A manager wants to send only leavers whose accounts were removed on time.
Best response
Provide the complete population; let the auditor sample; disclose known late removals with the remediation plan.
Why
Auditors sample from complete populations; filtering evidence is misleading.
Scenario 2: the old SOC report
Situation
A SaaS supplier's latest SOC 2 Type 2 report covers a period that ended ten months ago.
Best response
Ask for a bridge or gap letter and any significant changes since, check the scope and exceptions, and confirm your complementary controls.
Why
Attestations cover a period and scope; gaps need to be addressed.
Scenario 3: the regulator's visit
Situation
A data protection authority announces an inspection after a breach notification.
Best response
Coordinate through legal counsel, prepare the incident timeline, risk assessments and policies, and answer accurately.
Why
Regulatory audits need accurate cooperation; due care evidence matters.
Scenario 4: findings that never close
Situation
Audit findings from two years ago are still "in progress" with no owner.
Best response
Assign owners and dates, report overdue items to the audit committee, and escalate or formally accept the risk.
Why
Audit value depends on follow-up; open findings without owners are unmanaged risk.

Key takeaways

  • Process: plan, gather evidence (sampling from complete populations), evaluate, report, follow up.
  • Types: internal, external and third-party; location changes what auditors can see.
  • Supporting audits: agreed scope, one coordinator, complete evidence from sources, briefed interviewees, early discussion of findings; never alter evidence.
  • Cloud and suppliers: attestation reports (scope, period, exceptions, complementary controls), gap letters, right to audit, your side of shared responsibility.
  • After: management responses, tracking to the audit committee, verification, lessons into the strategy.
