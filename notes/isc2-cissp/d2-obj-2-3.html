2.3: Provisioning information and assets securely

Giving every asset an accountable owner and an accurate inventory record, managing it through its life, and issuing information and equipment to people in a known, protected state.

Overview

Objective 2.3

2.3 Provision information and assets securely. Sub-topics: information and asset ownership; asset inventory (tangible, intangible); asset management.

Inventories and the asset life cycle are introduced in CC 5.4; retirement and end of support are on the 2.5 page; access provisioning for people on the 5.5 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. A discovery scan last year found 1,100 devices and 60 SaaS services that no inventory listed.

Information and asset ownership

Objective 2.3Giving every asset a named, accountable owner who makes its key decisions.

Every asset needs a named owner, usually a business manager rather than IT, who is accountable for it: deciding its classification, approving who may use it and how, accepting its risks, funding its protection, and deciding when it is retired. NIST describes the information owner as the official with authority over specified information and "responsibility for establishing the controls for its generation, collection, processing, dissemination, and disposal."

  • Information owners own data sets (customer data: head of e-commerce; payroll: HR director).
  • System owners own systems and are responsible for their operation and security; a system may hold several owners' information.
  • Custodians (often IT) operate and protect assets on the owner's behalf; they do not decide classification or access.
  • Users are assigned assets and must use them according to policy.

Ownership must be recorded in the inventory and kept current when people change roles; an asset whose owner has left is effectively unowned. Unowned assets are where risk hides: no one approves access, accepts risk or decides to retire them.

Asset inventory

Objective 2.3Keeping an accurate inventory of tangible and intangible assets, found automatically where possible.

An asset is "an item of value to the achievement of organizational mission/business objectives" (CNSSI 4009). Inventories cover both kinds:

  • Tangible: hardware (servers, endpoints, network and OT devices, mobile devices), media, facilities and equipment.
  • Intangible: data, software and licenses, cloud and SaaS services, intellectual property, domain names and certificates, brand and reputation.

CSF 2.0 expects inventories of hardware (ID.AM-01), of "software, services, and systems" (ID.AM-02), of supplier services (ID.AM-04) and of "data and corresponding metadata" (ID.AM-07). SP 800-53 CM-8 requires a component inventory that "accurately reflects the system" and includes all components, updated during installation and removal (CM-8(1)), with CM-8(3) requiring organizations to "detect the presence of unauthorized hardware, software, and firmware components" and disable, isolate or report them. For US federal agencies, CISA's BOD 23-01 requires automated asset discovery every 7 days and vulnerability enumeration every 14 days.

A useful inventory record holds at least: identifier, type, owner, location (physical or cloud account and region), classification and criticality, software and version, support status and end-of-support date, and relationships to other assets. Automated discovery (network scanning, endpoint agents, cloud APIs, SaaS discovery through a CASB) keeps it true; a manually maintained spreadsheet is usually wrong within weeks.

You cannot protect what you do not know you have. Asset inventory is the foundation for patching, vulnerability management, incident response and recovery. When a question asks what to do first in building a security program or responding to a new vulnerability, knowing your assets is often the answer.

Asset management through the life cycle

Objective 2.3Managing assets from acquisition to disposal so the inventory and protection stay current.

CSF 2.0 expects "systems, hardware, software, services, and data" to be "managed throughout their life cycles" (ID.AM-08). The system life cycle runs through initiation, development and acquisition, implementation, operation and maintenance, and disposal (SP 800-37). Asset management keeps security attached at each stage:

  1. AcquireFrom approved suppliers and channels, with security requirements in the purchase; record the asset on arrival.
  2. DeployBuild to a secure baseline, enroll in management, assign an owner and user.
  3. OperatePatch, monitor, review access, update the inventory when assets move or change.
  4. MaintainControl repairs and maintenance (sanitize or supervise media that leaves for repair), renew support.
  5. RetireMigrate or archive data, sanitize, remove from the inventory, revoke credentials and licenses (objective 2.5).

Shadow IT (technology used without approval, often SaaS bought on a credit card) is an asset management problem: CSF 2.0 lists identifying "unofficial uses of technology to meet mission objectives (i.e., shadow IT)" as an example. The answer is to discover it, bring useful tools under management or replace them, and make the approved route easy. Redundant and forgotten systems also "unnecessarily increase the organization's attack surface" and should be retired.

Provisioning securely

Objective 2.3Issuing information and assets to the right people in a known, protected state.

  1. Request and approveThe owner or manager approves the need; the asset is assigned to a named user in the inventory.
  2. Build to baselineDevices are built from a hardened image, enrolled in management (encryption, endpoint protection, patching) and checked before issue.
  3. Grant access by roleAccess to information follows the classification and least privilege; no "copy the access of a colleague".
  4. Hand over with obligationsThe user acknowledges acceptable use and handling rules.
  5. Track and recoverThe asset is tracked while in use and recovered, sanitized and reissued or disposed of when the user leaves or the asset is retired.

Provisioning information works the same way: data sets are shared through approved repositories with the right permissions, rather than emailed copies; access to new data sets is granted by the owner; and cloud resources are created from templates that apply encryption, logging and network controls by default. At the retail group, a new store manager's laptop arrives encrypted, enrolled in device management and tagged to them in the inventory before their first day; access to store financial data is granted by role, and the laptop is collected and sanitized on the day they leave.

AI assets

Objective 2.3Inventorying and owning AI systems, models and their data like any other asset.

The NIST AI RMF expects "mechanisms are in place to inventory AI systems" (GOVERN 1.6) and safe decommissioning "in a manner that does not increase risks" (GOVERN 1.7); its generative AI profile adds that inventories should include underlying foundation models, their versions and access modes. AI providers retire models on their own schedules, so dependencies on specific models belong in the inventory with their end-of-support dates. Each AI system needs an owner, a classification for its data and outputs (objective 2.1), and approved provisioning: who may use it, with which data.

Keeping the inventory accurate

Objective 2.3Reconciling sources, tracking relationships and measuring how complete the inventory is.

No single source sees everything, so good inventories reconcile several:

  • Procurement and finance records: what was bought, from whom, and its support contract.
  • Network and endpoint discovery: what is actually connected and running, including devices no one bought through official channels.
  • Cloud provider APIs: accounts, subscriptions, resources and their tags, which change by the hour.
  • Identity and SaaS discovery: applications users sign into, found through the identity provider and a CASB.
  • Software and license management: installed software, versions and license entitlements, also needed for vulnerability management and audits.

Differences between sources are findings: a device on the network that procurement never bought, a paid license with no install, a cloud account with no owner. A configuration management database (CMDB) often holds the reconciled view as configuration items with their relationships (this application runs on these servers and depends on that database), which is what incident responders and recovery planners need.

Less obvious assets belong in the inventory too: TLS certificates and their expiry dates, domain names and DNS zones, API keys and service accounts, encryption keys, code repositories, and AI models in use. Expired certificates and forgotten domains cause outages and takeovers that an inventory would have prevented.

Measure the inventory like any other control:

  • Coverage: percentage of discovered assets that are in the inventory with an owner.
  • Accuracy: sample checks of location, owner and version against reality.
  • Timeliness: time from an asset appearing to being recorded, and from retirement to removal.
  • Support status: number of assets past or near end of support (objective 2.5).

The retail group reports inventory coverage monthly to its security steering committee; after the discovery scan that found 1,100 unlisted devices, coverage rose from 81% to 98% within a year, and every unknown device now triggers a ticket to the store or team where it was found.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying ownership and inventory reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the urgent vulnerability
Situation
A critical vulnerability is announced in a network appliance model. Nobody can say how many the company has or where.
Best response
Run discovery now to find affected devices and patch or isolate them; then fix the root cause with automated, continuous asset discovery feeding the inventory.
Why
Inventory is the foundation of vulnerability management; CM-8 and ID.AM-01 expect an accurate, current inventory.
Scenario 2: the orphaned database
Situation
A database of old promotions has no recorded owner since its manager left. It still holds customer email addresses.
Best response
Assign an owner (the manager's successor or the head of the function), who decides whether to keep it under the retention schedule, re-classify and protect it, or delete it securely.
Why
Every asset needs an accountable owner; unowned data is unmanaged risk, and keeping personal data without purpose may breach privacy law.
Scenario 3: the credit card SaaS
Situation
Marketing has been using an unapproved survey SaaS, bought on a corporate card, to collect customer feedback including email addresses.
Best response
Assess it like any supplier; either bring it under management (contract, security review, inventory, owner) or migrate the data to an approved tool and close the account.
Why
Shadow IT is an unmanaged asset and supplier; the goal is to manage it, not to punish the team for meeting a business need.
Scenario 4: the expired certificate
Situation
The online store went down for two hours because a TLS certificate expired. No one knew who owned it or when it was due.
Best response
Add certificates (with owners and expiry dates) to the inventory, automate renewal where possible, and alert owners well before expiry.
Why
Intangible assets such as certificates and domains need owners and inventory records like any other asset; availability failures are security failures.

Key takeaways

  • Ownership: every asset has a named, accountable owner (usually business) who classifies, approves access, accepts risk and decides retirement; custodians operate; keep ownership current.
  • Inventory: tangible and intangible assets; CSF ID.AM-01, -02, -04, -07; CM-8 accurate and complete, CM-8(3) detect unauthorized components; BOD 23-01 discovery every 7 days; automate discovery.
  • Asset management (ID.AM-08): acquire, deploy, operate, maintain, retire with security at each stage; find and manage shadow IT; retire redundant systems.
  • Provisioning: approve, build to baseline, grant by role, hand over with obligations, track and recover; provision data and cloud resources from secure defaults.
  • AI assets: inventory AI systems and their underlying models and versions (AI RMF GOVERN 1.6), owners, classification, safe decommissioning.
