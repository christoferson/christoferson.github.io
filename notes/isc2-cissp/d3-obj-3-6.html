Overview

Objectives 3.6 and 3.7

3.6 Select and determine cryptographic solutions. 3.7 Understand methods of cryptanalytic attacks.

The basics (symmetric and asymmetric encryption, hashing, salting and post-quantum algorithms) are in CC 5.1. This page goes up a level: how to choose and run cryptography in an organization, and how it is attacked. Hardware for keys (TPMs and HSMs) is on the 3.3–3.4 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It runs an internal certificate authority, encrypts card data with keys held in HSMs, and has started a post-quantum migration plan.

Cryptographic life cycle and methods

Objective 3.6Selecting algorithms that fit the need and replacing them before they become weak.

Algorithms have a life cycle too: they are approved, become legacy and are eventually disallowed as computing power and cryptanalysis improve. NIST SP 800-131A tracks these transitions. Recent examples: Triple DES encryption was disallowed after December 31, 2023; SHA-1 is being phased out by December 31, 2030; and NIST IR 8547 (final October 2026) says NIST "will deprecate quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography after 2030 and disallow its use under NIST standards and guidelines after 2035." Selecting an algorithm means matching it to the goal and the data's lifetime:

  • Symmetric (AES with 128, 192 or 256-bit keys; AES-GCM for authenticated encryption): fast, for bulk data, but the shared key must be distributed and kept secret. Symmetric algorithms are far less affected by quantum computers.
  • Asymmetric (RSA; FIPS 186-5 covers RSA, ECDSA and EdDSA signatures): a key pair, used for key exchange and digital signatures. RSA-2048 gives about 112 bits of security; RSA-3072 about 128.
  • Elliptic curve: asymmetric cryptography with much shorter keys for the same strength (P-256 is about 128 bits, like RSA-3072), suiting mobile and embedded devices. Like RSA, it is vulnerable to a future quantum computer.
  • Post-quantum: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, key establishment), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) for signatures, approved in August 2024. Hybrid key exchange (classical plus ML-KEM) is already used in TLS 1.3. Data that must stay secret for years is at risk from harvest now, decrypt later and should move first.
  • Hashing: SHA-2 or SHA-3 for integrity; HMAC when a key is needed; password hashing with a salt and a slow function.

Two practices make the life cycle manageable: a cryptographic inventory (what algorithms, keys and certificates are used where), and crypto agility, "the capabilities needed to replace and adapt cryptographic algorithms" without disrupting operations (NIST CSWP 39). The retail group's inventory found RSA-2048 in its payment terminals, VPN and internal CA; long-lived customer data was the first priority for post-quantum key exchange.

Key management practices

Objective 3.6Protecting keys from generation to destruction, because the security of encrypted data is only as good as the keys.

NIST SP 800-57 Part 1 Rev. 5 (2020) is the main key management guidance; Rev. 6 was released as a draft in December 2025, adding the post-quantum algorithms. Key practices:

  1. GenerateWith an approved random source, inside a validated module where possible.
  2. DistributeSecurely: wrapped under another key, through key agreement, or out of band.
  3. StoreIn an HSM, TPM or key management service, never hard-coded or alongside the data it protects.
  4. UseFor one purpose only (separate keys for encryption and for signing), with access limited to the systems and people that need it.
  5. RotateAt the end of the key's cryptoperiod: "the time span during which a specific key is authorized for use", or at once if compromise is suspected.
  6. Revoke, archive and destroyRevoke compromised keys, archive only keys needed to decrypt retained data, and destroy the rest so they cannot be recovered.

Controls on who can use and manage keys (key access management):

  • Split knowledge: "a cryptographic key is split into n key shares, each of which provides no knowledge of the key"; for example, two custodians each hold half of a master key component.
  • Dual control: two authorized people must act together for a sensitive key operation, so no one person can misuse it.
  • Key escrow: keeping a copy of a key (NIST: the private key behind an encryption certificate) with a trusted party so encrypted data can be recovered, for example when an employee leaves. It must itself be tightly protected.
  • Separation of duties between key custodians and the administrators of the systems that use the keys, with every key operation logged.

Rotation needs a plan for old data. Rotating a key does not re-encrypt what was encrypted under the old one. Keep old keys (archived, decrypt-only) until that data is re-encrypted or destroyed, or the data becomes unreadable.

PKI and key distribution

Objective 3.6Using public key infrastructure to bind public keys to identities and distribute them at scale.

Symmetric cryptography has a key distribution problem: n parties need n(n-1)/2 shared keys. Public key cryptography solves it, but only if you can trust that a public key belongs to who it claims to. PKI is "a framework that is established to issue, maintain and revoke public key certificates" (NIST). Its parts:

  • Certificate authority (CA): signs certificates. A root CA is kept offline and signs intermediate (issuing) CAs, which sign end-entity certificates, forming a chain of trust back to a root the relying party already trusts.
  • Registration authority (RA): verifies the identity of whoever requests a certificate, before the CA issues it.
  • Revocation: a certificate revocation list (CRL) is "a list of revoked public key certificates created and digitally signed by a certification authority"; OCSP is "an online protocol used to determine the status of a public key certificate". Let's Encrypt, a large public CA, stopped running OCSP in August 2025 and relies on CRLs.
  • Certificate Transparency (RFC 9162): public logs of issued TLS certificates, so wrongly issued certificates can be spotted.

Certificate lifetimes are shrinking: under a CA/Browser Forum ballot, public TLS certificates can be valid for at most 200 days from March 15, 2026, 100 days from March 15, 2027 and 47 days from March 15, 2029. That makes automated issuance and renewal essential.

Quantum key distribution (QKD) uses quantum physics to detect eavesdropping when sharing keys. It needs special hardware and dedicated links and still relies on classical authentication; NSA has stated that it does not recommend QKD for national security systems and prefers post-quantum algorithms.

Digital signatures and certificates

Objective 3.6Using signatures and certificates for integrity, origin authentication and non-repudiation.

A digital signature is the result of a cryptographic transformation that provides "origin authentication, data integrity and signatory non-repudiation" (FIPS 186-5). The sender hashes the message and signs the hash with their private key; anyone verifies it with the sender's public key. Any change to the message breaks the signature (integrity), and because only the signer holds the private key, the signer cannot credibly deny signing it (non-repudiation, which a third party can verify).

A digital certificate (X.509) binds a public key to a subject's identity, signed by a CA. It includes the subject, the public key, the issuer, a serial number, a validity period, permitted key uses and where to check revocation.

Which key does what. Encrypt to someone with their public key (only their private key decrypts: confidentiality). Sign with your private key (anyone verifies with your public key: integrity, authentication, non-repudiation). A MAC or HMAC gives integrity and authentication but not non-repudiation, because both parties share the key.

The retail group signs its software releases and its store-terminal firmware with keys held in an HSM, so terminals refuse unsigned updates.

Cryptanalytic and implementation attacks

Objective 3.7Telling attacks on the mathematics apart from attacks on how cryptography is built and used.

Classic cryptanalytic attacks are defined by what the attacker has:

AttackAttacker hasDefense
Brute forceCiphertext or a hash, and time: tries every key or passwordLong keys; slow, salted password hashing; lockouts
Ciphertext onlyOnly ciphertextModern algorithms resist it; it is the weakest attack position
Known plaintextSome plaintext and its matching ciphertextAlgorithms resistant to key recovery from pairs
Chosen plaintextCan get plaintext of their choice encryptedRandomized encryption (proper IVs and nonces)
Chosen ciphertextCan get ciphertext of their choice decrypted, or learn whether it decryptsAuthenticated encryption; uniform error messages
Frequency analysisCiphertext from a simple substitution cipher: counts letter patternsAny modern cipher; works only on classical ciphers

Most real breaks are implementation attacks: they target how the algorithm is built or run rather than its mathematics.

  • Side-channel attacks: "enabled by the leakage of information from a physical cryptosystem" (NIST), such as power use, electromagnetic emissions or sound.
  • Timing attacks: a side channel in which differences in how long operations take reveal key bits or whether a guess was partly right. Defense: constant-time code.
  • Fault injection: deliberately causing errors (voltage or clock glitches, lasers, heat) so the device skips a check or produces faulty output that reveals the key. Defenses: tamper-responsive hardware and checking results before releasing them.
  • Others: weak random numbers, reused nonces, padding oracles, and keys left in memory or code.

Attacks on authentication and systems

Objective 3.7Recognizing attacks that steal or abuse credentials and keys, or hold data hostage, and how to stop them.

  • Adversary-in-the-middle (MITRE ATT&CK T1557): the attacker positions themselves between two parties to read, change or replay traffic, for example through ARP poisoning, DHCP spoofing, name-resolution poisoning or an evil-twin Wi-Fi access point. AitM phishing kits proxy a real login page and steal session cookies, which defeats one-time codes. Defenses: TLS with certificate validation, mutual authentication, phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2), and switch protections.
  • Pass the hash (T1550.002): attackers "pass the hash" using stolen password hashes to move laterally, authenticating without ever knowing the password (a weakness of NTLM). Defenses: limit privileged logons to hardened admin workstations, unique local admin passwords, credential protection features, and moving off NTLM.
  • Kerberos exploitation (T1558): Kerberoasting requests service tickets and cracks them offline to recover service account passwords (weakest with RC4 encryption); a golden ticket is forged with the domain's ticket-signing (KRBTGT) key and grants access anywhere; a silver ticket is forged with one service's key. Defenses: long random service account passwords or managed service accounts, AES only, protecting domain controllers, and resetting the KRBTGT key twice after a compromise.
  • Ransomware: encrypts (and often steals) data and demands payment. Attackers use cryptography as the weapon, and go after backups first. Defenses: offline, immutable and tested backups, segmentation, EDR, patching and MFA (CC 5.3).
  • Zero-day attacks: "an attack that exploits a previously unknown hardware, firmware, or software vulnerability" (CNSSI 4009). No patch exists, so defense in depth, least privilege, behavior-based detection and fast patching once a fix arrives matter. CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog is meant as "an input to" patch prioritization.
  • AI threats: adversarial AI (inputs crafted to make a model misclassify or misbehave, including prompt injection), model and data poisoning (NIST AI 100-2: an adversary controls some training data, or the model itself, which can hit integrity or availability) and deepfakes (synthetic voice or video used to impersonate executives). Defenses: data provenance and validation, adversarial testing, monitoring model behavior, and call-back verification for payment or access requests, however convincing the voice.

Spot the root cause. Pass the hash and golden tickets are not cryptographic breaks: the attacker steals or forges a credential. The fixes are credential hygiene and protecting the systems that hold the secrets, not stronger algorithms.

Key takeaways

  • Life cycle: algorithms go from approved to disallowed (3DES after 2023, SHA-1 by 2030, quantum-vulnerable public-key deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035). Keep a crypto inventory and build crypto agility.
  • Methods: symmetric (AES) for bulk data, asymmetric and ECC for key exchange and signatures, PQC (FIPS 203/204/205) for the future, hashing for integrity.
  • Keys (SP 800-57): generate, distribute, store in hardware, use for one purpose, rotate by cryptoperiod, revoke, archive, destroy. Split knowledge, dual control, escrow, separation of duties.
  • PKI: CA, RA, root and intermediate chain, CRL and OCSP, Certificate Transparency; TLS certificate lifetimes falling to 47 days by 2029. NSA prefers PQC to QKD.
  • Signatures: sign with the private key, verify with the public key: integrity, origin authentication, non-repudiation. MACs give no non-repudiation.
  • Attacks: brute force, ciphertext only, known plaintext, chosen plaintext and ciphertext, frequency analysis; implementation attacks (side-channel, timing, fault injection) are more common; AitM, pass the hash, Kerberos tickets, ransomware, zero-days and AI threats (adversarial inputs, poisoning, deepfakes).
