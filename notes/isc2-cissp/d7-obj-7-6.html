Overview

Objective 7.6

7.6 Conduct incident management. Sub-topics: detection; response; mitigation; reporting; recovery; remediation; lessons learned.

The incident response basics (life cycle, plans, playbooks, roles) are in CC 5.3; logging, SIEM and SOAR on the 7.2 page; evidence handling on the 7.1 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. On a Saturday morning, EDR alerts that a store manager's laptop is encrypting files on a shared drive.

The incident management process

Objective 7.6Moving an incident through detection, response, mitigation, recovery, remediation and lessons learned.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (April 2025) maps incident response onto CSF 2.0: preparation sits in Govern, Identify and Protect; the response itself is Detect, Respond and Recover; and improvement (ID.IM) runs throughout, because "lessons learned during incident response should often be shared as soon as they are identified, not delayed until after recovery concludes." The outline's steps:

  1. DetectionAlerts, user reports and threat intelligence are triaged; analysts confirm whether adverse events amount to an incident, rate its severity and declare it, which starts the plan and names an incident lead.
  2. ResponseThe incident team coordinates technical work, decisions, legal advice and communications; evidence is preserved from the start.
  3. MitigationContainment ("preventing the expansion of an incident"): isolating hosts, disabling accounts, blocking addresses, segmenting networks. Short-term containment buys time; long-term containment keeps the business running while eradication is prepared.
  4. ReportingInternal escalation and external notifications on legal and contractual deadlines (below).
  5. Recovery"Restoring systems from clean backups, rebuilding systems", verifying their integrity, and returning them to production in stages while monitoring closely.
  6. RemediationEradicate the attacker ("eliminate persistence mechanisms and entry points") and fix the root cause before production use, so the same route cannot be used again.
  7. Lessons learnedAn after-action report and review: what happened, what worked, what did not, and owned improvement actions for plans, controls and training.

Contain before you eradicate, and do not tip off. Removing malware from one host while the attacker still holds other footholds tells them they are discovered and they may react destructively. CISA's playbooks say eradication should follow only once persistence is accounted for and evidence collected. Leadership decides high-impact actions such as shutting down critical services.

At the retail group, EDR isolated the laptop automatically; the SOC disabled the manager's account, found the same tool on two other hosts through EDR hunting, isolated them, and restored the shared drive from immutable backups. The root cause, a phishing email that bypassed MFA through a fake login page, led to phishing-resistant MFA for all store managers.

Reporting during and after an incident

Objective 7.6Notifying the right people on time, through designated channels, with accurate information.

  • Internal: severity-based escalation to leadership, legal, privacy, communications and affected business owners; status updates on a fixed rhythm.
  • Regulators and authorities: deadlines start from awareness or determination, such as 72 hours for GDPR personal data breaches to the supervisory authority, and four business days after determining materiality for a US public company's SEC disclosure (objective 1.4). Sector rules (payment brands, health, finance) add their own.
  • Others: affected individuals and customers, insurers (who may require notice before you engage responders), law enforcement, partners, and information-sharing groups.

SP 800-61 Rev. 3 says stakeholders are notified "per plan" by designated individuals, and that incident records "can contain sensitive information" and are still evidence, so access to them is restricted and they are retained under evidence rules.

Preparing for incidents

Objective 7.6Having the plan, people, tools and agreements ready before the first alert.

  • Policy and plan: the incident response policy defines what counts as an incident, authority (who may isolate systems, shut down services, call law enforcement) and reporting duties; the plan defines the process, roles and communications.
  • Team and roles: an incident lead, technical responders, and named contacts in legal, privacy, HR, communications, business units and suppliers, with deputies for each.
  • Playbooks: step-by-step guides for common incident types (ransomware, account compromise, business email compromise, data leak, DDoS, insider), with decision points and who approves them.
  • Tools and access: EDR, log search, forensic kits, clean admin workstations and break-glass accounts that work even when the directory is compromised.
  • Outside help on retainer: an incident response firm, legal counsel, and a crisis communications adviser agreed in advance, along with insurer requirements.
  • Exercises: tabletop exercises for leadership and technical drills for responders, at least yearly and after major changes, with lessons fed back into the plan.

Preparation is where most of the difference between a contained incident and a crisis is made: the retail group's Saturday response worked because EDR could isolate laptops automatically and the on-call lead had authority to disable accounts without waiting for Monday.

Severity and prioritization

Objective 7.6Deciding how serious an incident is, which sets who is involved and how fast.

Severity scales are defined in the plan so that everyone rates incidents the same way. Common factors:

  • Functional impact: how much the incident disrupts business services (none, some users, a critical service, the whole organization).
  • Information impact: whether sensitive data was accessed, changed or taken (personal data, card data, trade secrets), which also drives notification duties.
  • Recoverability: whether recovery is possible with existing resources, needs outside help, or is not possible (data destroyed).
  • Scope and spread: number of systems, sites or accounts, and whether the attacker is still active.

Each severity level maps to actions: who must be told, how quickly, who leads, and whether the crisis management team convenes. Severity is reassessed as facts emerge; an incident that starts as one infected laptop can become critical when the same tool is found on a domain controller. Incidents involving possible breaches of regulated data are flagged early so legal can track notification clocks.

Responding to ransomware

Objective 7.6Applying the process to the incident type that tests it hardest.

  1. IsolateDisconnect affected systems from the network (but keep them powered for evidence where possible); if many systems are affected, isolate whole segments.
  2. ScopeIdentify affected systems, the ransomware variant, and how the attacker got in and moved; look for data theft, since many groups steal data before encrypting it.
  3. Protect recoveryCheck that backups are intact and isolated before the attacker can reach them; secure identity systems and reset privileged credentials, including service accounts.
  4. ReportNotify leadership, legal, insurers and the authorities required; CISA and the FBI ask US victims to report.
  5. Eradicate and recoverRemove persistence, rebuild from clean images and backups in priority order (identity first), and monitor closely for the attacker's return.

Paying a ransom is a business and legal decision, not a technical one. US authorities discourage payment because it funds criminals and does not guarantee recovery, and the US Treasury (OFAC) has warned that paying sanctioned parties can breach sanctions law. Decisions involve leadership, counsel, insurers and law enforcement, and are documented.

Communicating during an incident

Objective 7.6Keeping information accurate, timely and controlled while the incident is live.

  • Out-of-band channels: assume the attacker can read corporate email and chat; use pre-arranged separate channels and phone bridges for the response team.
  • Need-to-know: details of the investigation go only to those working on it, to avoid tipping off an insider or attacker and to protect evidence.
  • One voice: external statements come only from designated spokespeople, approved by legal and communications; staff are told not to comment and where to send questions.
  • Prepared messages: holding statements and customer notices drafted in advance for common incident types, then adapted to the facts.
  • Accuracy over speed: share what is known and what is being done, avoid speculation about causes or numbers affected, and correct earlier statements when facts change.
  • Record decisions: an incident log of what was decided, when, by whom and why, which supports the lessons learned review and any later legal scrutiny.

Incident metrics

Objective 7.6Measuring how well incidents are detected and handled.

  • Time to detect, contain and recover, measured from first malicious activity where it can be determined.
  • Dwell time: how long attackers were present before detection.
  • Detection source: the share found by internal tools versus by users, customers or outside parties.
  • Volume and severity by incident type, showing trends.
  • Lessons learned closure: the share of improvement actions completed on time.
  • Repeat incidents with the same root cause.

Leadership should see a few of these over time, tied to risk, rather than raw alert counts.

Incidents involving suppliers

Objective 7.6Handling incidents that start at, or spread through, a supplier.

  • Contacts in advance: named security contacts at critical suppliers, and their notification duties in contracts.
  • Cut or limit connections: disable integrations, rotate shared credentials and API keys, and restrict supplier remote access while the scope is unclear.
  • Get facts: ask for indicators of compromise, affected data and timelines, and verify rather than rely only on statements.
  • Your own duties: a supplier breach of your data can still trigger your notification obligations.
  • Afterwards: reassess the supplier and update contracts or architecture.

The NIST four-phase life cycle and incident terms

Objective 7.6The earlier model many study materials use, and the definitions behind declaring an incident.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 (2012), replaced by Rev. 3 in 2025 but still widely taught, used four phases:

  1. PreparationPolicy, team, tools, training and prevention.
  2. Detection and analysisRecognize, validate, prioritize and document incidents.
  3. Containment, eradication and recoveryLimit the damage, remove the cause, restore systems.
  4. Post-incident activityLessons learned, evidence retention and improvement.

The ISC2 outline's steps fit inside these: detection belongs to detection and analysis; response, mitigation, recovery and remediation to containment, eradication and recovery; reporting runs throughout; lessons learned is post-incident activity.

  • Event: any observable occurrence in a system or network.
  • Adverse event: an event with negative consequences, such as a crash or unauthorized use of privileges.
  • Incident: a violation or imminent threat of violation of security policies, acceptable use policies or standard security practices.
  • Breach: an incident in which data, often personal data, is confirmed to have been disclosed to or accessed by unauthorized parties, which usually triggers legal notification duties.

A computer security incident response team (CSIRT) handles incidents; the plan names who may declare an incident and its severity.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the eager administrator
Situation
An administrator finds malware on one server and wants to remove it immediately; the SOC suspects the attacker is on other systems.
Best response
Contain and scope first (isolate, hunt across the estate, collect evidence), then eradicate everywhere at once.
Why
Removing one foothold early tips off the attacker, who may react destructively.
Scenario 2: the notification clock
Situation
On day two of an incident, investigators confirm that customer personal data was taken from an EU system.
Best response
Inform legal and the privacy team at once so they can assess and notify the supervisory authority within 72 hours of awareness where required.
Why
Reporting deadlines start from awareness; legal owns notification decisions.
Scenario 3: email is compromised
Situation
During a business email compromise, the response team is coordinating through company email.
Best response
Move response coordination to pre-arranged out-of-band channels.
Why
Assume the attacker can read normal channels.
Scenario 4: no lessons learned
Situation
After recovery, the team closes the incident without a review because everyone is busy.
Best response
Hold a lessons learned review with owned improvement actions, and track them to completion.
Why
Improvement is part of incident management; without it the same incident repeats.
Scenario 5: found by a customer
Situation
Most incidents last year were first reported by customers or partners.
Best response
Improve detection coverage and monitoring, and track detection source as a key metric.
Why
Detection source shows whether internal monitoring is working.

Key takeaways

  • Incident management (SP 800-61 Rev. 3, CSF Detect, Respond, Recover): detect and declare, respond, mitigate (contain), report, recover from clean sources, remediate root causes, lessons learned throughout.
  • Contain before eradicating, preserve evidence, avoid tipping off; leadership approves high-impact actions.
  • Reporting: internal escalation; regulators on deadlines (GDPR 72 hours, SEC four business days after materiality); customers, insurers, law enforcement; records are evidence.
  • Preparation and severity: policy with authority, team and deputies, playbooks, tools that work when the directory is down, retainers, exercises; severity by functional and information impact, recoverability and scope.
  • Ransomware: isolate, scope (including data theft), protect backups and identity, report, rebuild identity first; ransom payment is a leadership and legal decision. Communications: out-of-band, need-to-know, one voice, decision log.
