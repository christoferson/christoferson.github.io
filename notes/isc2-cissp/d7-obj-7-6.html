Overview

Objectives 7.6 and 7.7

7.6 Conduct incident management. 7.7 Operate and maintain detection and preventative measures.

The incident response basics (life cycle, plans, playbooks, roles) are in CC 5.3; firewall types and endpoint controls on the 4.2 page; logging, SIEM and SOAR on the 7.1–7.2 page. This page covers running an incident and operating the defenses. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. On a Saturday morning, EDR alerts that a store manager's laptop is encrypting files on a shared drive.

The incident management process

Objective 7.6Moving an incident through detection, response, mitigation, recovery, remediation and lessons learned.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (April 2025) maps incident response onto CSF 2.0: preparation sits in Govern, Identify and Protect; the response itself is Detect, Respond and Recover; and improvement (ID.IM) runs throughout, because "lessons learned during incident response should often be shared as soon as they are identified, not delayed until after recovery concludes." The outline's steps:

  1. DetectionAlerts, user reports and threat intelligence are triaged; analysts confirm whether adverse events amount to an incident, rate its severity and declare it, which starts the plan and names an incident lead.
  2. ResponseThe incident team coordinates technical work, decisions, legal advice and communications; evidence is preserved from the start.
  3. MitigationContainment ("preventing the expansion of an incident"): isolating hosts, disabling accounts, blocking addresses, segmenting networks. Short-term containment buys time; long-term containment keeps the business running while eradication is prepared.
  4. ReportingInternal escalation and external notifications on legal and contractual deadlines (below).
  5. Recovery"Restoring systems from clean backups, rebuilding systems", verifying their integrity, and returning them to production in stages while monitoring closely.
  6. RemediationEradicate the attacker ("eliminate persistence mechanisms and entry points") and fix the root cause before production use, so the same route cannot be used again.
  7. Lessons learnedAn after-action report and review: what happened, what worked, what did not, and owned improvement actions for plans, controls and training.

Contain before you eradicate, and do not tip off. Removing malware from one host while the attacker still holds other footholds tells them they are discovered and they may react destructively. CISA's playbooks say eradication should follow only once persistence is accounted for and evidence collected. Leadership decides high-impact actions such as shutting down critical services.

At the retail group, EDR isolated the laptop automatically; the SOC disabled the manager's account, found the same tool on two other hosts through EDR hunting, isolated them, and restored the shared drive from immutable backups. The root cause, a phishing email that bypassed MFA through a fake login page, led to phishing-resistant MFA for all store managers.

Reporting during and after an incident

Objective 7.6Notifying the right people on time, through designated channels, with accurate information.

  • Internal: severity-based escalation to leadership, legal, privacy, communications and affected business owners; status updates on a fixed rhythm.
  • Regulators and authorities: deadlines start from awareness or determination, such as 72 hours for GDPR personal data breaches to the supervisory authority, and four business days after determining materiality for a US public company's SEC disclosure (objective 1.4). Sector rules (payment brands, health, finance) add their own.
  • Others: affected individuals and customers, insurers (who may require notice before you engage responders), law enforcement, partners, and information-sharing groups.

SP 800-61 Rev. 3 says stakeholders are notified "per plan" by designated individuals, and that incident records "can contain sensitive information" and are still evidence, so access to them is restricted and they are retained under evidence rules.

Network defenses: firewalls, IDS and IPS, lists

Objective 7.7Keeping preventive and detective network controls effective through rule management, tuning and updates.

  • Network firewalls: stateful packet filtering between zones, deny by default. Operating them means reviewing rules regularly, removing unused and overly broad rules, documenting the owner and reason for each, and change control for every rule change.
  • Next-generation firewalls (NGFW): add application awareness, user identity, intrusion prevention, TLS inspection and threat intelligence feeds to the same device.
  • Web application firewalls (WAF): filter HTTP in front of web applications and APIs against injection, cross-site scripting and bots; a stopgap ("virtual patch") while code is fixed, not a substitute for fixing it.
  • IDS and IPS: keep signatures current, tune to the environment, place sensors where they see the important traffic (including east-west), and decide carefully what an inline IPS may block, since a false positive blocks legitimate business.
  • Allowlists and denylists: a denylist blocks known-bad items (addresses, domains, file hashes) and lets everything else through; an allowlist permits only approved items and blocks the rest. Allowlists are stronger but need maintenance; denylists are easy but miss anything new.

Third-party services, sandboxing and deception

Objective 7.7Using outside services, safe detonation and decoys to extend what the organization can detect and withstand.

  • DDoS scrubbing services: traffic is routed (by DNS or BGP) through a provider's large network, which filters attack traffic and forwards clean traffic. Arrange it before an attack, test failover, and make sure origin servers accept traffic only from the provider.
  • Managed security service providers (MSSPs) and managed detection and response: outsourced monitoring and response, often around the clock. The contract must define what they monitor, response times, what actions they may take on your systems, escalation, and access to your logs; accountability stays with the organization.
  • Sandboxing: running suspicious files or links in an isolated environment to observe their behavior before delivery, as in email and web gateways. Some malware detects sandboxes and waits, so sandboxing is one layer, not a guarantee.
  • Honeypots and honeynets: decoy systems (or whole networks) with no legitimate use, so any interaction is suspicious. They give early warning and intelligence about attackers' methods. Keep them isolated so they cannot become a launch point, and get legal advice: using them to detect is fine; luring someone into committing a crime they would not otherwise commit (entrapment) is not. Honeytokens (fake credentials or records) apply the same idea to data.

Endpoint protection and AI-based tools

Objective 7.7Operating endpoint defenses and understanding what machine learning tools can and cannot do.

  • Anti-malware: signature and behavior-based scanning; keep engines and definitions updated and alert when protection is disabled.
  • EDR: continuous recording of endpoint activity, behavioral detection, remote investigation and isolation. Its value depends on coverage (every endpoint and server, monitored) and on someone responding to its alerts. CISA's red team assessments include cases where EDR blocked the initial phishing attempt.
  • XDR: correlates endpoint, network, email, identity and cloud telemetry to see whole attack chains and respond across them.
  • ML and AI-based tools: anomaly detection, alert triage and summarization, phishing classification, and assistants that help analysts query data. They find patterns at scale but produce false positives and negatives, can be evaded by adversarial inputs (NIST AI 100-2), and need human review of consequential actions. Treat their outputs as evidence to check, protect their data and models, and measure their accuracy like any other control.

Preventive and detective together. Firewalls, IPS, allowlists and anti-malware try to stop attacks; IDS, EDR, honeypots and SIEM detect what gets through. A strong answer pairs them, because prevention alone eventually fails silently.

Key takeaways

  • Incident management (SP 800-61 Rev. 3, CSF Detect, Respond, Recover): detect and declare, respond, mitigate (contain), report, recover from clean sources, remediate root causes, lessons learned throughout.
  • Contain before eradicating, preserve evidence, avoid tipping off; leadership approves high-impact actions.
  • Reporting: internal escalation; regulators on deadlines (GDPR 72 hours, SEC four business days after materiality); customers, insurers, law enforcement; designated spokespeople; records are evidence.
  • Network defenses: firewall rule hygiene, NGFW, WAF as virtual patching, tuned IDS and IPS; allowlists stronger than denylists.
  • Services and deception: DDoS scrubbing arranged in advance; MSSP contracts define actions and keep accountability; sandboxing is one layer; honeypots, honeynets and honeytokens detect and inform (no entrapment).
  • Endpoints and AI: anti-malware, EDR, XDR with full coverage and response; ML tools help at scale but are fallible and attackable; pair prevention with detection.
