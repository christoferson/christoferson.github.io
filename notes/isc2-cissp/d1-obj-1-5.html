1.5: Investigation types

What changes when an investigation is administrative, criminal, civil or regulatory: who runs it, what standard of proof applies, who must be involved, and how evidence has to be preserved and presented so the outcome holds up.

Overview

Objective 1.5

1.5 Understand requirements for investigation types (i.e., administrative, criminal, civil, regulatory).

The 2027 outline lists four types; the 2024 outline also listed "industry standards", now covered here under regulatory and industry investigations. The hands-on side (evidence collection, forensics and chain of custody) is on the 7.1 page; the laws themselves are on the 1.4 page. The official outline is on isc2.org. This page is general information, not legal advice.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services.

The four investigation types

Objective 1.5Matching the purpose, leadership and standard of proof to each type of investigation.

TypePurpose and who leadsStandard of proof
AdministrativeInternal: policy violations or operational problems; led by the organization (HR, security, management)Set by internal policy and employment law; usually the lowest bar
CriminalBreaking criminal law; led by law enforcement and prosecutors; can lead to prisonBeyond a reasonable doubt, which "requires more certainty than any other burden of proof in law"
CivilDisputes between parties, such as breach of contract or IP infringement; led by the parties and their lawyers; leads to damages or injunctionsPreponderance of the evidence: "more likely true than not"
RegulatoryBreaches of regulations; led by a regulator such as the FTC, SEC or a data protection authority; leads to fines, orders or auditsDepends on the regulator and the law

The type decides almost everything that follows: who has authority to investigate, what methods are allowed, whether the organization controls the process, how much proof is needed, and how evidence must be handled. Because the type can change as facts emerge, the safe default is to collect and preserve evidence from the start as if it may go to court.

Administrative investigations

Objective 1.5Running internal investigations of policy violations and operational problems fairly and lawfully.

Administrative investigations are internal. They look at policy violations (misuse of systems, breaches of the acceptable use policy, harassment through company systems) and operational problems (an outage's root cause, a failed control). The organization controls the scope and pace, and the outcome is an internal decision: training, discipline, dismissal, or a process change.

  • Authority: the investigator needs written authorization from management, usually HR or legal, with a defined scope.
  • Monitoring and privacy: access to an employee's email, files or device must be allowed by policy, consistent with what staff were told, and lawful where they work; EU employees in particular have strong privacy rights. Involve HR and legal before looking.
  • Fairness: the employee's side is heard, conclusions follow the evidence, and findings are kept confidential to those who need them.
  • Operational investigations into failures focus on root cause and improvement rather than blame, as in a post-incident review.

Do not let the investigator act alone. A security analyst who reads a colleague's email "to check" without authorization may break the law and taint any later case. Authorization, scope and involvement of HR and legal come first.

Criminal investigations

Objective 1.5Knowing when and how to involve law enforcement, and what changes once they are involved.

Criminal investigations concern crimes such as unauthorized access, fraud, extortion, theft of trade secrets or child abuse material. Law enforcement and prosecutors lead; the organization is a victim and a witness, not the decision-maker. Consequences can include prison, so the standard of proof is the highest: "beyond a reasonable doubt", leaving jurors "firmly convinced".

  • Deciding to involve police: a management and legal decision, weighing the seriousness of the crime, legal duties to report, the chance of recovery or deterrence, publicity, and the loss of control over the timeline. Some crimes (such as child abuse material) must be reported.
  • Legal process: authorities obtain evidence through warrants, subpoenas and court orders; the organization should comply through legal counsel, not informally.
  • Acting as an agent of the state: if the organization starts collecting evidence at police direction, constitutional protections (in the US, the Fourth Amendment on searches) can apply to what it does. Let counsel manage the boundary.
  • Entrapment: inducing someone to commit a crime they would not otherwise commit is not allowed; observing a crime, for example through a honeypot, is not entrapment (objective 7.7).

Evidence must survive a defense lawyer's challenge, so chain of custody, forensic soundness and documentation matter most here (below).

Civil investigations and eDiscovery

Objective 1.5Supporting litigation between parties, and preserving and producing electronic information when required.

Civil cases are disputes between parties: breach of contract, negligence after a breach, IP infringement, trade secret theft, or employment claims. The parties and their lawyers drive them; the remedy is money or a court order. The standard of proof is the "preponderance of the evidence": "more likely true than not".

Civil cases bring eDiscovery, the exchange of electronically stored information (ESI) between parties:

  • Legal hold: once litigation is reasonably anticipated, a notice from counsel suspends routine deletion of relevant data: email, files, logs, chat, backups. Security and IT must apply it technically (holds in email and storage, pausing log rotation where needed).
  • Consequences of loss: under US Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e), if ESI that should have been preserved is lost because reasonable steps were not taken and it cannot be restored, the court may order measures "no greater than necessary to cure the prejudice"; only with "intent to deprive" may it presume the lost data was unfavorable, instruct the jury so, or dismiss the case. The rule notes reasonable steps do not "call for perfection".
  • The process: the Electronic Discovery Reference Model (EDRM 2.0, September 2026) describes identification, preservation, collection, processing, review, production, presentation and, new in 2.0, disposition, with analysis running throughout. Preservation means "safeguarding relevant ESI and documents against alteration or destruction."

A legal hold overrides the retention schedule. Routine deletion that destroys data under a hold can lead to sanctions even if the schedule says to delete it. Retention and records systems must be able to suspend deletion for specific people, systems and date ranges.

Regulatory and industry investigations

Objective 1.5Cooperating with regulators and industry bodies that investigate compliance after an incident or complaint.

Regulatory investigations are carried out by government agencies under the laws they enforce: data protection authorities under GDPR, the FTC on unfair or deceptive security practices, the SEC on disclosure, health regulators under HIPAA. They can start from a breach notification, a complaint, or the regulator's own review. The regulator sets the process and may demand documents, interviews and audits; the outcome can be fines, orders to improve, or mandated audits for years. The standard of proof depends on the law and the agency.

  • Cooperate through counsel: respond accurately and on time; misleading a regulator is often worse than the original failing.
  • Show due care and diligence: risk assessments, policies, training records, incident timelines and decisions are the evidence regulators ask for (objective 1.3).
  • Industry investigations: some investigations come from contracts rather than law. After a suspected card data breach, the card brands can require a forensic investigation by an approved PCI Forensic Investigator, whose findings can lead to fines and conditions on the merchant.

At the retail group, after a card data incident the bank required a PCI forensic investigation, while the EU data protection authority opened its own inquiry following the GDPR notification; the same preserved evidence and incident timeline served both.

Evidence that holds up

Objective 1.5Collecting and keeping evidence so it is admissible and persuasive in any type of investigation.

For evidence to be useful, it must be relevant (it relates to the facts in question), authentic (it is what it claims to be), reliable (collected and kept in a way that can be trusted) and legally obtained. In US courts, key rules for digital evidence:

  • Authentication (Federal Rule of Evidence 901): the party offering evidence must produce "evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is", for example a witness with knowledge, distinctive characteristics, or evidence that a process or system produces an accurate result.
  • Best evidence rule (FRE 1002): "an original writing, recording, or photograph is required in order to prove its content", unless another rule allows a copy; for digital data, an accurate, verified copy such as a hashed forensic image usually counts as the original.
  • Business records (FRE 803(6)): records made at or near the time by someone with knowledge, kept in a regular business activity as a regular practice, can be admitted despite the hearsay rule. Routine logs qualify, which is a reason to keep logging consistent and documented.

Evidence is commonly classified as direct (proves a fact by itself, such as a witness who saw the act), circumstantial (implies a fact, such as login records at the time), documentary (records and logs), real or physical (a seized laptop), and hearsay (second-hand statements, generally excluded unless an exception applies).

Practices that keep evidence strong, detailed on the 7.1 page: preserve before analyzing, follow the order of volatility, hash and work on copies, keep a chain of custody that records every handler, time and reason, and document every step at the time.

When a case changes type

Objective 1.5Recognizing that one incident can become several investigations, and preparing for that from the start.

Many cases start small and grow. An administrative investigation into a policy breach can uncover a crime; a breach can lead at once to a regulatory inquiry, civil claims from customers and partners, and a criminal investigation of the attackers. The parallel investigations have different owners, timelines and evidence needs, and what the organization says in one can be used in another.

  1. Start as if it will go to courtPreserve evidence, keep chain of custody and record actions from the first day.
  2. Bring in counsel earlyCounsel decides on legal holds, privilege, law enforcement involvement and notifications.
  3. Escalate by criteriaThe incident response plan defines when to notify legal, HR, regulators and police.
  4. CoordinateOne evidence store and one timeline serve every investigation; one spokesperson speaks externally.

Example at the retail group. A manager suspected of copying customer lists starts as an administrative case run by HR and security. When the copies are found at a competitor, counsel issues a legal hold; the evidence already preserved with hashes and a chain of custody supports a civil claim for trade secret theft; the data protection authority is notified because customer data left the company; and the police are informed because the theft may also be a crime.

Scenario check

PracticeChoosing the right next step for an investigation's type and stage.

Scenario 1: the suspicious downloads
Situation
DLP shows an engineer downloading design files at 2 a.m. the week before resigning. Their manager wants you to look through the engineer's personal webmail on the company laptop.
Best response
Get authorization from HR and legal, preserve the laptop and logs forensically, and examine only what policy and law allow; do not access the personal webmail account.
Why
It starts as an administrative case that may become civil or criminal; unauthorized access to a personal account could be illegal and would taint the evidence.
Scenario 2: the routine cleanup
Situation
A customer has threatened to sue over a failed delivery system. The nightly job that deletes 90-day-old order logs is about to run.
Best response
Alert legal counsel, who issues a legal hold, and suspend deletion of the relevant logs and records.
Why
Once litigation is reasonably anticipated, relevant ESI must be preserved; losing it can bring sanctions under FRCP 37(e) despite the retention schedule.
Scenario 3: the ransomware gang
Situation
Attackers encrypted file servers and stole HR data. The board asks which standard of proof applies if the attackers are ever prosecuted, and who leads.
Best answer
Law enforcement and prosecutors lead a criminal case, which needs proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The organization supports it with preserved evidence, while separately handling notifications and any regulatory inquiry.
Why
Criminal cases have the highest standard and are led by the state; regulatory and civil consequences for the organization run in parallel.

Key takeaways

  • Four types: administrative (internal, policy and operations), criminal (state-led, beyond a reasonable doubt), civil (parties, preponderance of the evidence), regulatory (agency rules and process). The type sets authority, methods, proof and handling.
  • Administrative: written authorization and scope, lawful monitoring consistent with policy, fairness, HR and legal involved.
  • Criminal: management and counsel decide on police involvement; legal process for evidence; acting at police direction can engage constitutional limits; no entrapment.
  • Civil: eDiscovery; legal holds override retention schedules; FRCP 37(e) remedies and adverse inference only with intent to deprive; EDRM 2.0 stages including disposition.
  • Regulatory and industry: cooperate through counsel; show due care and diligence; card brands can require a PCI forensic investigation.
  • Evidence: relevant, authentic, reliable, lawfully obtained; FRE 901, 1002 (verified copies), 803(6) (business records such as logs); direct, circumstantial, documentary, real, hearsay.
  • Cases change type: treat evidence as court-bound from day one, bring counsel in early, escalate by criteria, coordinate one evidence store and timeline.
