Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It has 4,000 workforce identities, 9,000 service accounts and API keys, 12 million customer accounts, and a growing number of AI agents.

Implementing authentication and authorization systems

Objective 5.6Knowing the systems that carry out authentication and authorization, and how to deploy them securely.

  • Directories (LDAP, RFC 4511; enterprise directories): store identities, groups and attributes. Use LDAPS or signed and sealed LDAP binds, restrict anonymous queries, and treat directory servers as tier 0.
  • Kerberos (RFC 4120): ticket-based single sign-on inside a domain. The user authenticates once to the key distribution center (KDC), whose authentication service issues a ticket-granting ticket (TGT); the ticket-granting service then issues service tickets for each resource, so passwords never cross the network. It needs synchronized clocks (tickets are time-limited) and a well-protected KDC, which holds every key. Weaknesses: the KDC as a single point of failure, offline cracking of tickets encrypted with weak service passwords, and forged tickets if the KDC's key is stolen.
  • AAA servers: RADIUS (RFC 2865, UDP, encrypts only the password, combines authentication and authorization) for network access and 802.1X; TACACS+ (TCP, encrypts the whole payload, separates authentication, authorization and accounting) for administrator access to network devices, with per-command authorization and logging.
  • Identity providers and federation servers: SAML and OpenID Connect sign-in for applications (objective 5.3), with conditional access as the policy engine.
  • Certificate-based authentication: smart cards and device certificates from an internal PKI, checked against revocation.
  • PAM and identity governance: PAM vaults and brokers privileged sessions; identity governance and administration (IGA) tools run provisioning, role management and access reviews.

Implementing them well means high availability (authentication is a dependency for everything), hardened and monitored servers, secure protocols only (no NTLM where it can be avoided, no LDAP without encryption), central logging of every authentication and authorization decision, and testing failure modes, including how people get in when the identity provider is down (break-glass accounts kept in a safe, monitored and tested).

Designing a resilient identity architecture

Objective 5.6Building identity systems that stay available and recoverable, because everything depends on them.

  • High availability: redundant directory servers, identity provider instances in more than one region, and tested failover; an identity outage is an outage of every application.
  • Hybrid design: decide which system is authoritative for which identities and attributes, protect synchronization servers as tier 0, and plan how users authenticate if the link between on-premises and cloud fails.
  • Break-glass accounts: a small number of emergency accounts, excluded from normal dependencies such as federation or a single MFA service, with strong credentials kept in a safe, monitored for any use and tested regularly.
  • Recovery: backups of directory data, documented procedures to rebuild a compromised directory or identity provider, and the ability to rotate all secrets and signing keys quickly.
  • Integration standards: applications integrate through standard protocols (SAML, OIDC, SCIM, LDAPS, Kerberos) rather than custom password stores.

Protecting identity systems

Objective 5.6Treating the systems that grant access as the most valuable targets in the estate.

Attackers aim for identity systems because controlling them means controlling everything. Joint guidance led by Australia's ASD with CISA, NSA and partners on detecting and mitigating Active Directory compromises describes the most common techniques, including Kerberoasting, golden tickets and abuse of directory replication. Protections:

  • Tiered administration: separate admin accounts and dedicated hardened workstations for domain controllers, identity providers and PAM; tier-0 credentials never used on ordinary systems.
  • Hardening: remove legacy protocols (NTLM where possible, unsigned LDAP), require AES for Kerberos, long random passwords or managed accounts for services, and minimal privileged group membership.
  • Monitoring: alerts on changes to privileged groups, unusual ticket requests, replication requests from non-domain controllers, new federation trusts and signing certificates, and sign-ins without matching authentication events.
  • Cloud identity baselines: block legacy authentication, require phishing-resistant MFA for administrators, and avoid permanent assignment of the highest privileged roles, as CISA's cloud security baselines require of US federal agencies.

Choosing and integrating identity systems

Objective 5.6Knowing which system does which job, and integrating applications with them consistently.

  • Directory (on-premises or cloud): the store of identities, groups and attributes, and the source for Kerberos and LDAP authentication in traditional networks.
  • Identity provider: modern sign-in, MFA, conditional access and federation to cloud applications; often synchronized from the directory or HR system.
  • Identity governance (IGA): provisioning workflows, role management, access requests and certifications.
  • Privileged access management (PAM): vaulting, rotation and brokering of administrator credentials, just-in-time elevation and session recording.
  • Secrets management: storage and rotation of application secrets, keys and certificates.
  • AAA servers: RADIUS for network access and TACACS+ for device administration.

Integration rules keep the estate manageable: every new application must use the identity provider (SAML or OIDC) rather than its own passwords, support SCIM or another automated provisioning method, log authentication and authorization events to the SIEM, and support MFA enforced at the identity provider. Applications that cannot meet these rules go on an exception list with compensating controls and a replacement plan.

Kerberos and directory internals

Objective 5.6Understanding enough of how Kerberos and LDAP work to secure them.

Kerberos uses a trusted third party, the key distribution center (KDC), with two services: the authentication service verifies the user and issues a ticket-granting ticket (TGT) encrypted with the KDC's own key; the ticket-granting service accepts the TGT and issues service tickets encrypted with each service's key. The client presents the service ticket to the server, which can verify it without contacting the KDC. Security consequences:

  • The KDC holds every key, so it is the most critical system in the domain.
  • Tickets are time-limited, so clocks must be synchronized (time sources are a security dependency).
  • Service tickets are encrypted with service account keys; weak service passwords can be cracked offline (Kerberoasting).
  • Delegation settings that let a service act on behalf of users broadly are high risk and should be restricted.
  • Stealing the KDC's ticket-signing key allows forged tickets (golden tickets), so it must be reset after a compromise (objective 3.7).

LDAP directories answer queries and handle simple binds; without protection, credentials and data cross the network in clear text. Require LDAPS or signed and sealed binds, restrict anonymous queries, limit who can read sensitive attributes, and monitor for bulk enumeration of users and groups, an early sign of an attacker mapping the domain.

Identity threat detection and response

Objective 5.6Watching identity systems for attacks and responding fast.

Most intrusions now use valid credentials, so identity telemetry is a primary detection source. Identity threat detection and response (ITDR) focuses on:

  • Sign-in attacks: password spraying, credential stuffing, MFA fatigue patterns, and impossible travel.
  • Token abuse: session tokens reused from new devices or locations, and tokens with unusual lifetimes or claims.
  • Privilege changes: new administrators, role assignments, federation trust changes and new application credentials.
  • Directory attacks: bulk enumeration, Kerberoasting, replication requests from non-domain controllers, and changes to sensitive groups.
  • Response actions: revoke sessions and tokens, reset credentials, disable accounts, remove malicious authenticators or app grants, often automated through SOAR playbooks.

Retiring legacy authentication removes whole attack classes: protocols such as NTLM, basic authentication for mail and LDAP simple binds cannot enforce MFA. Inventory where they are still used, move applications to modern protocols, and block the old ones in stages with monitoring for breakage.

Integrating legacy and on-premises applications

Objective 5.6Bringing older applications under central identity when they cannot speak modern protocols.

  • Application proxies and gateways: a proxy in front of the application handles modern sign-in and MFA, then passes identity to the application through headers or Kerberos.
  • Kerberos constrained delegation: lets a gateway obtain tickets on behalf of users for specific back-end services only, rather than unconstrained delegation.
  • LDAP and RADIUS integration: older applications can authenticate against the central directory, with MFA added through a RADIUS or LDAP proxy where supported.
  • Privileged access gateways: for systems with only local accounts (network devices, OT, old servers), a PAM gateway vaults the local credentials and brokers access after central sign-in and MFA.
  • Directory synchronization: on-premises directories synchronized with the cloud identity provider, with care over which attributes and passwords or hashes are synchronized.
  • Retirement plan: integration is often a bridge; record each legacy application with an owner, its compensating controls and a target date for replacement.

At the retail group, the old warehouse system with its own user table now sits behind an application proxy with MFA; its local accounts are disabled for everyone except a vaulted service account.

Testing identity systems

Objective 5.6Checking that authentication and authorization systems resist attack and failure.

  • Review identity provider and directory configuration against vendor and government baselines, regularly and after changes.
  • Include identity attacks (password spraying, token theft, privilege escalation paths) in penetration tests and red team exercises.
  • Map attack paths in the directory with graph analysis tools to find indirect routes to administrator rights.
  • Test recovery of the identity provider and directory, including break-glass access, in DR exercises.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the identity provider outage
Situation
The cloud identity provider has a regional outage and no one, including administrators, can sign in to fix production.
Best response
Use tested break-glass accounts that do not depend on the failed service, and design for multi-region identity availability.
Why
Identity is a dependency for everything; emergency access must be planned and tested.
Scenario 2: Kerberoasting alerts
Situation
Monitoring shows one workstation requesting service tickets for dozens of service accounts in a minute.
Best response
Investigate as a likely Kerberoasting attack, isolate the host, and reset affected service account passwords to long random values or move to managed accounts with AES only.
Why
Weak service account passwords can be cracked offline from service tickets.
Scenario 3: admins browsing the web
Situation
Domain administrators read email and browse the web while logged in with their admin accounts.
Best response
Separate admin accounts used only from dedicated privileged access workstations, with no email or browsing.
Why
Tiered administration keeps tier-0 credentials away from systems attackers can reach.
Scenario 4: TACACS+ or RADIUS
Situation
The network team needs per-command authorization and logging for administrators of switches and routers.
Best response
Use TACACS+ for device administration, keeping RADIUS for network access such as 802.1X and VPN.
Why
TACACS+ separates authentication, authorization and accounting and encrypts the whole payload.
Scenario 5: the help desk reset
Situation
Shortly after a help desk password reset, a new MFA method is registered on an administrator account from an unknown device.
Best response
Revoke sessions, remove the new method, verify the user through a trusted channel, investigate, and tighten help desk verification for privileged accounts.
Why
Identity threat detection watches recovery and authenticator changes, a common takeover path.

Key takeaways

  • Systems: LDAP directories, Kerberos (KDC, TGT, service tickets, time sync), RADIUS vs TACACS+, IdPs, PKI and certificates, PAM and IGA.
  • Resilience: high availability, hybrid authority and sync as tier 0, tested break-glass accounts, recovery and key rotation, standard integration protocols.
  • Protection: tiered administration and privileged workstations, hardening (no legacy protocols, AES Kerberos, managed service accounts), monitoring of privileged changes and ticket anomalies, cloud identity baselines.
