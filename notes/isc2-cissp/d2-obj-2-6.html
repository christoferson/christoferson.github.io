Overview

Objective 2.6

2.6 Determine data security controls and compliance requirements. Sub-topics: data states (in use, in transit, at rest); scoping and tailoring; standards and controls selection; data protection methods (DRM, DLP, CASB, CNAPP).

Selecting controls from system requirements is also on the 3.3–3.4 page; legal and contractual obligations on the 1.4 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its new loyalty analytics platform will hold pseudonymized data on 12 million customers in one cloud.

Data states

Objective 2.6Matching controls to whether data is stored, moving or being processed.

  • At rest: stored on any medium. Controls: access control and least privilege, encryption (SP 800-53 SC-28; CSF PR.DS-01), integrity protection such as write-once storage, backups protected at the same level, and secure disposal.
  • In transit (in motion): moving across networks. Controls: TLS, IPsec and SSH (SC-8; PR.DS-02), certificate validation, network segmentation, and DLP on email and web traffic.
  • In use: being processed in memory or displayed. Controls: application-level access control, masking of sensitive fields, endpoint DLP (copy, print, screenshot controls), memory protection, and confidential computing, which NIST describes as "hardware-enabled features that isolate and process encrypted data in memory" (objective 3.4). CSF PR.DS-10 covers data in use.

Data is weakest where it changes state: decrypted for processing, exported to a spreadsheet, pasted into an AI tool. Controls should follow the data through those transitions, which is what labels, DRM and DLP are for.

Standards and control selection

Objective 2.6Starting from a recognized standard or baseline rather than choosing controls one by one.

Requirements come from the data's classification, from laws and contracts (GDPR, PCI DSS, HIPAA), from the organization's own standards, and from the risk assessment. Rather than choosing each control from scratch, organizations start from a control baseline: "the set of controls that are applicable to information or an information system to meet legal, regulatory, or policy requirements" and protection needs (NIST SP 800-37). NIST SP 800-53B provides three security baselines (low, moderate, high, chosen by the FIPS 199 impact level) and a privacy baseline that applies to all systems; its 2025 minor release made "no changes to the baselines".

Other starting points: the ISO/IEC 27002 control set within an ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS, the CIS Controls with their implementation groups, PCI DSS for cardholder data, and industry overlays that adapt a baseline to a sector or technology. Organizations also turn these into their own standards (encryption standard, logging standard) so teams have one set of rules to follow (objective 1.6).

Scoping and tailoring

Objective 2.6Adjusting a baseline so it fits the system, without dropping protection the risk requires.

NIST defines tailoring as the process by which security and privacy control baselines are modified by:

  1. Common controlsIdentifying and designating controls inherited from elsewhere (a data center's physical security, a cloud provider's controls).
  2. ScopingApplying scoping considerations to decide which baseline controls apply and how.
  3. Compensating controlsSelecting controls "employed in lieu of the controls in the baselines" that give equivalent or comparable protection.
  4. ParametersAssigning values to organization-defined parameters (how often, how long, which roles).
  5. SupplementingAdding controls or enhancements the risk calls for.
  6. SpecificationProviding extra detail for implementation.

Scoping considerations in NIST's list include policy and regulatory, technology, physical infrastructure, system component allocation, operational and environmental, public access, scalability, common control and security objective considerations. For example, controls for physical media make no sense for a serverless application with no removable storage, and public access considerations change authentication controls for a public website.

Scoping vs tailoring. Scoping is one step inside tailoring: deciding which baseline controls apply to this system. Tailoring is the whole process, including adding controls. Every tailoring decision should be documented with its reason. Industry frameworks are scoped the same way: PCI DSS applies to the cardholder data environment, so segmenting card systems shrinks its scope.

For the loyalty analytics platform, the retail group starts from its moderate baseline, inherits physical and infrastructure controls from the cloud provider, scopes out media controls, supplements with stronger monitoring of data exports, sets parameters (retention of 13 months, quarterly access reviews), and documents a compensating control where the platform cannot support customer-managed keys.

Data protection methods

Objective 2.6Knowing what DRM, DLP, CASB and CNAPP tools do and when each fits.

NIST's glossary lists most of these terms without full definitions, so the descriptions below are the common industry meanings:

  • Digital rights management (DRM; for business documents often called information rights management): persistent controls that travel with a file, such as who may open it, whether it can be copied, printed or forwarded, and when access expires. Protection continues after the file leaves the organization.
  • Data loss prevention (DLP): identifies sensitive data by content and labels, and monitors or blocks it in use on endpoints, in motion on networks and email, and at rest in storage. It needs tuning to avoid blocking legitimate work, and it is weak against encrypted or deliberately disguised data.
  • Cloud access security broker (CASB): a policy enforcement point between users and cloud services, working through APIs or as a proxy. It discovers unsanctioned cloud use (shadow IT), applies DLP and access policies to SaaS data, and detects risky activity.
  • Cloud-native application protection platform (CNAPP): a combined platform for securing cloud workloads and their configuration, usually bundling cloud security posture management (misconfigurations), workload protection, cloud identity and entitlement management, and scanning of infrastructure-as-code and containers before deployment.

Other methods that protect data directly: encryption and tokenization (replacing card numbers with tokens that are useless if stolen), masking and pseudonymization, and data discovery and classification tools. The retail group applies DRM to board papers and product designs shared with suppliers, DLP on endpoints and email for card numbers and customer exports, a CASB to control which SaaS apps hold company data, and a CNAPP across both cloud providers to catch exposed storage and over-privileged roles.

Meeting compliance requirements

Objective 2.6Mapping data obligations to controls and proving they are met.

  1. Identify obligationsFor each data type: laws (GDPR, CCPA, HIPAA), contracts and industry standards (PCI DSS), and internal policy.
  2. Map to controlsLink each requirement to the controls in the tailored baseline that meet it, so one control can satisfy several obligations.
  3. Implement and record evidenceConfigurations, logs, reports and procedures that show each control works.
  4. AssessSelf-assessment, internal audit or external assessment (a PCI QSA, ISO/IEC 27001 certification body, SOC 2 auditor).
  5. MaintainRevisit when laws, contracts, data or systems change.

Compliance is a floor, not the goal. Meeting PCI DSS does not mean customer data outside the card environment is protected, and a certificate covers only its scope. Choose controls by risk and classification first, and use compliance requirements to confirm nothing mandatory is missing.

Worked example: building a control set

Objective 2.6Putting state-based controls, a baseline, tailoring and protection tools together for one system.

Building the control set for the retail group's loyalty analytics platform, step by step:

  1. Classify and categorizePseudonymized customer data: Confidential; moderate impact for confidentiality and integrity, low for availability; GDPR applies because pseudonymized data is still personal data.
  2. Gather requirementsGDPR principles and processor terms, the group's encryption, logging and access standards, the cloud provider's shared responsibility model, and the risk assessment (main risks: re-identification, bulk export, misconfiguration).
  3. Start from the baselineThe group's moderate baseline (based on SP 800-53B) plus its privacy baseline.
  4. TailorInherit physical and infrastructure controls from the provider; scope out media controls; set parameters; supplement with export monitoring and re-identification testing; document a compensating control for key management.
  5. Add protection methodsCNAPP for posture and entitlements, DLP on exports, masking in analyst views; DRM is not needed because data does not leave as documents.
  6. Map and evidenceLink each GDPR and internal requirement to controls; define the evidence (configuration reports, access reviews, logs).
Control set summary: loyalty analytics platform
At rest
Provider encryption with keys in the provider's managed key service (compensating: key access logged and reviewed monthly); storage not public; backups encrypted in an EU region
In transit
TLS 1.2 or higher everywhere; private endpoints between services
In use
Role-based views with masked identifiers; exports above 1,000 rows need approval and are logged; endpoint DLP on analyst laptops
Identity
SSO with phishing-resistant MFA; quarterly access reviews by the data owner
Monitoring
CNAPP posture checks daily; alerts on public exposure, new admin roles and large exports
Compliance mapping
GDPR principles and security obligations, internal standards; evidence collected automatically into the GRC tool

Choosing between overlapping tools comes down to where the data goes: DRM when files must stay protected outside your systems; DLP when you need to stop sensitive data leaving through channels you control; CASB when the problem is which cloud services hold data and how they are used; CNAPP when the risk is in the cloud infrastructure and workloads themselves. Many organizations need all four, each covering a different gap.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying control selection reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the control that does not apply
Situation
The moderate baseline includes media marking controls, but the new analytics platform is fully cloud-hosted with no removable media.
Best answer
Scope the control out using technology and physical infrastructure scoping considerations, and document the reason in the system's security plan.
Why
Scoping is a legitimate, documented part of tailoring; quietly ignoring a control is not.
Scenario 2: the shared design files
Situation
Product designs must be shared with overseas manufacturers, who need to view but not copy or forward them, and access must end when a contract ends.
Best answer
Use DRM (information rights management) on the files, with view-only rights and expiry tied to the contract.
Why
DRM controls travel with the file after it leaves the organization; DLP and CASB control flows within the organization's reach.
Scenario 3: data in use
Situation
Customer data is encrypted at rest and in transit, but analysts can export full records to spreadsheets on their laptops.
Best answer
Add in-use controls: role-based views with masked fields, export restrictions, and endpoint DLP that blocks or alerts on bulk exports.
Why
Encryption at rest and in transit does not protect data once an authorized user decrypts and uses it.
Scenario 4: compliance says no
Situation
A PCI DSS assessment of the card environment passed. The board asks whether that means customer data is safe.
Best response
Explain that the assessment covers only the cardholder data environment and the PCI requirements; customer data elsewhere (loyalty, marketing) needs its own controls chosen by classification and risk.
Why
Compliance is a floor and applies only within its scope; risk-based control selection covers the rest.

Key takeaways

  • Data states: at rest (access control, SC-28 encryption, WORM, backups), in transit (SC-8, TLS, IPsec, SSH), in use (masking, endpoint DLP, confidential computing); protect the transitions.
  • Selection: requirements from classification, law, contract, policy and risk; start from a baseline (SP 800-53B low, moderate, high plus privacy; ISO/IEC 27002; CIS; PCI DSS; overlays).
  • Tailoring: common controls, scoping, compensating controls, parameters, supplementing, specification; scoping is one step; document every decision.
  • Tools: DRM (controls travel with the file), DLP (detect and block sensitive data), CASB (control cloud app use), CNAPP (secure cloud workloads and configuration); plus encryption, tokenization and masking.
  • Compliance: identify obligations, map to controls, keep evidence, assess, maintain; compliance is a floor and covers only its scope.
