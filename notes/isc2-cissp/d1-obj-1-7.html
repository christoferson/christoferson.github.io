1.7: Business continuity requirements

Working out what the business must keep running, how quickly it must recover and how much data it can lose, which suppliers it depends on to do that, and turning those answers into funded continuity requirements.

Overview

Objective 1.7

1.7 Identify, analyze, assess, prioritize, and implement Business Continuity (BC) requirements. Sub-topics: business impact analysis (BIA); external dependencies.

This objective is about the requirements: what continuity the business needs and why. The strategies and operations that meet them (backups, recovery sites, DR processes and tests) are on the 7.10 page; the basics of BC, DR and plan types are in CC 2.2. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Most of its annual profit is made between November and January.

What business continuity requirements are

Objective 1.7Placing business continuity among related plans, and following the steps from requirements to plans.

A business continuity plan (BCP) is a documented set of predetermined instructions and procedures to sustain mission and business processes during and after a significant disruption (NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1). It is broader than IT: it covers people, facilities, suppliers and processes. Related plans in SP 800-34:

  • Disaster recovery plan (DRP): site-specific; recovers information systems at an alternate facility after a major disruption.
  • Information system contingency plan (ISCP): recovers one system, possibly at another location.
  • Continuity of operations plan (COOP): the US government's term for sustaining essential functions at an alternate site; private organizations usually use a BCP.
  • Cyber incident response plan, crisis communications plan, critical infrastructure protection plan, and the occupant emergency plan (people's safety, executed first).

SP 800-34 describes seven contingency planning steps: develop the policy; conduct the BIA; identify preventive controls; create recovery strategies; develop the plans; test, train and exercise; and maintain the plans. The outline's verbs map onto the early steps:

  1. IdentifyThe business processes, products and services, and the people, systems, data, sites and suppliers they need.
  2. AnalyzeWhat happens over time if each is disrupted (the BIA).
  3. AssessThe threats and current controls: which disruptions are likely, and how well the organization could cope today.
  4. PrioritizeWhich processes must be recovered first and how fast, within the budget leadership approves.
  5. ImplementTurn the requirements into strategies, plans, contracts and controls, and sustain them.

BC is a business program, owned by management. Senior management approves the BC policy, scope and budget and accepts any gap between requirements and capability. IT and security contribute, but a BCP written only by IT is a DR plan, not a BCP.

The business impact analysis

Objective 1.7Measuring the impact of disruption over time, and deriving recovery objectives from it.

The BIA is an analysis of business functions and the effect a disruption would have on them. NIST's version has three steps: identify the processes and their recovery criticality; identify the resources they require; and identify recovery priorities. It is performed by interviewing and surveying process owners, not guessed by IT.

Impacts are assessed in several dimensions and, crucially, over time: an outage of one hour, one day and one week can have very different effects.

  • Financial: lost sales, extra costs, fines, contractual penalties.
  • Customer and reputational: customers leaving, damage to the brand.
  • Legal and regulatory: breaches of obligations and notification duties.
  • Operational and safety: knock-on effects on other processes and on people.
  • Timing: impacts often depend on the calendar, such as the holiday season for a retailer or month-end for finance.

The BIA sets the recovery objectives for each process:

  • Maximum tolerable downtime (MTD, earlier also called maximum allowable outage): how long a process can be disrupted before the harm becomes unacceptable.
  • Recovery time objective (RTO): the time a system or resource can stay in recovery before the business is harmed. It must be shorter than the MTD, leaving time to verify and resume work.
  • Recovery point objective (RPO): "the point in time to which data must be recovered after an outage", which is the maximum data loss the business accepts. It drives backup and replication frequency.

Industry practice also uses work recovery time (WRT) for the time needed after systems are back to verify data and catch up, so that RTO plus WRT fits within the MTD; this term comes from practitioners, not NIST.

BIA comes before strategy. If a question asks what to do first in continuity planning, after getting management approval and scope, the answer is usually the BIA: you cannot choose recovery sites or backup frequencies before you know the RTO and RPO they must meet.

Prioritizing processes and resources

Objective 1.7Ranking what to recover first, and understanding what each priority process depends on.

From the BIA, processes are grouped into recovery tiers, for example: tier 1 within hours (payments, online orders), tier 2 within a day (distribution, customer service), tier 3 within a week (marketing analytics, HR reporting). Each process is then mapped to everything it needs:

  • People and skills, including deputies, so recovery does not depend on one person; orders of succession and delegations of authority for decision-makers.
  • Systems and data, including the identity, network and logging services every system needs. These "invisible" dependencies often have the shortest real RTO.
  • Facilities and equipment, including alternate work locations and remote working.
  • Suppliers and partners (section 4).

Dependencies set a floor: a tier 1 process cannot recover faster than the slowest system or supplier it relies on, so the dependency map often raises the priority of shared services. Priorities are also a cost decision. Shorter RTOs and RPOs need more expensive strategies, so leadership weighs the cost of the capability against the impact the BIA found, and accepts any remaining gap.

External dependencies

Objective 1.7Identifying and managing the suppliers, services and utilities outside the organization that continuity relies on.

Modern organizations depend heavily on others: cloud and SaaS providers, payment networks, telecom carriers, logistics firms, utilities, and the software vendors whose updates run on every endpoint. NIST CSF 2.0 makes this explicit:

  • GV.OC-05: "Outcomes, capabilities, and services that the organization depends on are understood and communicated", with examples such as an inventory of dependencies on external resources and documenting external dependencies that are potential points of failure.
  • GV.SC-07: supplier and third-party risks are understood, assessed, responded to and monitored over the relationship, including planning "for unexpected supplier and supply chain-related interruptions to ensure business continuity".
  • GV.SC-08: "Relevant suppliers and other third parties are included in incident planning, response, and recovery activities", for example including critical suppliers in exercises.

SP 800-53 CP-2(7) adds that the contingency plan should be coordinated with the contingency plans of external service providers, so both sides' assumptions match. Questions to answer for each critical dependency:

  • What are the supplier's own recovery objectives, and do they meet ours? Are they in the contract, with remedies?
  • Is there an alternative supplier, or a manual workaround, if the supplier fails?
  • Where does the supplier run, and does it depend on the same regions, carriers or software as we do (concentration risk)?
  • How will the supplier notify us of incidents, and will they join our exercises?

Dependencies fail without attacks. On July 19, 2024, a faulty content update to a widely used endpoint security product crashed millions of Windows computers worldwide; CISA noted the outage was "not ... malicious cyber activity" and warned of phishing that exploited it. The lesson for continuity (our framing): a single trusted vendor, cloud region or update channel can be a single point of failure across the whole estate, so BIA dependency maps must include software and services, not only data centers.

The retail group found that its two "independent" cloud regions both relied on one identity provider, and that its stores' backup 4G links and primary broadband used the same carrier in 40 towns. It added a break-glass identity path and a second carrier.

Implementing the requirements

Objective 1.7Turning approved requirements into strategies, plans, contracts and a sustained program.

  • Preventive controls reduce the chance or effect of disruption: redundant power and networks, resilient architecture, patching and change control.
  • Recovery strategies meet the RTO and RPO: backup frequency, replication, alternate sites and cloud recovery, chosen by cost against the BIA (objective 7.10).
  • Continuity of people and decisions: deputies, cross-training, succession and delegations of authority, remote working.
  • Manual workarounds: paper or offline procedures for critical processes while systems are down.
  • Contracts: recovery objectives, notification and exercise participation written into supplier agreements (objective 1.8).
  • Plans, testing and maintenance: written, owned plans, exercised regularly and updated after changes and lessons learned (objectives 7.12 and 7.13).

Security has its own continuity requirements: security controls must keep working during a disruption and in recovery mode. Alternate sites need the same access control and monitoring, emergency access accounts must be protected, and backups must be protected as strongly as live data. A recovery that restores systems without their security controls creates a new incident.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying BC requirements reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the eager IT team
Situation
The IT team proposes buying a hot site for all systems as the first step of a new continuity program.
Best response
Get management sponsorship and scope, then perform a BIA to set MTD, RTO and RPO per process; choose sites and strategies only when the requirements are known.
Why
Strategies follow requirements. A hot site for everything may be far more than most processes need, and still miss non-IT dependencies.
Scenario 2: the impossible objective
Situation
The BIA sets an RTO of 2 hours for online orders, but the current backup approach would take 12 hours to restore.
Best response
Report the gap with options and costs to the process owner and leadership, who either fund a faster strategy or formally accept the risk; track the decision.
Why
Management owns the trade-off between continuity cost and impact; quietly lowering the objective or ignoring the gap both fail governance.
Scenario 3: the hidden single point of failure
Situation
During an exercise, both "independent" data centers turn out to need the same SaaS identity provider to let anyone log in.
Best response
Add the identity provider to the BIA as a critical dependency, agree recovery terms with the provider, and build a protected break-glass access path.
Why
External dependencies set the floor for recovery; CSF GV.OC-05 expects dependencies that are potential points of failure to be identified and documented.

Key takeaways

  • BC sustains business processes through disruption; it is broader than DR (systems at an alternate site) and owned by management. SP 800-34: seven steps, several plan types; the occupant emergency plan protects people first.
  • Verbs: identify processes and resources, analyze impact (BIA), assess threats and current capability, prioritize, implement.
  • BIA: interview process owners; impacts by type and over time (seasonality); sets MTD, RTO (shorter than MTD) and RPO (acceptable data loss); WRT is industry practice. BIA comes before strategy.
  • Prioritize: recovery tiers; map people, systems (including shared services such as identity), facilities and suppliers; dependencies set the floor; cost versus impact decided by leadership.
  • External dependencies: CSF GV.OC-05, GV.SC-07, GV.SC-08; CP-2(7) coordination with providers; concentration risk; non-malicious failures such as a faulty vendor update.
  • Implement: preventive controls, recovery strategies, people continuity, manual workarounds, contracts, plans, tests and maintenance; security controls must survive into recovery.
