1.7–1.8: Continuity requirements and personnel security

Working out what the business must keep running and what it depends on, then the people side of security: checking who you hire, what they agree to, how access follows them through their career, and how contractors and suppliers are held to the same rules.

Overview

Objectives 1.7 and 1.8

1.7 Identify, analyze, assess, prioritize, and implement Business Continuity (BC) requirements. 1.8 Contribute to and enforce personnel security policies and procedures.

Objective 1.7 is the requirements end of continuity planning: what must keep running, for how long it can stop, and what it relies on. Building and testing the recovery itself is Domain 7 (objectives 7.10 to 7.13). The definitions of MTD, RTO and RPO, the plan types and alternate sites are in CC 2.2; joiner, mover and leaver basics are in CC 3.1. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It hires about 1,500 seasonal staff each year and uses contractors for development and store maintenance.

Business continuity requirements

Objective 1.7Turning a business impact analysis into prioritized, funded continuity requirements that senior management approves.

The business impact analysis (BIA) is the core tool. NIST SP 800-34 describes three steps: identify the business processes and how critical their recovery is, identify the resources each needs, and set recovery priorities. The outline's verbs describe the full requirements cycle a CISSP should be able to run:

  1. IdentifyList business processes with their owners, and the systems, people, sites, data and suppliers each depends on.
  2. AnalyzeEstimate the impact of losing each process over time: lost revenue, extra costs, fines, contract penalties, customer and reputation damage, safety.
  3. AssessSet the maximum tolerable downtime (MTD) with the owner, then recovery time (RTO, shorter than MTD) and recovery point (RPO) targets; compare them with what current capabilities can achieve.
  4. PrioritizeRank processes by impact and urgency; the gaps between target and current capability become the risk list.
  5. ImplementChoose and fund strategies (redundancy, alternate sites, manual workarounds, supplier commitments), approved by senior management, then build and test the plans (Domain 7).

Two points matter at this level. The BIA is owned by the business, not IT: process owners set the MTD, and senior management accepts the cost of meeting it or the risk of not meeting it. And it must be revisited when the business changes, such as a new sales channel, an acquisition or a new critical supplier.

External dependencies

Objective 1.7Finding the suppliers, services and infrastructure outside the organization that critical processes rely on, and planning for their failure.

Most continuity failures now start outside the organization. NIST CSF 2.0 expects that "outcomes, capabilities, and services that the organization depends on are understood and communicated" (GV.OC-05), with examples such as inventorying dependencies on external resources like cloud hosting and identifying "external dependencies that are potential points of failure." It also expects that "relevant suppliers and other third parties are included in incident planning, response, and recovery activities" (GV.SC-08), including in exercises. SP 800-53 CP-2(7) asks organizations to "coordinate the contingency plan with the contingency plans of external service providers."

Common external dependencies: cloud and SaaS providers, network carriers, payment networks, utilities, logistics partners, managed service providers, key software vendors, and even single individuals at a supplier. Watch for concentration risk, where many functions depend on one provider, or where several "different" providers rely on the same underlying service.

Not every outage is an attack. On July 19, 2024, a faulty content update to a widely used endpoint security product crashed Windows machines worldwide, about 8.5 million devices by Microsoft's estimate. CISA relayed that it was due to the update "and not to malicious cyber activity", but attackers quickly used it for phishing. Continuity planning must cover a trusted supplier's mistake as well as an attack, and update testing and staged rollouts are part of resilience.

The retail group's dependency map showed that both of its "independent" network carriers used the same fiber route into the payment data center; it added a third route through a different conduit and wrote carrier recovery times into the contracts.

Screening and hiring

Objective 1.8Matching the depth of pre-employment checks to the risk of each role, within the law.

NIST SP 800-53 starts personnel security before hiring. PS-2 requires assigning "a risk designation to all organizational positions" and establishing screening criteria for each; PS-3 requires organizations to "screen individuals prior to authorizing access to the system" and to rescreen them under defined conditions, such as moving into a more sensitive role. PS-9 adds security and privacy responsibilities to job descriptions. The retail group screens a seasonal store associate (identity and right to work) far less deeply than a payments database administrator (identity, employment and education history, criminal record where lawful, and a credit check where allowed for financial roles).

Checks must be lawful. In the US, the joint FTC and EEOC guidance explains the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) rules when an employer uses a background screening company:

  • tell the candidate in writing, in a stand-alone document (not in the job application), and get their written permission;
  • before rejecting someone because of the report, send a pre-adverse action notice with a copy of the report and a summary of their rights;
  • after the decision, send an adverse action notice naming the screening company and explaining the right to dispute;
  • apply the same checks to everyone in the same role, and never base them on race, religion, sex, national origin, disability, genetic information or age; criminal-record exclusions must be "job related and consistent with business necessity";
  • dispose of reports securely.

Other countries limit checks more strictly. Under GDPR Article 10, criminal conviction data may be processed only under official authority or where EU or national law allows it with safeguards, so the retail group's EU stores use only checks that local law permits for each role.

Employment agreements and policy requirements

Objective 1.8Using signed agreements, rules of behavior and policy-driven practices to set expectations and deter misuse.

SP 800-53 PS-6 requires individuals to "sign appropriate access agreements prior to being granted access" and re-sign when they change, giving as examples "nondisclosure agreements, acceptable use agreements, rules of behavior, and conflict-of-interest agreements." PL-4 requires documented acknowledgment that people "read, understand, and agree to abide by the rules of behavior." Typical agreements:

  • Non-disclosure agreement (NDA): protects confidential information and trade secrets during and after employment (objective 1.4).
  • Acceptable use policy and rules of behavior, including AI tools and social media.
  • Conflict of interest declarations.
  • Non-compete clauses, which depend on local law. In the US, the FTC's 2024 rule banning most non-competes never took effect: a court stopped it in August 2024, and the FTC removed it in February 2026, so state law still governs.

Policy-driven practices reduce fraud and insider risk: separation of duties, job rotation and mandatory vacations (which can expose fraud that needs constant hands-on cover-up), covered in CC 1.4. SP 800-53 PS-8 requires "a formal sanctions process for individuals failing to comply" with security and privacy policies, applied consistently, so that rules are credible.

Onboarding, transfers and termination

Objective 1.8Coordinating HR, managers and IT so access is right on day one, adjusted on every move, and removed on departure.

The identity mechanics are in CC 3.1; at CISSP level the focus is the process and its owners:

  1. OnboardingScreening complete, agreements signed (PS-6), security awareness training before access, role-based access granted from HR data.
  2. TransferReview and confirm ongoing need for current access when someone is reassigned (PS-5): add the new role's access, remove the old, and rescreen if the new role is more sensitive.
  3. TerminationDisable access within a defined time, revoke credentials, recover badges, laptops and tokens, hold an exit interview reminding the person of continuing obligations such as NDAs, and keep access to the information they controlled (PS-4).

Hostile terminations. When someone is dismissed for cause or may react badly, disable their access at the same moment they are told, not afterwards, and have security present if needed. Friendly departures can follow the normal schedule. Either way, change any shared passwords the person knew.

Vendors, consultants and contractors

Objective 1.8Holding third parties to the organization's security requirements through contracts, SLAs and oversight.

SP 800-53 PS-7 requires organizations to set personnel security requirements for external providers, require them to follow the organization's policies, and require them to "notify ... of any transfers or terminations" of their staff who hold the organization's credentials, badges or privileges, and to "monitor provider compliance". SA-9 requires providers of external system services to meet the organization's security requirements, with oversight and continuous monitoring.

A service level agreement (SLA) "defines the specific responsibilities of the service provider and sets the customer expectations" (CNSSI 4009). SP 800-53 notes that SLAs "define the expectations of performance for implemented controls" with measurable outcomes and remedies. Security clauses worth having in supplier contracts include:

  • security requirements and the evidence the supplier must provide (certifications, audit reports);
  • the right to audit or assess;
  • incident and breach notification within a set time, and supply chain compromise notification (SR-8);
  • locations where data is processed and stored, with notice of changes;
  • service levels for availability, response and recovery times;
  • return and deletion of data at the end of the contract;
  • the same requirements passed down to subcontractors (SR-3(3) "sub-tier flow down").

EU financial firms must include most of these by law in contracts for ICT services (DORA Article 30). CISA's "Secure by Demand" guide (2024) adds questions to ask software suppliers before buying, such as single sign-on and phishing-resistant MFA at no extra cost and security logs retained "for at least six months at no additional charge."

Key takeaways

  • BC requirements: identify, analyze, assess, prioritize, implement. The BIA (owned by the business) sets MTD, RTO and RPO per process; gaps between targets and capability go to senior management to fund or accept. Revisit after business changes.
  • External dependencies: map suppliers, cloud, carriers and utilities (CSF GV.OC-05); include them in plans and exercises (GV.SC-08, CP-2(7)); watch concentration risk. A supplier's mistake can be as disruptive as an attack (July 2024 outage).
  • Screening: risk-designate positions (PS-2), screen before access and rescreen (PS-3). US FCRA: written disclosure and consent, pre-adverse and adverse action notices; EEOC: consistent, non-discriminatory. GDPR Article 10 limits criminal checks.
  • Agreements: NDAs, acceptable use, rules of behavior, conflict of interest signed before access (PS-6, PL-4). US non-compete rule never took effect. Separation of duties, job rotation, mandatory vacation; formal sanctions (PS-8).
  • Life cycle: onboarding (screened, signed, trained), transfer (review access, PS-5), termination (disable promptly, recover assets, exit interview, PS-4); hostile terminations disabled at the moment of notice.
  • Third parties: PS-7 and SA-9; SLAs with measurable levels and remedies; contract clauses for audit, breach notification, data location, return and deletion, flow-down to subcontractors.
