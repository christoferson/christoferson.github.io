Overview

Objectives 7.1 and 7.2

7.1 Understand and comply with investigations. 7.2 Conduct logging and monitoring activities.

Investigation types, evidence rules and legal holds are on the 1.5 page; logging and monitoring basics in CC 5.2. This page covers how investigations and monitoring are actually run. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its security operations center (SOC) runs a cloud SIEM and SOAR; this quarter it is investigating a buyer suspected of leaking supplier pricing to a competitor.

Evidence collection and handling

Objective 7.1Collecting digital evidence so it is complete, unaltered and provably so.

  • Authorize and scope first: know whether the investigation is administrative, civil, criminal or regulatory (it sets the standard of proof), involve legal counsel and HR, and respect privacy law and policy on monitoring employees.
  • Preserve: isolate rather than switch off; issue legal holds; stop routine deletion of relevant logs and mailboxes.
  • Order of volatility (RFC 3227): collect the most short-lived data first: registers and cache; routing and ARP tables, process table and memory; temporary file systems; disk; remote logs; physical configuration; archival media. RFC 3227: "Don't shutdown until you've completed evidence collection."
  • Integrity: make forensic images with write blockers, hash the original and the image (SHA-256) and work only on verified copies.
  • Chain of custody: "a process that tracks the movement of evidence through its collection, safeguarding, and analysis lifecycle" (NIST SP 800-72): who collected it, when, where, every transfer and why, with evidence stored securely (evidence storage).

Do not let IT "clean it up". Rebooting, running antivirus or reimaging before collection destroys memory and changes disks. The first responder's job is to contain without destroying evidence, and to call in trained investigators. Evidence that cannot be shown to be unaltered (US Federal Rules of Evidence 901: "evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is") may be worthless.

Forensic process, tools and artifacts

Objective 7.1Following a repeatable forensic process with validated tools, across every kind of artifact.

NIST SP 800-86 describes four phases: collection, examination (extracting relevant data), analysis (drawing conclusions) and reporting, verifying integrity with message digests throughout. Tools include hardware write blockers, imaging tools, memory capture, forensic suites for file systems and timelines, network capture and analysis, and mobile extraction tools. Tools should be tested and validated (NIST runs a computer forensics tool testing program), and examiners trained, so results are defensible.

The outline's artifact types:

  • Data: documents, email, databases and cloud files, including metadata (author, timestamps) and deleted or previous versions.
  • Computer: memory (running processes, network connections, keys, malware that never touches disk), disk images, the registry, event logs, browser history, prefetch and similar execution traces, and file system timestamps for timelines.
  • Network: packet captures, flow records, firewall, proxy and DNS logs; they show communication even when hosts have been wiped.
  • Mobile devices: messages, call logs, locations, app data and backups; strong encryption and remote wipe make quick isolation (airplane mode or a shielded bag) important.

Cloud evidence depends on the provider: you may not have physical disks, so collect provider logs, snapshots and API audit trails, and make sure the contract and retention settings allow it before an incident.

Investigative techniques and reporting

Objective 7.1Using sound techniques to reconstruct what happened, and documenting it so others can rely on it.

  • Techniques: timeline analysis across sources (synchronized clocks matter), correlation of logs, keyword and pattern searches, file carving to recover deleted data, malware analysis (static and dynamic, in a sandbox), interviews and interrogation (by trained staff, with HR and legal), and surveillance where lawful.
  • Method: form hypotheses, test them against the evidence, and look for evidence that would disprove them, not only confirm them.
  • Documentation: notes made at the time of every action, with times, tools and versions, so another examiner could repeat the work.
  • Reporting: facts and findings separated from opinions, with the evidence for each conclusion, written for its audience (management, legal, law enforcement or a court). Records kept in the regular course of business can be admitted as business records (FRE 803(6)).

In the pricing-leak case, investigators imaged the buyer's laptop under legal hold, built a timeline from file access logs, cloud sharing logs and DLP alerts, and found the file uploaded to a personal cloud account the evening before the competitor's offer. HR and legal ran the interview with the report in hand.

Log management

Objective 7.2Generating, protecting, retaining and disposing of logs so they support detection and investigations.

Log management is "the process for generating, transmitting, storing, accessing, and disposing of log data" (NIST SP 800-92 Rev. 1 draft; the 2006 SP 800-92 is still the final version). SP 800-53's AU family sets the requirements:

  • What: events adequate for after-the-fact investigations (AU-2), with what, when, where, source, outcome and identity in each record (AU-3).
  • Time: timestamps in UTC or with an offset (AU-8), from synchronized clocks.
  • Protection: "protect audit information and audit logging tools from unauthorized access, modification, and deletion" (AU-9), ideally on a separate system (AU-9(2)) that the administrators of monitored systems cannot change.
  • Review and correlation: AU-6, including correlation across repositories.
  • Retention (AU-11): long enough for investigations and legal requirements. For US federal agencies, OMB M-26-14 (May 2026) replaced the earlier logging memo with a "risk-based, prioritized logging approach", logs "actively searchable for a minimum of 6 months" and retrievable for a year.

CISA's baseline goals add: centralize logs, restrict access to them, and alert when a critical log source stops sending.

Detection: IDPS, SIEM, SOAR and UEBA

Objective 7.2Combining detection tools so attacks are spotted quickly and handled consistently.

  • Intrusion detection and prevention systems (IDPS): network or host based; signature detection is effective for known threats, anomaly detection finds unknown ones at the cost of false positives. An IPS sits inline and blocks; an IDS alerts.
  • SIEM: "a program that provides centralized logging capabilities for a variety of log types" (SP 800-92), adding normalization, correlation rules, alerting, dashboards and search for investigations.
  • SOAR (security orchestration, automation and response): playbooks that enrich alerts, open tickets and take actions automatically (disable an account, isolate a host, block an address), with human approval for risky steps. CISA and ASD ACSC published joint SIEM and SOAR implementation guidance in 2025.
  • User and entity behavior analytics (UEBA): baselines normal behavior for each user, host and service account and flags deviations, such as a buyer downloading ten times their usual volume or a service account logging in interactively. Useful for insider threats and stolen credentials.
  • Continuous monitoring and tuning: continuous monitoring is "maintaining ongoing awareness to support organizational risk decisions" (NIST SP 800-137 sets out a program for it). Tuning removes noisy rules, adds detections for new threats and checks coverage against frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK; NIST's incident response guidance says to tune "to reduce false positives and false negatives to acceptable levels."

False negatives are the dangerous ones. False positives waste analysts' time and cause alert fatigue; false negatives mean an attack is missed. Tuning that only suppresses alerts can create false negatives, so test detections (with attack simulations) after tuning.

Egress monitoring, threat intelligence and hunting

Objective 7.2Watching what leaves the organization, using intelligence about attackers, and searching proactively for what tools missed.

  • Egress monitoring: watching outbound traffic and data for theft and command-and-control: DLP on endpoints, email, web and cloud for sensitive content; proxy and DNS logs for connections to unusual or newly registered domains; volume anomalies; and encrypted traffic inspection where policy allows. Steganography and encrypted archives can hide data, so watch for behavior as well as content.
  • Threat intelligence: threat feeds of indicators of compromise ("technical artifacts or observables that suggest that an attack is imminent or is currently underway") shared in standard formats such as STIX over TAXII, plus reports on adversaries' tactics, techniques and procedures. Intelligence is strategic (trends for leadership), operational (campaigns) or tactical (indicators). Indicators age quickly; behaviors last longer.
  • Threat hunting: analysts proactively search for attackers who evaded detection, starting from a hypothesis (for example, "an attacker is using legitimate admin tools to move laterally"), often based on intelligence or ATT&CK techniques. Successful hunts become new automated detections.

Key takeaways

  • Evidence: authorize and scope; preserve (legal hold, isolate, do not shut down early); order of volatility (RFC 3227); write blockers, hashes, work on copies; chain of custody.
  • Forensics (SP 800-86): collection, examination, analysis, reporting with validated tools. Artifacts: data and metadata, memory and disk, network captures and flows, mobile; plan cloud evidence in advance.
  • Investigations: timelines, correlation, carving, malware analysis, interviews; test hypotheses; contemporaneous notes; reports separate facts from opinion.
  • Logs (AU family, SP 800-92): what to log, UTC time, protected from admins, correlated, retained (M-26-14 for federal agencies); alert when logging stops.
  • Detection: IDPS, SIEM, SOAR playbooks, UEBA baselines, continuous monitoring (SP 800-137) and tuning; false negatives are the dangerous errors.
  • Egress, intelligence, hunting: DLP and outbound anomaly monitoring; feeds (IoCs, STIX/TAXII) and TTPs; hypothesis-driven hunts become detections.
