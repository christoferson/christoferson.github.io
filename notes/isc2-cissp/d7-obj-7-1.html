Overview

Objective 7.1

7.1 Understand and comply with investigations. Sub-topics: evidence collection and handling; reporting and documentation; investigative techniques; digital forensics tools, tactics, and procedures; artifacts (data, computer, network, mobile device).

Investigation types, evidence rules and legal holds are on the 1.5 page; the logs that feed investigations on the 7.2 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its security team is investigating a buyer suspected of leaking supplier pricing to a competitor.

Evidence collection and handling

Objective 7.1Collecting digital evidence so it is complete, unaltered and provably so.

  • Authorize and scope first: know whether the investigation is administrative, civil, criminal or regulatory (it sets the standard of proof), involve legal counsel and HR, and respect privacy law and policy on monitoring employees.
  • Preserve: isolate rather than switch off; issue legal holds; stop routine deletion of relevant logs and mailboxes.
  • Order of volatility (RFC 3227): collect the most short-lived data first: registers and cache; routing and ARP tables, process table and memory; temporary file systems; disk; remote logs; physical configuration; archival media. RFC 3227: "Don't shutdown until you've completed evidence collection."
  • Integrity: make forensic images with write blockers, hash the original and the image (SHA-256) and work only on verified copies.
  • Chain of custody: "a process that tracks the movement of evidence through its collection, safeguarding, and analysis lifecycle" (NIST SP 800-72): who collected it, when, where, every transfer and why, with evidence stored securely (evidence storage).

Do not let IT "clean it up". Rebooting, running antivirus or reimaging before collection destroys memory and changes disks. The first responder's job is to contain without destroying evidence, and to call in trained investigators. Evidence that cannot be shown to be unaltered (US Federal Rules of Evidence 901: "evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is") may be worthless.

Forensic process, tools and artifacts

Objective 7.1Following a repeatable forensic process with validated tools, across every kind of artifact.

NIST SP 800-86 describes four phases: collection, examination (extracting relevant data), analysis (drawing conclusions) and reporting, verifying integrity with message digests throughout. Tools include hardware write blockers, imaging tools, memory capture, forensic suites for file systems and timelines, network capture and analysis, and mobile extraction tools. Tools should be tested and validated (NIST runs a computer forensics tool testing program), and examiners trained, so results are defensible.

The outline's artifact types:

  • Data: documents, email, databases and cloud files, including metadata (author, timestamps) and deleted or previous versions.
  • Computer: memory (running processes, network connections, keys, malware that never touches disk), disk images, the registry, event logs, browser history, prefetch and similar execution traces, and file system timestamps for timelines.
  • Network: packet captures, flow records, firewall, proxy and DNS logs; they show communication even when hosts have been wiped.
  • Mobile devices: messages, call logs, locations, app data and backups; strong encryption and remote wipe make quick isolation (airplane mode or a shielded bag) important.

Cloud evidence depends on the provider: you may not have physical disks, so collect provider logs, snapshots and API audit trails, and make sure the contract and retention settings allow it before an incident.

Investigative techniques and reporting

Objective 7.1Using sound techniques to reconstruct what happened, and documenting it so others can rely on it.

  • Techniques: timeline analysis across sources (synchronized clocks matter), correlation of logs, keyword and pattern searches, file carving to recover deleted data, malware analysis (static and dynamic, in a sandbox), interviews and interrogation (by trained staff, with HR and legal), and surveillance where lawful.
  • Method: form hypotheses, test them against the evidence, and look for evidence that would disprove them, not only confirm them.
  • Documentation: notes made at the time of every action, with times, tools and versions, so another examiner could repeat the work.
  • Reporting: facts and findings separated from opinions, with the evidence for each conclusion, written for its audience (management, legal, law enforcement or a court). Records kept in the regular course of business can be admitted as business records (FRE 803(6)).

In the pricing-leak case, investigators imaged the buyer's laptop under legal hold, built a timeline from file access logs, cloud sharing logs and DLP alerts, and found the file uploaded to a personal cloud account the evening before the competitor's offer. HR and legal ran the interview with the report in hand.

Live response and memory forensics

Objective 7.1Collecting evidence from running systems without destroying it.

Many modern attacks leave little on disk: malware runs in memory, attackers use built-in administration tools, and full-disk encryption makes a powered-off disk unreadable without its key. Live response collects volatile evidence while the system is still running:

  • Memory capture: a full image of RAM shows running processes, injected code, network connections, logged-in users, command history and sometimes encryption keys. Capture it before any shutdown.
  • Volatile state: process lists, open network connections, ARP and routing tables, scheduled tasks and logged-on sessions, collected with trusted tools run from external media rather than the possibly compromised system's own binaries.
  • Minimal footprint: every action changes the system a little, so record each command and its time, and prefer tools that write output to external storage.
  • Remote collection: endpoint detection and response (EDR) agents can collect memory, files and triage packages from remote stores and laptops, which matters when the device is in a branch hundreds of miles away.
  • Containment without loss: network isolation through EDR or switch port changes stops the attacker while keeping the system powered for collection.

The decision to pull the plug, isolate or keep watching is a business and legal decision as much as a technical one: watching an attacker may reveal more, but lets damage continue. Make it with the incident manager and counsel, and record why.

Working with law enforcement and other parties

Objective 7.1Knowing when and how to bring in outsiders, and what changes when you do.

  • When to involve law enforcement: decided in advance in the incident response policy, usually by legal counsel and senior management, for crimes such as extortion, fraud, theft of trade secrets or child abuse material (which in many jurisdictions must be reported, and must never be copied or shared; follow legal counsel).
  • What changes: police may take evidence and set the pace; the organization loses some control over timing and publicity, but gains legal powers (warrants, international cooperation) it does not have itself.
  • Do not act as police: private investigators and security staff must not hack back, entrap, or seize property beyond what law and policy allow; evidence gathered unlawfully can be excluded and create liability.
  • External specialists: incident response and forensic firms are often engaged through legal counsel, so that their work may be protected by legal privilege where the law allows; check with counsel rather than assume.
  • Regulators, insurers and partners: notification duties and deadlines (objective 1.5), insurer requirements to use approved firms, and contractual duties to card brands and customers.

In the pricing-leak case, counsel decided to treat it as an internal disciplinary and civil matter first, while preserving evidence to the standard a criminal case would need in case the competitor's involvement led to a referral.

eDiscovery

Objective 7.1Finding, preserving and producing electronically stored information for legal proceedings.

Electronic discovery (eDiscovery) is the process of identifying, preserving, collecting, reviewing and producing electronically stored information (ESI) for litigation or regulatory requests. The Electronic Discovery Reference Model (EDRM) describes its stages:

  1. Information governanceKnowing what data exists and where, under retention schedules, before any case begins.
  2. IdentificationFinding the custodians, systems and date ranges that may hold relevant information.
  3. Preservation and collectionLegal holds stop deletion; data is collected defensibly with metadata intact.
  4. Processing, review and analysisReducing volume (deduplication, filtering), reviewing for relevance and privilege, often with technology-assisted review.
  5. Production and presentationDelivering ESI in agreed formats, and presenting it in proceedings.

Good information governance makes eDiscovery cheaper and less risky: data kept past its retention period can still be discoverable, so deleting it on schedule (before any hold) reduces exposure, while deleting it after a hold is issued can lead to sanctions for spoliation.

Insider investigations and employee privacy

Objective 7.1Investigating staff fairly and lawfully.

  • Policy basis: acceptable use and monitoring policies, acknowledged by staff, set what may be monitored and searched; in the EU, monitoring must also be necessary, proportionate and transparent.
  • Authorization: HR and legal approve an investigation into a named person before searches of their mailbox or devices.
  • Need-to-know: only investigators and decision makers know about the case, protecting both the evidence and the employee if the suspicion is wrong.
  • Fair process: the employee can respond to the evidence, often with a representative, before decisions are made.
  • Proportion: search only what the allegation requires, and handle personal data found along the way carefully.

Forensic analysis types and investigator concepts

Objective 7.1Categories of forensic work and the classic ideas investigators use.

  • Media analysis: disks, flash and other storage, including deleted data and slack space.
  • Network analysis: packet captures, flows and device logs; much network evidence must be collected as it happens.
  • Software analysis: reviewing code, malware and application logs (reverse engineering, code comparison for IP disputes).
  • Hardware and embedded device analysis: phones, IoT devices, firmware and vehicles.
  • Locard's exchange principle: every contact leaves a trace; in digital terms, attackers leave logs, artifacts and changes behind.
  • MOM: means, opportunity and motive, used to assess suspects.
  • Enticement vs entrapment: providing an opportunity is lawful; inducing a crime is not (objective 7.7).

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the helpful technician
Situation
A desktop technician finds malware on a finance laptop and plans to reimage it straight away so the user can work.
Best response
Isolate the laptop from the network but keep it powered, call the incident team, and capture memory and a disk image before any cleanup.
Why
Reimaging destroys evidence; contain without destroying, following the order of volatility.
Scenario 2: the unverified copy
Situation
An examiner made an image of a disk but did not record a hash, and the original has since been returned to service.
Best response
Document the gap honestly in the report; the evidence may still be usable but its weight is reduced. Fix the procedure so hashes are always recorded at acquisition.
Why
Integrity must be provable; hashes of the original and the image are the proof.
Scenario 3: the suspected crime
Situation
An investigation finds that an employee is receiving payments from a supplier in return for contracts.
Best response
Preserve evidence, involve legal counsel, and let management decide on referral to law enforcement under the policy.
Why
Criminal matters need the highest standard of evidence and a decision by counsel and management, not by the investigator.
Scenario 4: the litigation request
Situation
The company receives notice of a lawsuit about a product defect; email older than two years is normally deleted automatically.
Best response
Issue a legal hold immediately to suspend deletion for relevant custodians and systems.
Why
Deleting relevant data after litigation is reasonably anticipated is spoliation.
Scenario 5: the manager's request
Situation
A manager asks IT to quietly read an employee's email because they suspect the employee is job hunting.
Best response
Decline unless HR and legal authorize an investigation under the monitoring policy for a legitimate reason.
Why
Investigations need authorization, a lawful basis and proportion.

Key takeaways

  • Evidence: authorize and scope; preserve (legal hold, isolate, do not shut down early); order of volatility (RFC 3227); write blockers, hashes, work on copies; chain of custody.
  • Forensics (SP 800-86): collection, examination, analysis, reporting with validated tools. Artifacts: data and metadata, memory and disk, network captures and flows, mobile; plan cloud evidence in advance.
  • Investigations: timelines, correlation, carving, malware analysis, interviews; test hypotheses; contemporaneous notes; reports separate facts from opinion.
  • Live response: capture memory and volatile state first with trusted tools, record every action, isolate rather than power off.
  • Outsiders: law enforcement by policy and counsel decision; never hack back; specialists through counsel. eDiscovery (EDRM): governance, identification, preservation, collection, review, production; holds prevent spoliation.
