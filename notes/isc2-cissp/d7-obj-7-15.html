7.15: Addressing personnel safety and security concerns

Protecting people: when they travel, through awareness of how they are targeted, in emergencies, under duress, and wherever they work.

Overview

Objective 7.15

7.15 Address personnel safety and security concerns. Sub-topics: travel; security training and awareness; emergency management; duress.

Awareness program design is on the 1.12 page; personnel security policies on the 1.8 page; physical controls on the 7.14 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its buyers travel often to suppliers abroad, and its stores handle cash, often with one or two staff at opening and closing.

Travel

Objective 7.15Protecting people, devices and data when staff travel, especially abroad.

  • Before travel: check risk ratings and advice for the destination, register trips so the organization can find people in an emergency, brief travelers on local threats and laws (including laws on encryption and device inspection at borders).
  • Devices: for high-risk destinations, issue clean loaner laptops and phones with only the data needed, then wipe or rebuild them on return. Keep devices with you; hotel rooms and safes are not secure.
  • Connections: use the corporate VPN or a personal hotspot rather than hotel or public Wi-Fi; do not use public charging ports or accept gifted USB devices.
  • Behavior: be careful what is discussed in public places and on social media during the trip; watch for approaches that may be intelligence gathering.
  • Support: a 24-hour contact for medical, security or legal emergencies, and travel insurance with evacuation cover.

Security training and awareness

Objective 7.15Teaching people how they are targeted and what to do, so they protect themselves and the organization.

Awareness programs (designed on the 1.11–1.12 page) should cover the threats people face personally:

  • Insider threat: recognizing and reporting concerning behavior (unusual access requests, attempts to bypass controls, signs of coercion), through a confidential channel, in a culture that treats reporting as protective rather than punitive.
  • Social media impacts: oversharing job roles, travel plans, office photos and badge pictures gives attackers material for phishing, impersonation and physical intrusion; executives and their families can be targeted.
  • MFA fatigue (2FA fatigue, push bombing): attackers with a stolen password send repeated push prompts until the user approves one. Teach users never to approve a prompt they did not start, and to report it; technical fixes are number matching and phishing-resistant MFA.
  • Physical social engineering: tailgating, fake contractors and delivery staff, and lost USB drives left as bait.
  • Deepfake voice and video: verify unusual requests for payments, credentials or access through a known channel.

Emergency management and duress

Objective 7.15Keeping people safe in emergencies and giving them safe ways to signal and comply when threatened.

Emergency management covers fires, severe weather, medical emergencies, active threats and site evacuations. An emergency action plan sets: how emergencies are reported, alarms and warnings, evacuation routes and assembly points, how everyone is accounted for afterwards, shelter-in-place procedures, and roles such as floor wardens. Drills test it; emergency lighting, alarms and exit signs must work; and doors on escape routes must let people out even when power or access systems fail.

Duress is being forced, under threat, to act against the organization, for example a store manager made to open a safe or an administrator forced to log in:

  • Duress codes and alarms: a special PIN or code word that appears to work normally but silently raises an alarm; panic buttons for staff at counters and in cash rooms.
  • Policy: staff are told to comply and stay safe; no asset is worth an injury. Security then responds to the silent alarm.
  • Design: time-delay safes, two-person rules and limited cash on hand reduce what any one coerced person can give up, which also reduces the incentive to coerce them.

Life safety always wins. Whenever a question pits people's safety against protecting assets, data or evidence, safety comes first: evacuate, comply under duress, and let doors fail open on escape routes.

The retail group's store safes have time delays and a duress PIN linked to its monitoring center, and each store runs an evacuation drill twice a year with every staff member accounted for.

Protecting executives and high-risk staff

Objective 7.15Reducing the personal exposure of people attackers are most likely to target.

  • Who: executives, finance and payment staff, administrators, HR, public-facing spokespeople, and anyone with access to large amounts of money or data.
  • Digital exposure: review what is public about them (home addresses, family members, travel, personal accounts) and help remove what is unnecessary; doxxing can lead to harassment or physical threats.
  • Personal accounts: attackers target personal email, social media and phone accounts to reach work; offer guidance and tools for strong personal security, such as passkeys and carrier account PINs against SIM swapping.
  • Impersonation: monitor for fake profiles and lookalike domains using executives' names, and agree verification procedures for unusual requests made in their name.
  • Physical protection: risk assessments for events and travel, and executive protection where threats justify it.

Workplace violence and threat management

Objective 7.15Recognizing and responding to threats of harm from inside or outside.

  • Policy: zero tolerance for threats and violence, a clear way to report concerns, and protection for those who report.
  • Threat assessment team: HR, security, legal, management and, where needed, mental health professionals assess reported behavior and decide on support, intervention or protective measures. CISA promotes this behavior-based approach in its insider threat guidance.
  • Warning signs: threats, grievances, fixation on a person, sudden changes in behavior, or interest in weapons or past attacks; most attackers show warning behavior before acting.
  • Domestic situations: threats from a staff member's partner or family can follow them to work; plans may include parking escorts, changed schedules and reception alerts.
  • Active threat response: training on run, hide, fight (or local equivalents), lockdown procedures, and coordination with police.
  • Terminations: risk-assessed, with access removed at the time of notice and security present where needed.

Remote and lone workers

Objective 7.15Keeping people and data safe outside the office.

  • Home working: managed devices, secure home network guidance, screens not visible to others, secure storage and shredding of papers, and no sensitive calls where others can overhear.
  • Lone workers: staff who open stores alone, work late shifts or visit sites alone need check-in procedures, lone worker alarms and an escalation process when someone fails to check in.
  • Field staff: drivers, engineers and buyers visiting suppliers get route and site risk information, and a way to call for help.
  • Health and safety: safety laws apply wherever staff work; security and safety teams share incident reports and risk assessments.

At the retail group, store staff who open alone now use a check-in app linked to the monitoring center; a missed check-in within ten minutes triggers a call and, if unanswered, a patrol visit.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the robbery
Situation
An armed robber forces a store manager to open the safe.
Best response
The manager complies to stay safe and uses the duress code if it is safe to do so; security and police respond to the silent alarm.
Why
Life safety comes first; duress codes signal without increasing danger.
Scenario 2: the push prompts
Situation
A user receives a stream of MFA push prompts at 2 a.m. and approves one to make them stop.
Best response
Report immediately; the SOC resets the password and revokes sessions. Longer term, use number matching or phishing-resistant MFA and train users never to approve unexpected prompts.
Why
MFA fatigue attacks rely on users approving prompts they did not start.
Scenario 3: the border inspection
Situation
A buyer is traveling to a country where border officials may demand access to devices.
Best response
Issue a clean loaner laptop and phone with only necessary data, brief the traveler, and wipe or rebuild the devices on return.
Why
Travel security limits what can be exposed if a device is inspected or compromised.
Scenario 4: the fire alarm and the badge doors
Situation
During a fire drill, staff find that badge-controlled doors on the escape route stay locked when power is cut.
Best response
Change the doors to fail-safe (unlock on power loss or alarm) on escape routes, and protect assets by other means.
Why
Life safety requires that people can always get out.

Key takeaways

  • Travel: destination briefings, trip registration, loaner devices wiped on return, VPN or hotspot, careful behavior, 24-hour support.
  • Awareness: insider threat reporting, social media oversharing, MFA fatigue (never approve unexpected prompts; number matching, phishing-resistant MFA), physical social engineering, deepfakes.
  • Emergencies: emergency action plans, evacuation and accounting for people, drills, fail-safe escape doors. Duress: duress codes and panic alarms, comply and stay safe, time delays and two-person rules.
  • High-risk staff: reduce public exposure, secure personal accounts, watch for impersonation, physical protection where needed.
  • Threats and remote work: threat assessment teams, warning signs, active threat response, careful terminations; home working rules, lone worker check-ins, field staff support.
