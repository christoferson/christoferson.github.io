1.6: Policies, standards, procedures and guidelines

Turning governance decisions into written rules people can follow: which document says what, how policies are written, approved and communicated, how they are enforced and excepted, and how the whole set is kept current.

Overview

Objective 1.6

1.6 Develop, document, and implement security policy, standards, procedures, and guidelines.

The three verbs are the point: develop (write and approve), document (publish in a controlled, findable form), and implement (make people and systems actually follow it). The document types are introduced in CC 1.3; governance, roles and frameworks are on the 1.3 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its security policy set grew piecemeal over ten years and the new CISO has been asked to rationalize it.

The document hierarchy

Objective 1.6Knowing what each document type does, who approves it, and whether it is mandatory.

NIST SP 800-12 describes policy as "an aggregate of directives, regulations, rules, and practices that prescribes how an organization manages, protects, and distributes information", and standards as documents that "specify uniform use of specific technologies, parameters, or procedures". The documents form a hierarchy: each lower level supports the one above it and changes more often.

  1. PolicyManagement's statement of intent and direction: what must be achieved and why. Mandatory, short, technology-neutral, approved by senior management.
  2. StandardsMandatory, specific requirements that make the policy measurable: "laptops use full-disk encryption with AES-256", "passwords used alone are at least 15 characters".
  3. BaselinesThe minimum security configuration for a type of system or a set of controls; FIPS 200 uses the term for "the set of minimum security controls" for a low, moderate or high impact system. Mandatory.
  4. ProceduresMandatory, step-by-step instructions for carrying out a task consistently: how to grant access, rotate a key, or respond to a lost laptop.
  5. GuidelinesRecommendations and good practice where judgment is allowed; not mandatory.

Only guidelines are optional. Exam questions test which document is discretionary. Policies, standards, baselines and procedures are all mandatory; guidelines are recommendations. Also note that NIST warns organizational "standards" are not to be confused with national standards such as FIPS or ISO standards.

Types of policy

Objective 1.6Choosing the right kind of policy for the scope it must cover.

NIST SP 800-12 describes three kinds:

  • Program policy (also called the organizational or master security policy): sets the strategic direction of the security program, establishes it, assigns responsibilities and resources, and is issued by a senior official. It covers purpose, scope, responsibilities and compliance.
  • Issue-specific policy: addresses one area of concern across the organization, such as acceptable use, email, remote work, bring your own device, social media or AI use. NIST notes these "must be reviewed on a regular basis due to frequent technological changes."
  • System-specific policy: states security objectives and rules for one system: who may do what on it, and how it must be configured and operated.

NIST SP 800-53 builds policy into every control family: the first control in each family ("-1", such as AC-1 or IR-1) requires a documented policy and procedures for that family, with a designated official, and a review and update frequency. A typical organization therefore has a short program policy, a set of issue-specific policies, and family-level or topic standards that map to its control framework.

Writing an effective policy

Objective 1.6Writing policies that are clear, enforceable and durable.

NIST's OT guide puts the rule simply: "policies provide answers to the questions 'what' and 'why' without dealing with 'how.' Policies are normally stated in terms that are technology-independent." Detail that changes (products, settings, steps) belongs in standards and procedures, so the policy survives technology changes without re-approval by the board.

A policy usually contains:

  • Purpose: why the policy exists and which objectives or obligations it supports.
  • Scope: who and what it applies to: employees, contractors, systems, locations, data.
  • Policy statements: the rules, in clear mandatory language ("must", "must not").
  • Roles and responsibilities: who owns, implements and checks compliance.
  • Compliance and enforcement: how compliance is measured, and the consequences of breaching it.
  • Exceptions: how to request one and who can approve it.
  • Definitions, related documents and version: owner, approval date, review date, change history.

Qualities of a good policy: short enough that people read it; clear to its audience, without jargon; realistic, so it can be followed and enforced (a policy everyone breaks teaches people that policies do not matter); consistent with law, contracts and other policies; and supported visibly by leadership.

Mandatory language must be backed by enforcement. Writing "must" for something the organization does not check or enforce creates a gap that auditors and courts will notice. Either enforce it, or move it to a guideline.

Standards, baselines, procedures and guidelines

Objective 1.6Turning policy into specific, measurable and repeatable requirements.

  • Standards make policy measurable and consistent: approved algorithms and key lengths, minimum logging events and retention, patch timelines by severity, required MFA types. They are where external requirements (PCI DSS, SP 800-53 parameters) are translated into the organization's own rules.
  • Baselines set the minimum configuration for each platform: a server build, a cloud account guardrail set, a network device template. They usually start from a recognized secure configuration guide (NIST's National Checklist Program lists many) and are tailored, then enforced and monitored for drift (objective 7.3).
  • Procedures make tasks repeatable and auditable: the joiner, mover and leaver process, incident handling playbooks, backup restore steps. Good procedures name the role, the trigger, each step, the evidence to keep, and what to do when something goes wrong. They are often automated as workflows or runbooks.
  • Guidelines help people make good choices where rules would be too rigid: how to write secure code in a given language, how to judge whether a vendor email is genuine, how to use AI assistants safely.

Traceability ties them together. Every standard and baseline points to the policy statement it implements, and every policy statement can be traced to a business objective, law, contract or risk. That makes it easy to show auditors why a control exists, and to remove rules whose reason has gone.

Developing and approving documents

Objective 1.6Running a controlled process from identifying the need to publishing an approved document.

  1. Identify the needA new law, risk, technology, audit finding or business change. Check whether an existing document can be updated instead.
  2. Draft with stakeholdersBusiness owners, IT, HR, legal, privacy, and representatives of the people who must follow it; frameworks and templates are a starting point, not the answer.
  3. ReviewLegal and HR check enforceability and employment law (works councils in some EU countries must be consulted on monitoring rules); security checks it against risk and other documents.
  4. ApproveAt the right level of authority for the document type, recorded with a date and version.
  5. PublishIn a single, controlled repository with version history, so everyone finds the current version.
  6. CommunicateAnnounce changes, train affected staff, and obtain acknowledgment where needed.

Approval by senior management is what gives a policy its authority; without it, a security team's document is only a suggestion. CSF 2.0's Govern function expects policy for managing cybersecurity risk to be established from organizational context, strategy and priorities, then communicated and enforced (GV.PO).

Implementing and enforcing

Objective 1.6Making documented rules actually happen, consistently, with a controlled way to handle exceptions.

A policy is implemented when people know it, systems enforce it where possible, compliance is checked, and breaches have consequences:

  • Awareness and training: onboarding, annual refreshers and role-based training on the policies that apply (objective 1.12).
  • Acknowledgment: staff confirm they have read key policies; SP 800-53 PL-4 requires acknowledgment of rules of behavior.
  • Technical enforcement: configuration management, access control, DLP and policy-as-code make the rule the default rather than a request.
  • Monitoring and audit: compliance checks, internal audit and metrics show whether the rule is followed (objective 6.3).
  • Consistent consequences: sanctions applied the same way at every level; enforcing rules only on junior staff undermines them.

The US Sentencing Guidelines describe what courts consider an effective compliance program, and the elements map well to security policy: standards and procedures to prevent and detect misconduct, a knowledgeable board exercising reasonable oversight, keeping known bad actors out of positions of authority, training, monitoring, auditing and anonymous reporting, consistent enforcement and incentives, and responding to and learning from violations.

Exceptions are inevitable: a legacy system that cannot meet the encryption standard, a business need that conflicts with a rule. A sound exception process requires a written request, a risk assessment, compensating controls, approval by the risk owner at the right level, an expiry date, and an entry in a central register. Exceptions that never expire are how policy quietly stops applying.

Keeping the framework current

Objective 1.6Reviewing, updating and retiring documents so the set stays accurate and usable.

  • Scheduled review: every document has an owner and a review date, commonly annually for policies; SP 800-53's "-1" controls require organization-defined review frequencies.
  • Event-driven review: after a major incident, audit finding, regulatory change, acquisition or new technology (generative AI prompted many organizations to write AI use policies quickly).
  • Version control: a single source of truth, change history, and withdrawal of superseded versions.
  • Mapping: documents mapped to control frameworks and obligations, so a new requirement shows which documents to change.
  • Pruning: retire rules that no longer have a reason; a smaller, enforced set beats a large, ignored one.

Scenario check

PracticeChoosing the right document and process in situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: where does the setting go
Situation
The security team wants to require TLS 1.3 for all new public web services and asks to add it to the Information Security Policy.
Best answer
Put it in the cryptography standard, which implements the policy's requirement to protect data in transit; leave the policy technology-neutral.
Why
Policies say what and why; standards say which specific technologies and parameters. Putting settings in policy forces board re-approval every time technology changes.
Scenario 2: the policy nobody follows
Situation
An audit finds that 70% of stores share one login for the till back office, despite a policy requiring individual accounts.
Best response
Find out why (the tills may not support individual accounts), then either fix the technology and enforce the policy, or approve time-limited exceptions with compensating controls while a fix is delivered.
Why
A policy that cannot be followed must be made workable or formally excepted; ignoring the gap leaves unmanaged risk and erodes respect for all policies.
Scenario 3: the CISO's new policy
Situation
The CISO publishes a new remote work policy on the intranet without consulting HR or obtaining executive approval. Managers ignore it.
Best answer
Withdraw and re-run the process: draft with HR, legal and business stakeholders, obtain senior management approval, then communicate and train.
Why
Policy authority comes from senior management approval; stakeholder involvement makes it workable and lawful. A security team's document without approval is only a suggestion.

Key takeaways

  • Hierarchy: policy (what and why, mandatory, senior approval) → standards (specific, mandatory) → baselines (minimum configuration, mandatory) → procedures (how, mandatory) → guidelines (recommended, optional).
  • Policy types (SP 800-12): program, issue-specific (reviewed often), system-specific; SP 800-53 "-1" controls require family policies and procedures.
  • Good policy: technology-neutral, short, clear, realistic, consistent; purpose, scope, statements, roles, compliance, exceptions, version and review date.
  • Lower documents: standards make policy measurable; baselines start from secure configuration guides; procedures make tasks repeatable; guidelines guide judgment; everything traces to a reason.
  • Develop: identify need, draft with stakeholders, legal and HR review, approve at the right level, publish in one controlled place, communicate.
  • Implement: training, acknowledgment (PL-4), technical enforcement, monitoring, consistent consequences; exceptions with risk owner approval, compensating controls and expiry.
  • Maintain: scheduled and event-driven reviews, version control, mapping to frameworks, pruning.
