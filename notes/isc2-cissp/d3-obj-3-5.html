3.5: Vulnerabilities in architectures and designs

A tour of the kinds of system an organization runs, from laptops and databases to factory controllers, containers and AI agents: where each one is typically weak, and the mitigations that matter most.

Overview

Objective 3.5

3.5 Assess and mitigate the vulnerabilities of security architectures, designs, and solution elements.

The outline lists sixteen kinds of system. For each, a CISSP question usually wants one thing: the characteristic weakness and the control that best addresses it. The basics of ICS, IoT and cloud are in CC 4.1 and CC 4.3. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its distribution centers run automated conveyors and sorting machines, and its stores have smart cameras and shelf sensors.

Client, server and database systems

Objective 3.5Recognizing the weaknesses of endpoints, servers and databases and how each is protected.

  • Client-based systems: laptops, phones and desktop applications are exposed to users, email and the web. Weaknesses include unpatched browsers and plug-ins, local data caches, stored credentials and malicious downloads. Mitigations: hardened builds, patching, endpoint detection and response, encryption, and least privilege for local users.
  • Server-based systems: high-value targets reachable over the network. Weaknesses include unnecessary services and open ports, default accounts, misconfiguration and slow patching. Mitigations: hardening to a baseline, removing what is not needed, privileged access management, and monitoring.
  • Database systems: besides injection and excessive privileges, two classic risks come from the data itself. Aggregation is combining many items of lower sensitivity into something more sensitive (store-by-store sales figures that together reveal the group's unpublished results). Inference is deducing protected information from what a user can see (spotting a planned store closure from a sudden drop in its stock orders). Mitigations: least privilege and views, separating duties, restricting query patterns, and polyinstantiation: keeping different versions of a record at different classification levels so that lower-cleared users do not learn that a protected version exists.

Aggregation vs inference. Aggregation is about combining data to get something more sensitive; inference is about deducing hidden facts from visible ones. NIST's glossary uses both words in other senses (log consolidation, re-identification), so answer in the database-security sense.

Cryptographic systems and quantum computing

Objective 3.5Knowing that cryptographic systems usually fail through implementation and key handling, and preparing for quantum computers.

Strong algorithms are rarely broken directly. Cryptographic systems fail through weak or deprecated algorithms and key lengths, poor random number generation, hard-coded or badly stored keys, implementation bugs, and side-channel attacks: "an attack enabled by the leakage of information from a physical cryptosystem" (NIST), such as timing, power use or electromagnetic emissions. Mitigations are validated modules (FIPS 140-3, objective 3.3), keys in hardware, crypto agility (being able to swap algorithms without redesigning systems) and an inventory of where cryptography is used.

Quantum computing threatens today's public-key algorithms (RSA, Diffie-Hellman and elliptic curve), because a large enough quantum computer could solve the mathematical problems they rely on. The worry for long-lived secrets is harvest now, decrypt later: attackers record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it once such a machine exists. NIST approved the first post-quantum standards in August 2024 (FIPS 203 ML-KEM, FIPS 204 ML-DSA and FIPS 205 SLH-DSA), and its transition plan (IR 8547, final October 2026) says it "will deprecate quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography after 2030 and disallow its use under NIST standards and guidelines after 2035." The algorithms themselves are on the 3.6 page.

OT, ICS, IoT and embedded systems

Objective 3.5Securing systems that control physical processes or run on small, long-lived devices.

  • Operational technology and industrial control systems (SCADA, PLCs, HMIs): built for safety and availability, long-lived, hard to patch and often using protocols without authentication. NIST SP 800-82 Rev. 3 notes that OT "prioritizes safety, followed by availability, integrity, and confidentiality" (Rev. 4 was released as a draft in September 2026). CISA's recurring advisories describe attackers reaching internet-exposed PLCs and HMIs with default passwords. Mitigations: remove internet exposure, change defaults, segment IT from OT (Purdue levels, ISA/IEC 62443 zones and conduits, data diodes), secure remote access with phishing-resistant MFA, and plan for manual operation.
  • Internet of Things: cheap connected devices with weak defaults, little or no update support and limited logging. NIST IR 8259A lists the capabilities to expect: device identification, device configuration, data protection, logical access to interfaces, software update, and cybersecurity state awareness. Put IoT on its own network segment.
  • Embedded systems: computers built into another product (a till's card reader, a conveyor controller, a car). Firmware is hard to inspect and update and may contain hard-coded credentials, so secure boot, signed updates and a vendor support commitment matter.

The retail group keeps its distribution center controllers on an OT network behind a firewall with no direct internet path, gives vendors remote access only through a monitored jump host with MFA, and puts store cameras and sensors on a separate IoT network that cannot reach the tills.

Cloud, distributed, edge and HPC systems

Objective 3.5Understanding the weaknesses that come from sharing infrastructure and spreading processing across many locations.

  • Cloud-based systems: IaaS, PaaS, SaaS and serverless / function as a service (code that runs on demand with no servers to manage). The main weaknesses are misconfiguration, over-privileged identities, exposed keys and APIs, and confusion about the shared responsibility model. With serverless the provider runs the operating system, but the customer still owns the code, its dependencies, its permissions and the event inputs that trigger it. NIST SP 800-144 is clear that the organization remains accountable for the outsourced service.
  • Distributed systems: components on many machines communicating over a network. Weaknesses: more communication paths to protect, trust between nodes, keeping data consistent, and getting authentication right between services. Mitigations: mutual authentication, encrypted channels and central logging.
  • Edge computing: processing near where data is produced (in a store or a factory) rather than in a central data center; NIST describes the related idea of fog computing as a "distributed and federated computer model". Edge devices are often physically exposed and managed remotely, so they need tamper resistance, secure boot, encryption and central management.
  • High-performance computing: clusters built for speed, with shared file systems, many users and performance pressure against security tools. NIST SP 800-223 (2024) gives a zone-based reference architecture, and SP 800-234 (May 2026) is an HPC overlay that tailors 60 controls from the SP 800-53B moderate baseline.

Virtualization, microservices and containers

Objective 3.5Protecting the hypervisor and container layers that many workloads share.

  • Virtualized systems: a hypervisor is "the virtualization component that manages the guest OSs on a host" and controls their access to hardware (NIST SP 800-125). A Type 1 hypervisor runs directly on the hardware; a Type 2 runs on a host operating system. The worst case is VM escape, where code in one guest breaks out to the hypervisor and so to every other guest. Other risks: VM sprawl (unmanaged, unpatched virtual machines), insecure snapshots and images, and the management console as a single point of control. Mitigations: patch and harden the hypervisor, restrict and monitor management access, separate management networks, and keep workloads of different sensitivity on different hosts.
  • Microservices: an application split into small services that talk over APIs. More APIs and service-to-service traffic mean more to authenticate and authorize; use an API gateway, mutual TLS or a service mesh, and per-service least privilege.
  • Containers: "a form of operating system virtualization combined with application software packaging" (NIST SP 800-190). Containers share the host's kernel, so isolation is weaker than between virtual machines. Risks come from vulnerable or untrusted images, secrets baked into images, over-privileged containers and exposed orchestrators. Mitigations: trusted and scanned images, small base images, no root, read-only file systems, signed images, and hardening the orchestrator and host. Kubernetes security is covered in depth in the KCSA guide.

Supply chain and autonomous systems

Objective 3.5Allowing for weaknesses inherited from suppliers and for systems that act on their own.

Supply chain risk in an architecture means every component, library, firmware image, cloud service and AI model brings its supplier's weaknesses with it. Design responses are SBOMs, verified provenance and signatures, limiting what any one component can reach, and avoiding single points of supplier failure (management of the risk is on the 1.11 page).

Autonomous systems take actions without a human approving each step: robots and automated vehicles in warehouses, and increasingly AI agents that call tools and APIs. Their weaknesses are manipulated inputs (including prompt injection for AI agents), unsafe actions when something unexpected happens, over-broad permissions, and weak identity for the agent itself. NIST's Center for AI Standards and Innovation started an initiative on AI agent standards in 2026, including agent identity and authentication. Mitigations: give each agent its own identity and least privilege, require human approval for high-impact actions, set safe failure behavior, log every action, and test against adversarial inputs (AI threats are covered under objective 3.7).

Same pattern every time. For any system type, ask: what is exposed, what is shared, what is hard to patch, and who is accountable. Most answers come back to segmentation, least privilege, hardening, patching and monitoring.

Assessing an architecture for vulnerabilities

Objective 3.5Running a structured review of a design across all its system types, rather than checking components one by one.

Real systems combine many of the types above: the retail group's online store has browsers and mobile apps (clients), APIs and containers (microservices), a database, a CDN, cloud functions, payment cryptography, and suppliers' scripts on its pages. An architecture assessment looks at the whole:

  1. Model the architectureComponents, data flows, trust boundaries, and who operates each part (organization, cloud provider, supplier).
  2. Classify each elementBy system type, so the typical weaknesses of each are checked (this page's sections are a checklist).
  3. Threat model the flowsSTRIDE or attack trees at each boundary (objective 1.10).
  4. Check shared and inherited partsHypervisors, identity providers, CI/CD, libraries and suppliers that many components depend on, since one weakness there affects everything.
  5. Test assumptionsConfiguration reviews, penetration tests and cloud posture checks confirm that the design is built as intended.
  6. Prioritize and mitigateRate findings by risk, fix design flaws before go-live, and record accepted risks with owners.

Cross-cutting weaknesses appear in almost every architecture: excessive privilege, missing authentication between internal components, unencrypted internal traffic, secrets in code or configuration, single points of failure, unmonitored management interfaces, and dependence on one supplier or region. Checking these first finds most serious issues quickly.

Mitigation hierarchy. Prefer removing the weakness (eliminate the feature, reduce exposure), then designing it out (isolation, least privilege), then adding controls (monitoring, WAF rules), and only then accepting the residual risk.

Database security in depth

Objective 3.5Integrity, concurrency and inference controls in database systems.

  • ACID: transactions are atomic (all or nothing), consistent (move the database between valid states), isolated (concurrent transactions do not see each other's partial work) and durable (committed changes survive failures). Commit and rollback enforce this.
  • Concurrency control: locking prevents lost updates (two users overwrite each other) and dirty reads (reading uncommitted data); deadlocks must be detected and resolved.
  • Relational integrity: a primary key uniquely identifies each row; a foreign key references a primary key in another table; referential integrity makes sure references are valid; semantic integrity enforces allowed values.
  • Views: present a subset of data to each user or role, a form of content-dependent access control.
  • Aggregation: combining individually harmless data into something sensitive. Inference: deducing sensitive information from data the user is allowed to see. Countermeasures: polyinstantiation (different versions of a record at different classification levels), cell suppression, adding noise or perturbation, partitioning the database, and query restrictions.
  • Data warehouses and data mining: combining data from many sources creates a high-value target and aggregation risk, so warehouses need strong access control, masking and monitoring.
  • Interfaces: ODBC and JDBC connections need encrypted transport and least-privilege service accounts; stored procedures and parameterized queries limit what applications can do.

Common design flaws

Objective 3.5Weaknesses that appear across many kinds of system.

  • Time-of-check to time-of-use (TOCTOU) race conditions: a condition checked at one moment changes before it is used.
  • Maintenance hooks and backdoors: undocumented access left by developers for support; removed before release and found by code review.
  • Covert channels: storage and timing channels that leak data across isolation boundaries (objective 3.2).
  • Emanations: electromagnetic signals from equipment that can be captured to reconstruct data; countered by shielding (TEMPEST standards), distance and white noise.
  • Input validation failures: buffer overflows and injection in any system that accepts input (objective 8.5).
  • Data remanence: data left on reused storage or in shared cloud resources after deletion; countered by encryption and sanitization.
  • Single points of failure and fail-open designs: components whose failure removes protection.

Cloud deployment models (NIST SP 800-145): public (shared by many customers), private (one organization), community (a group with shared concerns) and hybrid (two or more combined). Public and community models rely on strong multitenant isolation. Industrial protocols such as Modbus and DNP3 were designed without authentication; secure variants (such as DNP3 Secure Authentication) and segmentation are needed.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the shared database
Situation
Three applications with different sensitivity share one database server and one database account.
Best response
Separate the data (different databases or schemas with separate accounts and least privilege), and isolate the most sensitive application.
Why
Least common mechanism and least privilege: a compromise of the least protected application should not expose the others' data.
Scenario 2: the internet-facing controller
Situation
A scan finds a distribution center's HMI reachable from the internet with its default password.
Best response
Remove the internet exposure immediately, change the credentials, check for signs of compromise, and route remote access through a monitored jump host with MFA.
Why
CISA's OT guidance puts removing internet exposure and changing defaults first; OT compromise can affect safety.
Scenario 3: the container image
Situation
Developers build production containers from a public base image pulled by the "latest" tag, running as root.
Best response
Use trusted, scanned, minimal base images pinned by digest, run as a non-root user with a read-only file system, and sign images.
Why
Containers share the host kernel; untrusted images and root privileges turn one flaw into a host compromise.
Scenario 4: the AI agent
Situation
A customer service AI agent can issue refunds of any size and reads customers' emails as input.
Best response
Limit its permissions (small refunds only, larger ones need human approval), give it its own identity, log every action, and test it against prompt injection hidden in emails.
Why
Autonomous systems need least privilege and human approval for high-impact actions; untrusted input can manipulate them.

Key takeaways

  • Clients and servers: harden, patch, least privilege, monitor. Databases: aggregation (combining) and inference (deducing); mitigate with views, least privilege and polyinstantiation.
  • Crypto systems fail through implementation, keys and side channels; use validated modules and crypto agility. Quantum: harvest now, decrypt later; FIPS 203/204/205 (2024); IR 8547: quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms deprecated after 2030, disallowed after 2035.
  • OT/ICS: safety and availability first; no internet exposure, segmentation (Purdue, 62443), secure remote access. IoT and embedded: weak defaults and updates; segment, secure boot, signed updates.
  • Cloud and serverless: misconfiguration and identities; the customer stays accountable. Distributed and edge: more paths and exposed devices. HPC: SP 800-223 zones, SP 800-234 overlay.
  • Virtualization: VM escape and sprawl; protect the hypervisor and its console. Containers share a kernel; trusted images, no root, harden the orchestrator. Microservices: authenticate every API call.
  • Supply chain: every component brings its supplier's risk. Autonomous systems and AI agents: own identity, least privilege, human approval for high-impact actions, logging.
