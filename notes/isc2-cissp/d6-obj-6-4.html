Overview

Objective 6.4

6.4 Analyze test/audit output and generate report. Sub-topics: remediation; exception handling; responsible disclosure.

Test techniques are on the 6.2 page; audits on the 6.5 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its annual PCI DSS assessment is due in three months, and last year's penetration test left 14 findings open.

Analyzing output and reporting

Objective 6.4Turning raw test and audit results into validated, prioritized findings and reports each audience can act on.

  1. ValidateRemove false positives and duplicates; confirm each finding with evidence.
  2. RateAssess risk in context: severity scores (such as CVSS), plus exploitability (is it on CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list?), exposure and the asset's value.
  3. Find root causesGroup findings by cause (a missing patch process, a weak build image) so the fix addresses the cause, not each symptom.
  4. ReportAn executive summary of risk and trends for leadership; detailed findings, evidence and remediation steps for technical owners; the scope, method and limitations so readers know what was not tested.
  5. TrackEach finding gets an owner, a due date by risk, and retesting to close it.

Test reports are sensitive: they are a map of the organization's weaknesses. Classify them, limit distribution, and protect them in transit and storage.

Remediation and exception handling

Objective 6.4Fixing findings by risk-based deadlines, and handling those that cannot be fixed through documented, time-limited exceptions.

Remediation follows the risk: critical and actively exploited issues within days, others within set service levels. Fixes are verified by retesting, and closed only with evidence. In the federal model, open weaknesses and their planned fixes are tracked in a plan of action and milestones (POA&M, SP 800-53 CA-5).

Exception handling covers findings that cannot be fixed on time or at all (a legacy system, a vendor dependency, a business need):

  • A written request stating the reason, the risk and any compensating controls.
  • Approval by the risk owner at the right level of authority, not by the team that wants the exception.
  • An expiry date and a review before renewal; exceptions without end dates become permanent risk.
  • A central register so the total accepted risk is visible.

Exceptions are risk acceptance. An exception is a formal decision to accept a risk, so it must be made by someone with authority to accept it, informed by the facts, and recorded. Silently leaving a finding open is not an exception; it is unmanaged risk.

Responsible disclosure

Objective 6.4Receiving and handling vulnerability reports from outsiders, and disclosing vulnerabilities you find in others' products responsibly.

Responsible (or coordinated) disclosure means a vulnerability is reported privately to the vendor or owner, who fixes it within a reasonable time before details are made public, so users can patch before attackers learn of it.

  • Vulnerability disclosure policy (VDP): tells researchers how to report, what is in scope, what testing is allowed, and that good-faith research will not lead to legal action. CISA's BOD 20-01 required US federal agencies to publish one.
  • Contact point: a security.txt file (RFC 9116) on the website tells researchers where to send reports.
  • Process: ISO/IEC 29147 covers receiving reports and publishing advisories; ISO/IEC 30111 covers handling them internally (triage, verification, fixing). Acknowledge quickly, keep the reporter informed, agree a disclosure date, credit them, and publish an advisory with a CVE identifier.
  • Bug bounties: a VDP plus payment for valid findings, run directly or through a platform.

The other direction matters too: when your testers find a flaw in a vendor's product, report it to the vendor (or a coordinator such as CISA or a CERT) under the same principles rather than publishing it.

Writing effective reports

Objective 6.4Producing reports that each audience can understand and act on.

  • Executive summary: overall risk, the few findings that matter most, trends since the last assessment, and decisions needed, in business terms.
  • Scope and method: what was tested, when, how, and what was not, so no one assumes more assurance than exists.
  • Findings: each with a clear title, affected assets, evidence, risk rating with reasoning, business impact, and practical remediation advice.
  • Positive observations: controls that worked, which helps leadership see what to keep investing in.
  • Appendices: technical detail, tool output and reproduction steps for engineers.

Reports are sensitive: classify them, share on a need-to-know basis, and store them securely. Ratings should use a consistent method across reports so findings can be compared and tracked.

Rating findings consistently

Objective 6.4Using one method to rate findings so that reports can be compared and deadlines applied.

Different testers, scanners and auditors use different scales. The organization should translate every finding into its own risk rating, so that remediation deadlines and reports are consistent:

  • Likelihood: exploitability, exposure (internet-facing or internal), exploitation evidence (KEV, threat intelligence), and the skill and access an attacker needs.
  • Impact: what the affected asset supports (card data, personal data, safety, revenue), drawn from asset classification and the BIA.
  • Compensating controls: segmentation, WAF rules or monitoring that reduce likelihood or impact, documented and verified.
  • Defined levels: each level (critical, high, medium, low) has a written definition and a remediation deadline in policy, for example critical within days, high within weeks, medium at the next scheduled patch cycle.

Ratings are agreed with system owners, not imposed: the tester knows the technical severity, but the owner knows the business context. Disagreements are escalated to the risk owner rather than left unresolved, and the final rating and the reasoning are recorded.

Validation matters as much as rating. False positives waste remediation effort and erode trust in the testing program; false negatives (missed issues) give false assurance and are found only through other methods, deeper testing or incidents. Testers confirm findings by manual checks or proof of exploitation, and the program tracks false positive rates per tool.

Root causes and trend reporting

Objective 6.4Looking beyond individual findings to the causes and patterns behind them.

Fixing each finding one at a time treats symptoms. Analysis across findings asks why they exist:

  • Group findings by cause: missing patches on one platform may point to no owner or no maintenance window; repeated injection flaws to missing framework use or training; misconfigurations to the absence of hardened baselines.
  • Techniques: the "five whys", cause-and-effect diagrams, and comparison with previous assessments.
  • Systemic fixes: baselines, automation, ownership, pipeline checks and training remove whole classes of findings.
  • Trends: counts and age of open findings by severity, time to remediate, recurrence rate, and findings per assessment over time, so leadership can see whether risk is falling.

At the retail group, 9 of last year's 14 open penetration test findings came from servers built by hand outside the standard images; the remediation plan fixed the 14, but the lasting fix was a rule that every server is built from a hardened image through the pipeline.

Communicating results to leadership

Objective 6.4Presenting assessment results so that leaders can make risk decisions.

  • Lead with risk and decisions: what could happen to the business, how likely it is, and what leadership is asked to approve (budget, deadline, risk acceptance).
  • Use business language: "attackers could reach the card environment from a store network" rather than a list of CVEs.
  • Show trends and context: improvement or decline since the last assessment, and comparison with risk appetite and tolerances.
  • Be honest about limits: scope exclusions, time constraints and untested areas, so leadership does not assume more assurance than the work gives.
  • Keep it short: one or two pages with a clear rating, the top issues and the plan; details in appendices or for owners.

Reports to the board or audit committee often combine results from several sources (penetration tests, internal audit, external audits, metrics) into one view of security posture against the risk register.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the vendor cannot patch
Situation
A critical finding in a vendor product cannot be fixed until the vendor releases a patch in three months.
Best response
Raise a time-limited exception approved by the risk owner, with compensating controls (isolation, WAF rule, monitoring) and a tracking date.
Why
Exceptions are formal risk acceptance with an expiry, not silence.
Scenario 2: the researcher's email
Situation
An external researcher emails the CEO about a vulnerability in the website and threatens to publish in 7 days.
Best response
Acknowledge quickly through the security contact, verify and fix the issue, agree a disclosure timeline, and use the VDP's terms.
Why
Coordinated disclosure works best with a published VDP and a clear contact (security.txt).
Scenario 3: the same findings again
Situation
The annual penetration test repeats six findings from last year.
Best response
Analyze root causes (missing ownership, no patch process for that platform), fix them, and track remediation to verified closure with retests.
Why
Remediation is only complete when verified; recurring findings point to process problems.
Scenario 4: the 200-page report
Situation
The board receives the full penetration test report and asks what it means for them.
Best response
Give an executive summary of risk, trends and decisions needed; keep technical detail for owners and engineers.
Why
Reports must be written for their audience.

Key takeaways

  • Analysis: validate, rate in context (KEV, exposure, value), find root causes, report by audience with scope and limits, track to closure; protect the reports.
  • Remediation by risk-based deadlines, verified by retest; POA&Ms (CA-5). Exceptions: written, compensating controls, approved by the risk owner, expiry, central register.
  • Responsible disclosure: private report, fix, then publish; VDP with safe harbor (BOD 20-01), security.txt (RFC 9116), ISO/IEC 29147 and 30111, CVEs, bug bounties.
  • Reports: executive summary, scope and method, evidence-based findings with ratings and remediation, positive observations, appendices.
