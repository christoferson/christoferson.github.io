Overview

Objective 7.7

7.7 Operate and maintain detection and preventative measures. Sub-topics: firewalls (next generation, web application, network); intrusion detection and prevention systems; allowlisting and denylisting; third-party provided security services (DDoS scrubbing, managed security service providers); sandboxing; honeypots and honeynets; endpoint protection (anti-malware, EDR, XDR); machine learning and artificial intelligence based tools.

Firewall types and endpoint controls in network design are on the 4.2 page; SIEM and SOAR on the 7.2 page; incident handling on the 7.6 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its online store sits behind a WAF and a DDoS scrubbing service, stores connect through next-generation firewalls, and every laptop and server runs EDR.

Network defenses: firewalls, IDS and IPS, lists

Objective 7.7Keeping preventive and detective network controls effective through rule management, tuning and updates.

  • Network firewalls: stateful packet filtering between zones, deny by default. Operating them means reviewing rules regularly, removing unused and overly broad rules, documenting the owner and reason for each, and change control for every rule change.
  • Next-generation firewalls (NGFW): add application awareness, user identity, intrusion prevention, TLS inspection and threat intelligence feeds to the same device.
  • Web application firewalls (WAF): filter HTTP in front of web applications and APIs against injection, cross-site scripting and bots; a stopgap ("virtual patch") while code is fixed, not a substitute for fixing it.
  • IDS and IPS: keep signatures current, tune to the environment, place sensors where they see the important traffic (including east-west), and decide carefully what an inline IPS may block, since a false positive blocks legitimate business.
  • Allowlists and denylists: a denylist blocks known-bad items (addresses, domains, file hashes) and lets everything else through; an allowlist permits only approved items and blocks the rest. Allowlists are stronger but need maintenance; denylists are easy but miss anything new.

Third-party services, sandboxing and deception

Objective 7.7Using outside services, safe detonation and decoys to extend what the organization can detect and withstand.

  • DDoS scrubbing services: traffic is routed (by DNS or BGP) through a provider's large network, which filters attack traffic and forwards clean traffic. Arrange it before an attack, test failover, and make sure origin servers accept traffic only from the provider.
  • Managed security service providers (MSSPs) and managed detection and response: outsourced monitoring and response, often around the clock. The contract must define what they monitor, response times, what actions they may take on your systems, escalation, and access to your logs; accountability stays with the organization.
  • Sandboxing: running suspicious files or links in an isolated environment to observe their behavior before delivery, as in email and web gateways. Some malware detects sandboxes and waits, so sandboxing is one layer, not a guarantee.
  • Honeypots and honeynets: decoy systems (or whole networks) with no legitimate use, so any interaction is suspicious. They give early warning and intelligence about attackers' methods. Keep them isolated so they cannot become a launch point, and get legal advice: using them to detect is fine; luring someone into committing a crime they would not otherwise commit (entrapment) is not. Honeytokens (fake credentials or records) apply the same idea to data.

Endpoint protection and AI-based tools

Objective 7.7Operating endpoint defenses and understanding what machine learning tools can and cannot do.

  • Anti-malware: signature and behavior-based scanning; keep engines and definitions updated and alert when protection is disabled.
  • EDR: continuous recording of endpoint activity, behavioral detection, remote investigation and isolation. Its value depends on coverage (every endpoint and server, monitored) and on someone responding to its alerts. CISA's red team assessments include cases where EDR blocked the initial phishing attempt.
  • XDR: correlates endpoint, network, email, identity and cloud telemetry to see whole attack chains and respond across them.
  • ML and AI-based tools: anomaly detection, alert triage and summarization, phishing classification, and assistants that help analysts query data. They find patterns at scale but produce false positives and negatives, can be evaded by adversarial inputs (NIST AI 100-2), and need human review of consequential actions. Treat their outputs as evidence to check, protect their data and models, and measure their accuracy like any other control.

Preventive and detective together. Firewalls, IPS, allowlists and anti-malware try to stop attacks; IDS, EDR, honeypots and SIEM detect what gets through. A strong answer pairs them, because prevention alone eventually fails silently.

Email and web protection

Objective 7.7Defending the two channels most attacks arrive through.

  • Email authentication: SPF lists the servers allowed to send for a domain, DKIM signs messages, and DMARC tells receivers what to do when both fail (monitor, quarantine or reject) and sends reports. A DMARC policy of reject stops attackers sending email that appears to come from your domain.
  • Email gateways: filter spam and malware, sandbox attachments, rewrite and check links at click time, and flag external senders and lookalike domains.
  • Secure web gateways and proxies: filter web traffic by category and reputation, inspect downloads, and enforce policy for users wherever they work.
  • Protective DNS: resolvers that refuse to resolve known malicious domains, cheaply blocking malware callbacks and phishing sites for every device using them.
  • Browser isolation: risky sites rendered remotely so that code never runs on the endpoint.

None of these stops every phishing message, which is why phishing-resistant MFA, endpoint protection and an easy way for staff to report suspicious messages are layered behind them.

Protecting cloud workloads

Objective 7.7Applying preventive and detective controls where there is no physical network to guard.

  • Cloud network controls: security groups and network access control lists act as distributed firewalls around each workload; cloud firewalls and WAFs at the edge protect public services.
  • Posture management: cloud security posture management (CSPM) tools continuously check configurations (public storage, open management ports, missing encryption) against policy.
  • Workload protection: agents or agentless scanning for vulnerabilities and malware on virtual machines and containers, and runtime detection for unexpected processes.
  • Provider threat detection: native services analyze control plane logs, DNS and flow data for signs of compromise, such as access from unusual locations or cryptocurrency mining.
  • Identity as the perimeter: most cloud breaches use stolen credentials or excessive permissions, so identity detections and least privilege are as important as network controls.

Findings from these tools go to the same SIEM and incident process as everything else, so the SOC sees one picture across data center and cloud.

Keeping defenses effective

Objective 7.7Maintaining preventive and detective controls so they keep working as the environment changes.

  • Health monitoring: alert when a sensor, agent or log source stops reporting, when protection is disabled, or when signatures are out of date.
  • Coverage: compare the asset inventory with the list of protected systems; unprotected endpoints and servers are where attackers go.
  • Rule review: firewall, WAF, IPS and allowlist rules reviewed on a schedule, with owners, reasons and expiry dates for temporary rules.
  • Testing: breach and attack simulation, purple teaming and penetration tests confirm that controls block and detect what they should (objective 6.2).
  • Updates: firmware and software of security devices patched promptly, since internet-facing security appliances are a frequent target of exploitation.
  • Measures: blocked versus detected attacks, false positive rates, coverage percentage and mean time to respond.

At the retail group, a coverage check found that 7% of store servers had no working EDR agent after an update failed; agent health is now a dashboard metric with a daily alert.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the blocking IPS
Situation
A new inline IPS signature starts blocking checkout traffic on the online store.
Best response
Switch the signature to alert-only, restore service, tune it with the application team, and re-enable blocking after testing.
Why
Inline prevention can cause outages; tune and test before blocking.
Scenario 2: the spoofed domain
Situation
Customers receive phishing emails that appear to come from the company's own domain.
Best response
Publish SPF and DKIM, then move DMARC to quarantine and reject after monitoring reports.
Why
DMARC with a reject policy stops direct spoofing of your domain.
Scenario 3: the honeypot alert
Situation
A decoy file server that no one should use receives a login from an internal workstation.
Best response
Treat it as a high-confidence alert: investigate the workstation and account as a possible compromise.
Why
Honeypots have no legitimate use, so any interaction is suspicious.
Scenario 4: the MDR provider
Situation
The MDR provider wants permission to isolate any host it believes is compromised, at any time.
Best response
Agree in the contract which actions it may take automatically (for example isolating workstations) and which need your approval (critical servers), with escalation contacts.
Why
Outsourcing detection keeps accountability with the organization; contracts define the provider's authority.

Key takeaways

  • Network defenses: firewall rule hygiene, NGFW, WAF as virtual patching, tuned IDS and IPS; allowlists stronger than denylists.
  • Services and deception: DDoS scrubbing arranged in advance; MSSP contracts define actions and keep accountability; sandboxing is one layer; honeypots, honeynets and honeytokens detect and inform (no entrapment).
  • Endpoints and AI: anti-malware, EDR, XDR with full coverage and response; ML tools help at scale but are fallible and attackable; pair prevention with detection.
  • Email and web: SPF, DKIM, DMARC reject; gateways with sandboxing and link checks; secure web gateways, protective DNS, browser isolation.
  • Cloud and upkeep: security groups, CSPM, workload protection, provider detections, identity as the perimeter; health monitoring, coverage checks, rule reviews, testing, prompt updates.
