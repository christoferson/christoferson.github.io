7.9: Understanding and participating in change management

Making sure every change to production is assessed, approved, tested, recorded and reversible, whether it goes through a change board or a pipeline.

Overview

Objective 7.9

7.9 Understand and participate in change management processes. The objective has no sub-bullets.

Configuration baselines are on the 7.3 page; patching on the 7.8 page; the development pipeline on the 8.1 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its change board meets weekly, and about 1,200 changes a month reach production, most of them automated pipeline deployments.

Change management

Objective 7.9Assessing, approving, testing, implementing and recording changes so they do not introduce outages or weaknesses.

SP 800-53 CM-3 (configuration change control) requires organizations to define which changes are controlled, review and approve or reject them with security and privacy impact analysis, document decisions, implement only approved changes, keep records, monitor change activity, and coordinate it through a change control board: "a group of qualified people with responsibility for the process of regulating and approving changes" (SP 800-128).

  1. RequestDescribe the change, its reason, the systems affected, and the implementation and rollback plans.
  2. Assess impact"Analyze changes to the system to determine potential security and privacy impacts prior to change implementation" (CM-4), plus operational risk and dependencies.
  3. ApproveBy the change board or delegated approvers, according to risk; routine changes can be pre-approved as standard changes.
  4. Test"Test, validate, and document changes ... before finalizing" (CM-3(2)), in a separate test environment (CM-4(1)).
  5. ImplementIn the agreed window, by authorized people only (CM-5 restricts who can make changes), with communication to those affected.
  6. Review and recordConfirm success, update baselines, documentation and the CMDB, and close the record; back out if verification fails.
  • Emergency changes: for urgent fixes and incident response, a faster path with a smaller approval group, but still documented and reviewed by the board afterwards.
  • Change types: standard (pre-approved, low risk, repeatable), normal (assessed and approved each time), emergency.
  • Unauthorized changes: detected through configuration monitoring and file integrity checks (objective 7.3) and treated as possible incidents.
  • Security's role: security staff take part in change review, so security impact is considered before a change, not discovered after it.

Pipelines are change management too. In DevOps, the change process moves into the pipeline: peer review of code and infrastructure changes, automated tests and security scans as gates, and deployment logs as the record. The controls are the same; they are enforced by tools rather than meetings.

Impact analysis in practice

Objective 7.9Asking the questions that catch risky changes before they reach production.

  • What does it touch? Systems, data, interfaces and dependencies, from the CMDB and architecture diagrams; changes to shared components (identity, DNS, networks, certificates) affect far more than they appear to.
  • Does it change security? New ports, firewall rules, permissions, accounts, data flows, encryption settings or third-party connections need security review; so do changes to logging and monitoring.
  • Does it affect privacy or compliance? New uses of personal data, moves of data to new locations, or changes to the card data environment may need a privacy assessment or a compliance check.
  • What if it fails? A tested rollback plan, the time it takes, and the point after which rollback is no longer possible.
  • When and who? A window with low business impact (not peak trading), the right people available, and those affected informed.

Scaling the analysis to the risk keeps the process fast: a standard change uses a pre-approved template, while a change to the payment platform gets full security review and board approval.

Change management in cloud and DevOps

Objective 7.9Keeping control when hundreds of changes a day go through pipelines.

  • Peer review as approval: a pull request reviewed and approved by someone other than the author is the change approval for code and infrastructure as code.
  • Automated gates: tests, security scans (SAST, SCA, IaC scanning) and policy checks must pass before merge or deployment.
  • Protected branches and environments: nobody can push directly to the production branch or deploy outside the pipeline; production deployment can require an extra approval.
  • Progressive delivery: canary releases, blue-green deployment and feature flags limit how many users a bad change reaches and allow fast rollback.
  • Records: the repository history, pipeline logs and deployment records together are the change record that auditors examine.
  • Exceptions still exist: database migrations, network changes and vendor-managed systems may still need traditional change review.

Auditors accept pipeline-based change control when they can see that it is enforced (no bypass), separates duties (the author cannot approve alone), and leaves complete records.

Change, configuration, release and incident management

Objective 7.9How change management connects to the processes around it.

  • Configuration management defines the approved state; change management approves movements from it; after each change the baseline and CMDB are updated (objective 7.3).
  • Release management plans and packages groups of changes into releases and coordinates their deployment.
  • Patch management produces many changes; routine patches usually run as standard changes, urgent ones as emergency changes (objective 7.8).
  • Incident management uses the change record as a first question ("what changed?"), since many outages follow changes; containment actions during incidents go through emergency change.
  • Problem management finds root causes of repeated incidents and raises changes to fix them.

At the retail group, linking incidents to recent changes showed that a third of major outages followed changes without tested rollback plans; rollback testing is now required for every normal change to tier 1 systems.

Measuring change management

Objective 7.9Knowing whether the process prevents problems or just adds delay.

  • Change failure rate: the share of changes that cause an incident, outage or rollback (also one of the DORA software delivery measures).
  • Emergency change share: a high share of emergency changes suggests poor planning or that the normal process is too slow.
  • Unauthorized changes: changes detected by configuration monitoring with no approved record; the target is zero.
  • Lead time: time from request to implementation; if the process is too slow, people find ways around it.
  • Post-implementation reviews: completed for failed and emergency changes, with actions tracked.

A good change process is fast for low-risk changes and careful for high-risk ones. Security's goal is not to block change but to make sure the risk of each change is understood and accepted by the right person.

The change control board

Objective 7.9Who decides on changes, and how the board works.

  • Membership: representatives of operations, development, security, the business owners of affected services, and service management, chaired by a change manager. Members are senior enough to accept risk for their area.
  • Delegation: the board sets which change types it reviews; lower-risk normal changes can be approved by delegated approvers, and standard changes need no review each time.
  • Emergency approvals: a smaller emergency group (often called an emergency change advisory board) can approve urgent changes at any hour, with the full board reviewing them afterwards.
  • Decisions recorded: approve, reject, or defer with conditions (more testing, a different window), with the reasons, so that later reviews and audits can see why.
  • Forward schedule: a calendar of approved changes and freeze periods (such as the holiday trading peak) that helps avoid conflicting changes.
  • Separation of duties: the person who requests or implements a change does not approve it alone.

The board's job is to make sure risk is understood and owned before a change happens; it should spend its time on the few high-risk changes rather than rubber-stamping routine ones.

Change records and freeze periods

Objective 7.9Documenting changes properly and protecting critical business periods.

  • A useful change record: what changed, why, who requested, approved and implemented it, systems affected, test evidence, the rollback plan, the actual outcome and any incidents caused.
  • Linking: change records link to incidents, problems and configuration items, so the CMDB and baselines stay accurate.
  • Freeze periods: no normal changes during peak business times (the holiday trading season, financial year end), with only emergency changes allowed under stricter approval.
  • Pre-freeze rush: plan so that a wave of risky changes does not land just before the freeze.
  • Calendar: the forward schedule of changes shows conflicts and dependencies between teams.

Who sits on the change, and who does not

Objective 7.9Making sure security has a voice in changes without becoming a bottleneck.

  • Security reviews changes that touch security controls, data flows, external connections or privileged access, chosen by rules in the change system.
  • Low-risk standard changes are pre-approved with security requirements built into their templates.
  • Security can request a deferral when risk is unclear, with a decision deadline.
  • Security's own changes (firewall rules, SIEM rules, EDR policies) go through change management too.

Changes to AI models and prompts

Objective 7.9Applying change control to AI components that change behavior without code changes.

  • Treat models, system prompts, retrieval sources and tool permissions as configuration items, version-controlled with owners.
  • Evaluate before release: run the agreed evaluation and safety tests on every model or prompt change, and compare with the previous version.
  • Provider model updates: hosted models can change under you; pin versions where possible and retest when the provider announces changes.
  • Roll back: keep the previous version deployable, and monitor production behavior after each change.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the Friday firewall change
Situation
An engineer wants to open a firewall rule to a new partner on Friday evening without a change ticket because the partner is waiting.
Best response
Raise a change with security review of the rule and a scheduled window; if it is urgent, use the emergency process, which is still recorded and reviewed.
Why
Changes to security controls need impact analysis and approval; urgency uses the emergency path, not no path.
Scenario 2: the change found by monitoring
Situation
File integrity monitoring detects a modified configuration on a payment server with no matching change record.
Best response
Treat it as a possible incident: investigate who changed it and why, then restore the approved configuration.
Why
Unauthorized changes may be attacks; monitoring and change records together reveal them.
Scenario 3: the self-approved deployment
Situation
A developer approves their own pull request and deploys to production through the pipeline.
Best response
Enforce branch protection requiring approval by another reviewer, and restrict production deployment to the pipeline with separate approval.
Why
Separation of duties applies to pipeline-based change control.
Scenario 4: the board that delays everything
Situation
Every change, including routine patches, waits for a weekly board meeting, so teams bypass the process.
Best response
Define standard pre-approved changes for low-risk routine work and reserve board review for high-risk changes.
Why
A risk-based process keeps control without encouraging bypass.
Scenario 5: the change during the freeze
Situation
A team wants to deploy a new feature during the holiday freeze because marketing has announced it.
Best response
Defer to after the freeze, or approve as an exception by senior management with extra testing and rollback readiness.
Why
Freeze periods protect critical business times; exceptions need higher approval.

Key takeaways

  • Change management (CM-3, CM-4, CM-5): request, impact analysis, approval by a change control board, test, implement by authorized staff, review and record.
  • Types: standard (pre-approved), normal, emergency (faster, still documented and reviewed); unauthorized changes are possible incidents.
  • Impact analysis: what it touches, security and privacy effects, rollback, timing; scaled to risk.
  • DevOps: peer review as approval, automated gates, protected branches, progressive delivery, pipeline records; no bypass, separated duties.
  • Links and measures: configuration, release, patch, incident and problem management; change failure rate, emergency share, unauthorized changes, lead time.
