3.1–3.2: Secure design principles and security models

The principles that make a system secure by construction rather than by patching, how privacy and cloud architectures apply them, and the formal models that define exactly what "secure" means for confidentiality, integrity and conflicts of interest.

Overview

Objectives 3.1 and 3.2

3.1 Research, implement, and manage engineering processes using secure design principles. 3.2 Understand the fundamental concepts of security models (e.g., Biba, Star, Bell-LaPadula).

Domain 3 is about building security in. Many of the principles in the outline were introduced in the CC guide: least privilege and separation of duties in CC 3.2, defense in depth and zero trust in CC 4.2, shared responsibility in CC 4.3, and threat modeling in objective 1.10. This page links them into an engineering process and adds the formal models. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is designing a new payment platform to replace its 15-year-old system.

Secure engineering

Objective 3.1Running an engineering process that applies security principles from requirements through design, build and change.

NIST SP 800-53 control SA-8 requires organizations to "apply the following systems security and privacy engineering principles in the specification, design, development," implementation and modification of systems, and lists 33 named principles as enhancements. NIST's systems engineering guide, SP 800-160 Vol. 1 Rev. 1 (2022), "Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems", describes how to build them into the life cycle. Most trace back to Saltzer and Schroeder's 1975 paper, "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems", whose eight principles still appear on the exam:

  • Economy of mechanism: keep the design small and simple.
  • Fail-safe defaults: base access on permission, not exclusion.
  • Complete mediation: check every access to every object.
  • Open design: security must not depend on keeping the design secret.
  • Separation of privilege: require more than one condition or key.
  • Least privilege: grant only what is needed.
  • Least common mechanism: minimize mechanisms shared between users.
  • Psychological acceptability: security must be easy enough that people use it correctly.

The process starts with threat modeling at design time (objective 1.10), so the design answers the threats found, and repeats it when the design changes. The retail group's architecture board requires a threat model, a list of applied principles and the security requirements for every new system before build starts.

Principles that limit damage

Objective 3.1Applying least privilege, layered defenses, divided duties and zero trust so that one failure or one person cannot cause a major breach.

  • Least privilege: every user, process and component gets the minimum access, for the minimum time (CC 3.2).
  • Defense in depth: multiple, diverse, coordinated layers so "loss control should not rely on a single defensive element" (CC 4.2).
  • Separation of duties: no one person can complete a sensitive task alone; rotation of duties moves people between roles so misuse is more likely to be found; two-person integrity (dual control) requires two authorized people to act together for critical actions, such as accessing master keys (CC 3.2).
  • Zero trust: no implicit trust from network location; every request is authenticated, authorized and checked against policy (CC 4.2).
  • Shared responsibility: in cloud and outsourced designs, write down which party secures each layer, and design for the customer's share, which never reaches zero (CC 4.3).

In the new payment platform, payment services run with their own narrowly scoped identities, releasing code to production needs approval by a second engineer, changing the encryption key hierarchy needs two key custodians present, and every service-to-service call is authenticated even inside the data center.

Secure defaults, fail securely, keep it simple

Objective 3.1Designing systems that start secure, stay secure when something goes wrong, and are simple enough to verify.

  • Secure defaults (SA-8(23)): the as-shipped configuration enforces policy conservatively, on the principle of "deny unless explicitly authorized"; a system may refuse to run until it is configured, and if initialization fails it uses its secure defaults rather than continuing open. CISA's Secure by Design program asks vendors for the same: security features on by default, not sold as add-ons.
  • Fail securely (SA-8(24), secure failure and recovery): neither a failure nor the response to it may break the security policy. Components fail in a state that "denies rather than grants access", and recover by reconfiguring, shutting down or rolling back to a known secure state, with transactions completed or undone as a whole. A badge reader that loses power should keep a secure door locked, unless life safety requires it to open (a "fail-safe" exit door), which is a deliberate, documented exception.
  • Keep it simple and small (SA-8(7), reduced complexity): "simpler systems contain fewer vulnerabilities" and are easier to analyze. NIST notes that transitions, such as running old and new systems side by side, temporarily add complexity that must be planned for.
  • Least common mechanism (SA-8(2)): minimize components shared between users or tenants, because shared mechanisms can become paths for information to leak or a compromise to spread.

Fail-secure versus fail-safe. In exam terms, fail-secure (fail closed) protects assets by denying access on failure; fail-safe (fail open) protects people, for example unlocking fire exits. When human safety is at stake, safety wins.

Privacy by design

Objective 3.1Building privacy into systems from the start, with defaults that protect personal data and technologies that reduce exposure.

Ann Cavoukian, as Ontario's privacy commissioner, set out seven foundational principles of privacy by design: proactive not reactive; privacy as the default setting; privacy embedded into the design; full functionality (positive-sum, not zero-sum); end-to-end security across the whole life cycle; visibility and transparency; and respect for user privacy. GDPR Article 25 makes the idea law as data protection by design and by default: controllers must build in "appropriate technical and organisational measures, such as pseudonymisation", and "by default, only personal data which are necessary for each specific purpose" are processed.

Privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) reduce how much personal data is exposed while still allowing useful processing. Common examples are pseudonymization and tokenization, differential privacy, homomorphic encryption, secure multiparty computation, federated learning, trusted execution environments, zero-knowledge proofs and synthetic data. NIST SP 800-226 (final March 2025) gives guidance on evaluating differential privacy, "a mathematical framework that quantifies privacy loss to entities when their data appears in a dataset."

The retail group's analytics team publishes store-level shopping trends using differential privacy, so no individual customer's purchases can be inferred, and trains its recommendation model only on tokenized customer identifiers.

SASE and well-architected frameworks

Objective 3.1Recognizing cloud-delivered network security architectures and the cloud providers' frameworks for designing workloads well.

Secure Access Service Edge (SASE), a term coined by the analyst firm Gartner in 2019, combines wide-area networking (SD-WAN) with security services delivered from the cloud close to the user: a secure web gateway, a cloud access security broker, firewall as a service and zero trust network access, among others. The security half on its own is often called security service edge (SSE). NIST's guide to the enterprise network landscape (SP 800-215, 2022) lists SASE among the security services for distributed, cloud-heavy networks. SASE suits organizations with many branches and remote users, like the retail group's stores, because traffic is secured near where it starts instead of being sent back to a head-office data center.

Well-architected frameworks are the cloud providers' design guidance, each with security as one pillar among several:

  • AWS Well-Architected Framework: six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization and sustainability.
  • Microsoft Azure Well-Architected Framework: five pillars: reliability, security, cost optimization, operational excellence and performance efficiency.
  • Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework (renamed from the Architecture Framework): six pillars, including "security, privacy, and compliance".

Their value for a CISSP is the review process: workloads are assessed against the pillars regularly, and the security pillar applies the same principles as this page in cloud terms.

Security models

Objective 3.2Knowing what each formal security model protects and the rules it enforces, and recognizing which model fits a scenario.

A security model is a precise, often mathematical, statement of a security policy that a system can be built and checked against. Several are state machine models (the system starts secure and every allowed transition leads to another secure state) and information flow models (they control how information moves between security levels).

ModelProtectsKey rules
Bell-LaPadula (1973)Confidentiality (military classification)Simple security property: no read up. Star (*) property: no write down. Strong star: read and write only at your own level
Biba (1977)IntegritySimple integrity: no read down. Star integrity: no write up. Invocation: cannot call a subject at a higher integrity level
Clark-Wilson (1987)Integrity in commercial systemsUsers change constrained data only through well-formed transactions (an access triple of user, program and data); verification procedures check data validity; separation of duties
Brewer-Nash (1989)Conflicts of interest ("Chinese Wall")Once a user accesses one company's data, access to competitors in the same conflict-of-interest class is blocked; access changes dynamically
Graham-DenningSecure creation and deletion of subjects, objects and rightsEight rules over an access matrix, such as create or delete a subject or object, and grant, transfer or delete a right
Harrison-Ruzzo-Ullman (1976)Analysis of how rights can changeShows that deciding whether a right can "leak" to an unauthorized subject is undecidable in general
Take-GrantRights transfer, as a graphTake, grant, create and remove rules; safety can be decided efficiently
Lattice-based, noninterferenceInformation flowLattice: levels with upper and lower bounds, flows only upward. Noninterference (1982): actions at a high level must not change what a low level observes

"Star" and the direction of the rules. The outline's example "Star" most likely refers to the Bell-LaPadula star (*) property: no write down. Bell-LaPadula protects confidentiality with "no read up, no write down"; Biba protects integrity with the reverse, "no read down, no write up". Bell-LaPadula says nothing about integrity, and Biba nothing about confidentiality. Clark-Wilson is the integrity model for business transactions; Brewer-Nash is the one for consultancies and banks serving competing clients.

The retail group's new payment platform follows Clark-Wilson ideas: settlement records can be changed only through approved transaction services, never by direct database edits, and a reconciliation job checks every day that the records are consistent.

Key takeaways

  • Secure engineering: apply named principles throughout the life cycle (SA-8, SP 800-160); start with threat modeling. Saltzer and Schroeder: economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, open design, separation of privilege, least privilege, least common mechanism, psychological acceptability.
  • Limit damage: least privilege, defense in depth, separation and rotation of duties, two-person integrity, zero trust, documented shared responsibility.
  • Secure defaults (deny unless authorized), fail securely (deny on failure, recover to a known secure state; safety overrides), keep it simple, minimize shared mechanisms.
  • Privacy by design: Cavoukian's seven principles; GDPR Art. 25 by design and by default; PETs such as pseudonymization, differential privacy (SP 800-226), homomorphic encryption, MPC, federated learning.
  • SASE = SD-WAN plus cloud-delivered security (SWG, CASB, FWaaS, ZTNA); SSE is the security part. Well-architected frameworks (AWS six pillars, Azure five, Google six) include a security pillar.
  • Models: Bell-LaPadula (confidentiality: no read up, star property no write down), Biba (integrity: no read down, no write up), Clark-Wilson (well-formed transactions, SoD), Brewer-Nash (Chinese Wall), Graham-Denning (eight rules), HRU (safety undecidable), Take-Grant, lattice, noninterference.
