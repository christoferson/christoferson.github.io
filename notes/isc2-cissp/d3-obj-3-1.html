3.1: Secure design principles

The principles that make systems secure by design rather than by patching afterwards: limiting what can go wrong, failing safely, keeping designs simple, trusting nothing by default, building in privacy, and dividing responsibility clearly with providers.

Overview

Objective 3.1

3.1 Research, implement, and manage engineering processes using secure design principles. Sub-topics: threat modeling; least privilege; defense in depth; secure defaults; fail securely; separation of duties; keep it simple and small; zero trust; privacy by design (privacy-enhancing technologies); shared responsibility; SASE; well-architected framework; rotation of duties; two-person integrity.

Least privilege, separation of duties, defense in depth and zero trust are introduced in CC 3.2 and CC 4.2; threat modeling methods are on the 1.10 page; security models on the 3.2 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is designing a new order management platform in the cloud.

Secure engineering

Objective 3.1Building security into the engineering process, and knowing where the principles come from.

NIST SP 800-160 Vol. 1 Rev. 1, Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems (2022), treats security as an engineering discipline applied throughout the life cycle (objective 3.10). SP 800-53 control SA-8 requires organizations to "apply the following systems security and privacy engineering principles in the specification, design, development", implementation and modification of systems, and lists 33 principles as enhancements, from clear abstractions and least common mechanism to secure defaults, least privilege and minimization.

Many trace back to Saltzer and Schroeder's 1975 paper "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems", whose eight principles still appear in exam questions:

  • Economy of mechanism: keep the design small and simple.
  • Fail-safe defaults: base access on permission, not exclusion.
  • Complete mediation: check every access to every object.
  • Open design: security should not depend on the design being secret.
  • Separation of privilege: require more than one condition (two keys) for sensitive actions.
  • Least privilege: give each subject only what it needs.
  • Least common mechanism: minimize mechanisms shared by many users.
  • Psychological acceptability: make the secure way easy to use correctly.

Threat modeling belongs in design: model the system, find threats at each trust boundary, and turn mitigations into requirements before code is written (objective 1.10). "Research, implement, and manage" means the principles are applied deliberately: chosen for the system, built into reference architectures and design reviews, and checked over time.

Principles that limit damage

Objective 3.1Designing so that one mistake, compromise or dishonest person cannot cause unlimited harm.

  • Least privilege (SA-8(14)): users, services and components get only the access they need, for only as long as needed; just-in-time elevation and narrowly scoped service identities apply it to modern systems.
  • Defense in depth: several independent, diverse layers (identity, network, host, application, data, monitoring), so the failure of one does not expose everything. Identical redundant layers give protection that NIST warns "may be illusory", because one flaw defeats them all.
  • Separation of duties: split critical tasks so no one person can complete them alone (develop code vs approve and deploy it; create a supplier vs approve payments). Built into systems as mutually exclusive roles and approval workflows.
  • Rotation of duties: moving people (and, by analogy, credentials and keys) periodically reduces dependence on individuals and exposes hidden misuse; mandatory vacations do the same.
  • Two-person integrity (dual control): two authorized people must act together for the most critical operations, such as releasing a payment above a threshold, using a root signing key or deleting production backups. It prevents a single insider, or a single stolen credential, from acting alone.
  • Least common mechanism (SA-8(2)): shared components (a common admin account, a shared library, a shared cache) can become paths for information flow and covert channels, so minimize them and isolate tenants.

In the order platform, each microservice has its own identity with access only to its own data store, administrators elevate through PAM for a limited time, production deployments need a second engineer's approval, and deleting backups needs two people from different teams.

Secure defaults, fail securely, keep it simple

Objective 3.1Making the safe state the starting state and the failure state, and keeping designs small enough to verify.

  • Secure defaults (SA-8(23)): the as-shipped configuration enforces policy conservatively, with "deny unless explicitly authorized"; a system may refuse to run until it is configured; if initialization fails, it uses secure defaults or does not perform the operation. No default passwords, services off until needed, logging on.
  • Fail securely (SA-8(24), secure failure and recovery): neither a failure nor the response to it may violate policy. Components should fail in a state that "denies rather than grants access", detect failures at startup, run time, shutdown and maintenance, and recover by reconfiguring, shutting down or rolling back to a known secure state; transactions should be atomic so an interrupted operation leaves no half-finished state.
  • Keep it simple and small (SA-8(7), reduced complexity; Saltzer's economy of mechanism): smaller, simpler designs are easier to analyze and "simpler systems contain fewer vulnerabilities". Remove unused features and code, standardize on fewer technologies, and keep the trusted computing base minimal (objective 3.4). NIST notes that transitions, such as running old and new systems side by side, temporarily add complexity, so plan to finish them.

Fail-secure vs fail-safe. In information systems "fail securely" means failing closed: an access control check that errors denies access. In physical safety, fail-safe often means the opposite: emergency exit doors unlock in a fire so people can escape. When human life is at stake, life safety wins (objective 3.8); for data, fail closed.

Privacy by design

Objective 3.1Building privacy into systems from the start, and using technologies that protect personal data in use.

Ann Cavoukian's seven foundational principles (2009), adopted by the international conference of data protection commissioners in 2010, are: proactive not reactive (preventive, not remedial); privacy as the default setting; privacy embedded into design; full functionality (positive-sum, not zero-sum); end-to-end security across the life cycle; visibility and transparency; and respect for user privacy. The GDPR turns the idea into law in Article 25, "data protection by design and by default": controllers must implement "appropriate technical and organisational measures, such as pseudonymisation", and "by default, only personal data which are necessary for each specific purpose of the processing are processed."

Privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) let organizations use data while exposing less of it:

  • Pseudonymization, tokenization and masking: replace identifiers.
  • Differential privacy: "a mathematical framework that quantifies privacy loss to entities when their data appears in a dataset" (NIST SP 800-226, final March 2025), by adding calibrated noise to results.
  • Homomorphic encryption and secure multiparty computation: compute on encrypted data, or jointly across parties without sharing inputs.
  • Federated learning: train models where the data is, sharing only model updates.
  • Trusted execution environments: process data in protected enclaves (objective 3.4).
  • Synthetic data, k-anonymity and zero-knowledge proofs.

In the order platform, analytics receive pseudonymized order data, location data is reduced to postcode area, and dashboards use differential privacy for small-store figures that could otherwise identify individual customers.

SASE and well-architected frameworks

Objective 3.1Using modern architecture patterns and cloud design frameworks that build in security principles.

Secure access service edge (SASE), a term coined by Gartner in 2019, combines wide-area networking (SD-WAN) with network security functions (secure web gateway, CASB, firewall as a service, zero trust network access, DLP) delivered as one cloud service close to users. Security service edge (SSE) is the security subset. NIST SP 800-215 (2022) covers SASE within its guide to the modern enterprise network landscape. SASE applies defense in depth and zero trust consistently to users wherever they are, instead of hauling traffic back to a data center.

Well-architected frameworks from cloud providers turn principles into review questions and design patterns:

  • AWS: six pillars: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization, sustainability.
  • Microsoft Azure: five pillars: reliability, security, cost optimization, operational excellence, performance efficiency.
  • Google Cloud: six pillars, including "security, privacy, and compliance".

Their security pillars repeat the same principles: strong identity and least privilege, protection at every layer, traceability, automation, data protection in transit and at rest, and preparation for incidents. Periodic well-architected reviews are a practical way to manage design principles over a system's life.

Zero trust and shared responsibility

Objective 3.1Designing without implicit trust in network location, and dividing security duties clearly with providers.

Zero trust, in NIST SP 800-207, "provides a collection of concepts and ideas designed to minimize uncertainty in enforcing accurate, least privilege per-request access decisions in information systems and services in the face of a network viewed as compromised." Its tenets include that "network location alone does not imply trust", access is granted per session with the least privilege needed, policy is dynamic and uses identity, device posture and context, and everything is monitored. NIST describes three approaches (enhanced identity governance, micro-segmentation, and software-defined perimeters) and says a full solution includes elements of all three. In design terms: authenticate and authorize every request, encrypt every connection, segment finely, and assume breach when deciding what an attacker could reach.

Shared responsibility divides security duties between a cloud provider and its customer by service model (CC 4.3): the provider secures the cloud infrastructure; the customer secures what it puts in and configures (data, identities, access, configuration, and in IaaS the operating systems and applications). NIST SP 800-144 is clear that "ultimately, the organization is accountable for the choice of public cloud and the security and privacy of the outsourced service." The design principle is to document the split for every service, verify the provider's part through attestations, and own the customer's part fully; AI services add their own split (model, application and usage layers).

Principles reinforce each other. Zero trust is least privilege and complete mediation applied per request; SASE delivers it to remote users; defense in depth assumes any one of these can fail; shared responsibility decides who builds each layer.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying secure design principles to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the authorization service outage
Situation
When the authorization service times out, the order API currently lets requests through "to avoid blocking customers".
Best response
Change it to fail closed (deny and show a retry message), and make the authorization service highly available so failures are rare.
Why
Fail securely: a failure must not grant access (SA-8(24)); availability is addressed with resilience, not by bypassing controls.
Scenario 2: the trusted internal network
Situation
Internal services call each other without authentication because "they are all inside our VPC".
Best response
Require mutual authentication and per-request authorization between services, with each service limited to the calls it needs.
Why
Zero trust: network location alone does not imply trust; least privilege limits lateral movement after a compromise.
Scenario 3: the backup deletion
Situation
One cloud administrator account can delete all production backups. Ransomware groups target exactly this.
Best response
Use immutable backups and require two-person approval from separate accounts for deleting or changing backup retention.
Why
Two-person integrity and separation of duties stop a single compromised identity from destroying recovery; defense in depth adds immutability.
Scenario 4: the provider's breach
Situation
A misconfigured storage bucket in your cloud account exposed customer orders. Management asks whether the provider is responsible.
Best answer
No: bucket configuration is the customer's responsibility under the shared responsibility model, and the organization remains accountable for its data in any case.
Why
The provider secures the infrastructure; customers secure their configuration, identities and data.

Key takeaways

  • Secure engineering: SP 800-160 Vol. 1 Rev. 1 and SA-8's 33 principles; Saltzer and Schroeder's eight (economy of mechanism, fail-safe defaults, complete mediation, open design, separation of privilege, least privilege, least common mechanism, psychological acceptability); threat model in design.
  • Limit damage: least privilege, diverse defense in depth, separation of duties, rotation of duties, two-person integrity, least common mechanism.
  • Defaults and failure: secure defaults (deny unless authorized), fail securely (deny rather than grant, roll back to a known secure state), keep it simple and small. Fail-secure for data, fail-safe for people.
  • Privacy by design: Cavoukian's seven principles; GDPR Art. 25; PETs (pseudonymization, differential privacy per SP 800-226, homomorphic encryption, MPC, federated learning, TEEs, synthetic data).
  • SASE (SD-WAN plus cloud-delivered security; SSE is the security part); well-architected frameworks (AWS six, Azure five, Google six pillars) as design reviews.
  • Zero trust (SP 800-207): no trust from location, per-request least privilege, dynamic policy, monitoring. Shared responsibility: document the split; the customer remains accountable.
