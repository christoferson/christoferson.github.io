4.3: Secure communication channels

Protecting the channels people and systems actually use: calls, video meetings and chat, remote connections for staff and administrators, the links that carry data between sites, and the connections that suppliers have into the network.

Overview

Objective 4.3

4.3 Implement secure communication channels according to design.

The protocols are on the 4.1 page (IPsec, TLS, SSH, SIP and SRTP); VPN basics are in CC 4.1. This page applies them to the channels an organization runs every day. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. About 1,200 office staff work partly from home, and a dozen vendors support its tills, HVAC and network equipment remotely.

Voice, video and collaboration

Objective 4.3Securing calls, meetings, meeting rooms and chat so conversations and shared files stay private.

  • Voice: IP phones on a separate voice VLAN, SIP over TLS and SRTP for media, hardened call managers, and controls against toll fraud (restricting international and premium-rate calls, alerting on unusual call volumes).
  • Conferencing: require authentication for internal meetings, use waiting rooms and meeting passcodes, restrict screen sharing and recording to hosts, and use end-to-end encryption for sensitive meetings where the service offers it (it can disable some features such as cloud recording). CISA's guidance for securing video conferencing sets safeguards at both the organization level and the user level.
  • Video meeting rooms: room systems are connected computers with cameras and microphones, so patch them, change default passwords, put them on their own network segment, disable auto-answer, and cover or switch off cameras and microphones when not in use.
  • Collaboration platforms (chat, shared workspaces, file sharing): control external sharing and guest access, apply retention and DLP policies, review third-party apps connected to them, and remember that chat is a target for social engineering (attackers posing as the help desk).

CISA has also issued best-practice guidance on mobile communications for "highly targeted" individuals, written after nation-state espionage against telecom providers. The general lesson: ordinary phone calls and SMS cross carrier networks that may be compromised, so sensitive conversations belong on end-to-end encrypted services the organization has approved.

Remote access

Objective 4.3Giving remote workers and administrators access through controlled, authenticated and encrypted paths.

SP 800-53 AC-17 requires organizations to "authorize each type of remote access to the system prior to allowing such connections"; its enhancements add encryption of remote sessions for confidentiality and integrity, routing remote access "through authorized and managed network access control points", and allowing privileged commands remotely only for documented needs. NIST SP 800-46 Rev. 2 is the telework security guide.

  • Remote working: a VPN (IPsec or TLS) or, increasingly, zero trust network access (ZTNA), which connects users to specific applications after checking identity and device posture rather than placing them on the whole network. Require phishing-resistant MFA, managed devices, and split tunneling only where the risk is understood. Virtual desktops keep data in the data center.
  • Network administrative functions: administrators connect through a jump host (bastion) or privileged access management system that enforces MFA, records sessions and limits which devices can be reached, ideally from dedicated admin workstations and over an out-of-band management network. Management interfaces should never face the internet.
  • Remote access gateways are targets: CISA has issued emergency directives about actively exploited VPN appliances (Ivanti Connect Secure in 2024, Cisco ASA in 2025), in which attackers kept their access even after reboots or resets. Patch fast, monitor them closely, and replace end-of-support devices (BOD 26-02).
  • Remote access software (remote monitoring and management tools): CISA's 2023 guide warns that "cyber threat actors increasingly co-opt these tools for access to victim systems." Allow only approved tools, block the rest, and watch for new ones appearing.
  • Exposed remote desktop (RDP) is a common ransomware entry point: never publish it directly to the internet.

VPN does not mean trusted. A VPN encrypts the channel; it does not make the device or user trustworthy. A compromised laptop on a full-network VPN is an attacker inside the network. Answers that add device posture checks, least-privilege application access and monitoring beat answers that only add encryption.

Data communications

Objective 4.3Protecting the links that carry data between sites, clouds and remote locations.

  • Backhaul networks: the links that carry traffic from the edge (stores, cell towers, branch offices) back to the core network or data center, over leased lines, MPLS, broadband, microwave or cellular. Treat carrier links as untrusted: encrypt over them (IPsec or MACsec), authenticate the endpoints, and monitor for outages and changes.
  • Satellite: for remote sites, ships and backup links. High latency, links run by a provider, and ground terminals that may be internet-exposed. Apply CISA's SATCOM advisory (AA22-076A): MFA and no default credentials on terminals, least privilege, independent encryption over the provider's link, and monitoring.
  • Telecom infrastructure itself: in 2024, PRC-affiliated actors "compromised networks of major global telecommunications providers". The joint CISA, NSA and FBI guidance for communications infrastructure stresses alerting on configuration changes made outside change control, central encrypted logging, out-of-band management, default-deny access lists, only secure management protocols (SNMPv3, SSHv2, TLS 1.3; no Telnet, FTP or HTTP), phishing-resistant MFA and fast patching. For customers, the lesson is not to rely on the carrier for confidentiality.

The retail group's stores reach the data center over SD-WAN with IPsec on both broadband and 5G links; card data is also encrypted end to end from the payment terminal, so even a compromised link reveals nothing usable.

Third-party connectivity

Objective 4.3Controlling the network connections that carriers, suppliers and support vendors have into the organization.

Every connection to an outside party should be documented and agreed. SP 800-53 CA-3 (information exchange) calls for agreements such as "interconnection security agreements, information exchange security agreements, memoranda of understanding or agreement" or service level agreements, which record the interface, security and privacy requirements and the impact level, and are reviewed regularly. NIST SP 800-47 Rev. 1 (2021) explains how to manage these exchanges.

  • Telecom providers: diverse carriers and routes for resilience, contract terms for security notifications, and your own encryption over their links.
  • Hardware and software support: SP 800-53 MA-4 (nonlocal maintenance) requires approving and monitoring remote maintenance, strong authentication, and ending sessions and connections when the work is done.
  • Design rules for vendor access: a dedicated vendor access path (a jump host or vendor privileged access tool) rather than a standing VPN; named accounts per technician, never shared; access enabled only for an approved maintenance window; session recording; and reach limited to the vendor's own systems.
  • Partner connections (site-to-site VPNs, private links, APIs): terminate them in a DMZ or partner segment with firewalls that allow only the agreed traffic, and review them when contracts change or end.

Key takeaways

  • Voice and video: voice VLANs, SIPS and SRTP, toll-fraud controls; authenticated meetings, waiting rooms, host-only sharing and recording, end-to-end encryption for sensitive meetings; patch and segment meeting rooms; govern chat and file sharing.
  • Remote access (AC-17, SP 800-46): authorize each type, encrypt, route through managed access points; VPN or ZTNA with phishing-resistant MFA and managed devices; admins through jump hosts or PAM; never expose RDP or management interfaces; approved remote tools only.
  • Remote access gateways are prime targets: patch, monitor and replace end-of-support devices. A VPN encrypts but does not make a device trusted.
  • Data communications: treat backhaul and satellite links as untrusted; encrypt and authenticate; apply SATCOM and telecom hardening guidance.
  • Third parties: documented agreements (CA-3, SP 800-47); monitored, approved, time-limited maintenance (MA-4); named accounts, session recording, least reach; partner links into a DMZ.
