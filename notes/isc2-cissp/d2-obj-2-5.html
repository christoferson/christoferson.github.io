2.5: Asset management and end of life

Keeping assets supported for as long as they are used, planning for end of life and end of support before they arrive, keeping the assets that must be kept, and retiring the rest without leaving data or risk behind.

Overview

Objective 2.5

2.5 Ensure appropriate asset management (e.g., End of Life (EOL), End of Support (EOS)). Sub-topics: asset management policy; asset retention; asset retirement.

Ownership, inventory and provisioning are on the 2.3 page; end-of-support edge devices on the 4.2 page; basics in CC 5.4. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is replacing 2,000 store tills whose operating system is out of support.

Asset management policy

Objective 2.5Setting the rules for how assets are acquired, tracked, supported and retired.

An asset management policy covers the whole life cycle so that security is not an afterthought at either end. It typically requires:

  • Acquisition only through approved channels and suppliers, with security requirements and a stated support period in the purchase.
  • Inventory and ownership: every asset recorded with an owner, classification, criticality and support dates (objective 2.3).
  • Supported use: assets kept within vendor support, patched and maintained; exceptions approved and recorded.
  • Lifecycle planning: replacement budgeted before end of support, not after.
  • Retention and retirement: checks for holds and dependencies, sanitization, inventory update and environmental disposal.

CSF 2.0 frames the outcomes: "software is maintained, replaced, and removed commensurate with risk" (PR.PS-02, with the example "Replace end-of-life software and service versions with supported, maintained versions") and hardware likewise (PR.PS-03, including "Perform hardware disposal in a secure, responsible, and auditable manner").

End of life and end of support

Objective 2.5Planning for the point where a vendor stops selling or supporting a product, and managing what remains.

End of life (EOL) usually means a product is no longer sold or developed; end of support (EOS) means the vendor stops providing fixes. Terms vary by vendor, but the security point is the same: after end of support, vulnerabilities stay open. Microsoft's description is typical: "Upon retirement or end of support, there will be no new security updates, non-security updates, free or paid assisted support options or online technical content updates." For example, Windows 10 reached end of support on October 14, 2025, with paid Extended Security Updates available to organizations for up to three years.

SP 800-53 SA-22 requires organizations to "replace system components when support for the components is no longer available from the developer, vendor, or manufacturer", or provide alternative support (in-house or a third party). Support "includes software patches, firmware updates, replacement parts, and maintenance contracts." Where an unsupported component must stay, reduce the risk "by prohibiting the connection of such components to public or uncontrolled networks, or implementing other forms of isolation."

  1. TrackRecord support end dates in the inventory and report what reaches EOS in the next 12 to 24 months.
  2. Plan and budgetReplace or upgrade before the date, since migrations take longer than expected.
  3. If it must stayIsolate it, restrict access, monitor closely, buy extended support if available, and record a time-limited risk acceptance by the owner.
  4. RetireRemove it as soon as the replacement is live.

CISA's Product Security Bad Practices guidance asks manufacturers to "clearly communicate the period of support for their products at the time of sale", which buyers should ask for and put in contracts. For US federal agencies, BOD 26-02 (February 2026) requires inventorying and removing end-of-support edge devices on fixed deadlines (objective 4.2).

Isolation reduces risk; it does not remove it. An unsupported system kept behind a firewall is still exploitable by anything that reaches it. The best answer is replacement on a plan; isolation and monitoring are compensating controls while that plan runs, with an owner's documented acceptance.

Asset retention

Objective 2.5Keeping assets that must be kept, and protecting them while they are kept.

Some assets must be kept after they leave service:

  • Legal hold: a laptop or phone relevant to litigation or an investigation must be preserved, often as a forensic image plus the original.
  • Archive readability: equipment, software and licenses needed to read archived data in old formats for the retention period.
  • Regulatory or contractual requirements: records of certain systems or media kept for set periods.
  • Spare parts and fallback: a small stock of old devices kept until a replacement is proven, then retired.

Retained assets stay in the inventory with a reason, an owner and a review date, are stored securely at their classification level, and are retired as soon as the reason ends. When the retail group retired its old tills, it kept three units and the software needed to read their transaction archives for the seven-year financial retention period.

Asset retirement

Objective 2.5Retiring assets so no data, access or dependency is left behind.

  1. CheckConfirm no legal hold, retention requirement or dependency (other systems, integrations, scheduled jobs) remains.
  2. Migrate or archiveMove data that must be kept to its new home under the retention schedule.
  3. Sanitize or destroyPurge or destroy storage according to its classification and destination, with certificates (objective 2.2).
  4. RevokeAccounts, service credentials, API keys, certificates, licenses, DNS records and firewall rules tied to the asset.
  5. UpdateInventory, network and monitoring configurations, and documentation.
  6. DisposeThrough a certified provider meeting environmental rules, with a chain of custody and certificates per serial number.

Cloud and SaaS retirement follows the same pattern: export what must be kept, delete data and confirm deletion with the provider, remove integrations and identities, and close the account. Forgotten cloud resources and DNS records left pointing at them are common causes of breaches and subdomain takeovers. AI systems need the same care: the NIST AI RMF expects processes for "decommissioning and phasing out AI systems safely and in a manner that does not increase risks".

Lifecycle planning across asset types

Objective 2.5Managing support and replacement for assets with very different lifetimes, from OT controllers to cloud resources.

  • End-user devices: short, predictable lifetimes (typically three to five years); replace on a rolling schedule and keep operating systems within support.
  • Servers and network equipment: track firmware and software support separately from hardware warranty, since software support often ends first.
  • Operational technology and embedded systems: controllers and building systems may run for decades, far beyond software support. Plan for long-term isolation, vendor support contracts, spare parts and eventual redesign; replacing them can require production downtime and safety testing.
  • Software and libraries: versions go out of support on vendor or community schedules; track them in the inventory and SBOMs, and budget upgrades as normal maintenance.
  • Cloud resources: often short-lived and created by automation; asset management depends on tagging (owner, data class, environment, cost center) and automatic cleanup of untagged or idle resources. Provider services and runtime versions also reach end of support.
  • SaaS and AI services: the provider controls the life cycle; contracts should give notice of retirement, and inventories should record which models and service versions the business depends on.

A lifecycle roadmap brings these together: for each asset class, the support end dates for the next two to three years, the replacement plan and budget, and the owner. It turns end of support from a crisis into a planned change, and gives leadership the information to fund replacements before they become urgent.

Total cost of ownership should include the security cost of keeping old assets: extended support fees, compensating controls, extra monitoring and the risk accepted. These costs often make an earlier replacement cheaper than it first appears. At the retail group, the lifecycle roadmap showed that keeping the old tills two more years would cost more in extended support and isolation work than bringing the replacement forward, which changed the budget decision.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying asset management reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the unsupported till system
Situation
The tills' operating system reached end of support last month; replacement will take nine months.
Best response
Buy extended security updates if available, isolate the tills on the payment network, restrict and monitor access, and have the owner accept the residual risk until the documented replacement date.
Why
SA-22 calls for replacement or alternative support, with isolation where unsupported components must remain; a time-limited, owned exception keeps the risk visible.
Scenario 2: the old file server
Situation
A file server is being decommissioned. One folder belongs to a former employee named in an ongoing employment claim.
Best response
Preserve that folder (and the server image if counsel requires) under the legal hold before retiring the rest of the server.
Why
Retirement starts with checking for holds and retention requirements; destroying held data can bring sanctions.
Scenario 3: the abandoned campaign site
Situation
A marketing microsite was shut down by deleting its cloud storage, but its DNS record still points to the old storage address.
Best response
Remove the DNS record and any certificates and credentials tied to the site, and add DNS and integration cleanup to the retirement checklist.
Why
Dangling records let attackers claim the address and serve content under the company's domain; retirement must revoke everything tied to the asset.
Scenario 4: the factory controller
Situation
A distribution center's conveyor controller runs software whose vendor support ended years ago. Replacing it means a two-week shutdown.
Best response
Isolate the controller on the OT network with strict access, monitor it, keep spares, document the owner's risk acceptance, and schedule replacement into a planned maintenance window.
Why
OT assets often outlive support; compensating controls and planned replacement manage the risk where immediate replacement is impractical.

Key takeaways

  • Policy: approved acquisition with stated support periods, inventory with support dates, supported use, budgeted replacement, controlled retention and retirement; CSF PR.PS-02 and PR.PS-03.
  • EOL and EOS: after end of support, flaws stay open; SA-22 replace or provide alternative support; isolate what must stay; track dates, plan early, accept residual risk formally and time-limited; extended support is a bridge.
  • Retention: keep assets for legal holds, archive readability, regulation or fallback, with a reason, owner and review date; protect them at their level.
  • Retirement: check holds and dependencies, migrate data, sanitize, revoke credentials, licenses, DNS and rules, update inventories, dispose with certificates; same for cloud, SaaS and AI systems.
