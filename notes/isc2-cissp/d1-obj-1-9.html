1.9–1.10: Risk management and threat modeling

The full risk cycle a security leader runs: finding threats and weaknesses, measuring risk in words or money, choosing how to respond (including insurance), checking that controls work, reporting and maturing the program, and modeling threats to systems before attackers do.

Overview

Objectives 1.9 and 1.10

1.9 Understand and apply risk management concepts. 1.10 Understand and apply threat modeling concepts and methodologies.

Risk management is the thread through the whole CISSP. The vocabulary (asset, threat, vulnerability, likelihood, impact, risk appetite), the NIST risk process and a worked SLE and ALE example are in CC 1.2; control types in CC 1.4; metrics in CC 2.4; and STRIDE, PASTA and attack trees in CC 5.5. This page adds the decisions and methods expected of a CISSP. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It also sells to a US defense agency through a small uniform-supply contract.

Identifying threats and vulnerabilities

Objective 1.9Setting the scope of a risk assessment and finding the threats and weaknesses that apply to it.

Every assessment starts by fixing its scope: which business processes, systems, locations and data are in, and which are out, so results are comparable and nothing important falls between assessments. Within scope:

  • Assets are identified and valued, by their cost and, more usefully, by the impact on the business if their confidentiality, integrity or availability is lost (the BIA in objective 1.7).
  • Threats come from four kinds of source in NIST SP 800-30: adversarial, accidental, structural (equipment and software failures) and environmental. Threat intelligence (objective 7.2) and threat modeling (below) make the list specific.
  • Vulnerabilities come from scans, penetration tests, audits, configuration reviews and supplier assessments; they include weak processes and people as well as software flaws.

CSF 2.0 ties these together: "Threats, vulnerabilities, likelihoods, and impacts are used to understand inherent risk and inform risk response prioritization" (ID.RA-05). Inherent risk is the risk before controls; residual risk is what remains after them.

Risk analysis and assessment

Objective 1.9Measuring risk qualitatively or in money, and using the result to decide whether a control is worth its cost.

Qualitative analysis rates likelihood and impact on scales such as low to very high and is quick and easy to communicate; quantitative analysis puts money on them and, as NIST notes, "most effectively supports cost-benefit analyses." Most organizations combine both: qualitative for the whole risk register, quantitative for the biggest decisions. The classic quantitative formulas, a common convention rather than a NIST standard:

  • Single loss expectancy (SLE) = asset value (AV) × exposure factor (EF, the fraction lost in one event).
  • Annualized loss expectancy (ALE) = SLE × annualized rate of occurrence (ARO).
  • Value of a safeguard = ALE before − ALE after − annual cost of the safeguard. A positive value means the control is worth it.
Cost-benefit: endpoint detection for store back-office PCs 
Asset value (store systems, sales, recovery)  $2,000,000
Exposure factor (ransomware event)             25%
SLE  = 2,000,000 x 0.25                       = $500,000
ARO before EDR  = 0.4  (once every 2.5 years)
ALE before      = 500,000 x 0.4               = $200,000
ARO after EDR   = 0.1
ALE after       = 500,000 x 0.1               =  $50,000
Annual cost of EDR (licences + monitoring)    =  $90,000
Value = 200,000 - 50,000 - 90,000             =  $60,000 per year
# positive: the control pays for itself

The numbers are only as good as the estimates behind them. The FAIR model, published as Open Group standards (the Open FAIR risk taxonomy and risk analysis standards), gives a more rigorous way to estimate loss frequency and magnitude and expresses cyber risk in financial terms "that the business understands."

Risk response, treatment and insurance

Objective 1.9Choosing to accept, avoid, mitigate, share or transfer each risk, and knowing what cyber insurance does and does not do.

NIST SP 800-39 lists the responses: "risk acceptance; risk avoidance; risk mitigation; risk sharing; risk transfer; or a combination." The risk owner chooses, within the appetite senior management set, and formally accepts the residual risk. Accepting a risk is a legitimate, documented decision; ignoring it is not.

Cyber insurance is the usual form of transfer. The FTC, with the National Association of Insurance Commissioners, separates:

  • First-party coverage for the organization's own losses: legal advice on notification duties, data restoration, customer notification and call centers, business interruption, crisis management, cyber extortion and fraud, forensics, and some fines and penalties.
  • Third-party coverage for claims by others: payments to affected consumers, settlements and damages, litigation and responding to regulators.

Standard property and liability policies generally "do not cover cyber risks", and cyber policies are "highly customized." In common market practice, insurers ask for controls such as MFA, endpoint detection and tested backups before covering a company, and the US GAO reported sharp premium rises from late 2020.

Insurance does not transfer accountability, and ransom payments carry legal risk. The organization still answers to customers and regulators after a breach. The US Treasury's sanctions office (OFAC) "continues to strongly discourage the payment of cyber ransom or extortion demands", warns that paying a sanctioned group can bring sanctions penalties, and gives significant credit for prompt reporting to law enforcement.

Controls and control assessments

Objective 1.9Selecting controls by type and function, and assessing whether security and privacy controls actually work.

Controls are grouped by category (administrative, technical, physical) and function (preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent, recovery, compensating), as in CC 1.4. Good designs layer functions: prevent what you can, detect what gets through, correct quickly.

NIST's Risk Management Framework (SP 800-37 Rev. 2) puts controls in a seven-step cycle: Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize, Monitor. The Assess step checks that controls are "in place, operating as intended, and producing the desired results"; in Authorize, a senior official accepts the remaining risk. NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5 provides the assessment procedures for both security and privacy controls; assessors typically examine documents, interview people and test the controls.

Assurance can also come from outside: independent audits, ISO/IEC 27001 certification, and SOC reports from service organizations. SOC 2 reports address the AICPA's Trust Services Criteria of security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy; a Type 2 report is commonly understood to cover how controls operated over a period, stronger evidence than a point-in-time Type 1. For privacy specifically, the NIST Privacy Framework (version 1.0, with a 1.1 draft in 2025 aligning it to CSF 2.0) helps assess privacy risk alongside security.

Monitoring, reporting and maturity

Objective 1.9Keeping risk information current, reporting it to the right audiences, and improving the program over time.

Continuous monitoring keeps risk decisions current as threats and systems change (metrics, KRIs and dashboards in CC 2.4). Reporting goes to two audiences: internal (risk owners, executives, the board's risk or audit committee) and external (regulators, auditors, customers, and for US public companies the SEC's annual disclosure of risk management and board oversight).

Maturity models measure how well-developed processes are and give a roadmap for improving them:

  • CMMI (ISACA's CMMI Institute): maturity levels 0 Incomplete, 1 Initial, 2 Managed, 3 Defined, 4 Quantitatively Managed and 5 Optimizing.
  • C2M2 (US Department of Energy, version 2.1): over 350 practices in 10 domains, rated at maturity indicator levels MIL1 Initiated, MIL2 Performed and MIL3 Managed.
  • CMMC (US Department of Defense): certification of contractors that handle federal contract information and controlled unclassified information. Its program rule took effect December 16, 2024 and the contract rule November 10, 2025, so it now appears in defense contracts.

CSF tiers are not a maturity model. NIST says the CSF 2.0 tiers (Partial, Risk Informed, Repeatable, Adaptive) describe the rigor of risk governance and management, and not every organization needs the highest tier. The retail group's small defense supply contract brings CMMC requirements to the systems that handle that contract's data, not to the whole company.

Risk frameworks

Objective 1.9Knowing the main risk frameworks and which suits which situation.

  • ISO 31000: general risk management guidelines for any organization. ISO/IEC 27005: guidance on managing information security risks within an ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS.
  • NIST: SP 800-37 (the RMF), SP 800-30 (risk assessment), SP 800-39 (organization-wide risk), IR 8286 (linking cyber risk to enterprise risk) and CSF 2.0.
  • COBIT 2019: IT governance and management, including risk; SABSA: risk-driven security architecture (objective 1.3).
  • PCI DSS: requires a targeted risk analysis for requirements where the organization chooses how often to perform an activity.
  • HIPAA Security Rule: requires covered entities to conduct a risk analysis of threats to electronic health information.
  • OCTAVE (Carnegie Mellon's Software Engineering Institute; OCTAVE Allegro, 2007): an organization-wide, asset-focused risk assessment method.

Threat modeling

Objective 1.10Choosing a threat modeling method for the system and the question being asked, and fitting it into design and risk work.

Threat modeling finds threats to a specific system early, ideally at design time and again with each significant change, using a model of the system and its trust boundaries (CC 5.5). Methods differ in focus:

  • STRIDE (Microsoft): spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege; used in Microsoft's Threat Modeling Tool.
  • PASTA (Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis): a risk-centric method linking business objectives to technical threats.
  • DREAD: scores threats on Damage, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected users and Discoverability. Still documented by Microsoft, but widely criticized as subjective.
  • LINDDUN (KU Leuven): privacy threats: linking, identifying, non-repudiation, detecting, data disclosure, unawareness and non-compliance; supports privacy by design under GDPR.
  • VAST (Visual, Agile, and Simple Threat modeling): separate application and operational models that scale across many agile teams.
  • Attack trees: start from the attacker's goal and break it into paths.
  • MITRE ATT&CK and ATLAS: real-world adversary techniques against enterprise systems and AI systems, used to check that a model covers how attackers actually behave.

NIST's own guide to data-centric threat modeling (SP 800-154) has been a draft since 2016. Threat modeling feeds risk management directly: each credible threat becomes a risk register entry with a likelihood, impact and response. When the retail group designed its AI shopping assistant, the team combined STRIDE for the application with ATLAS techniques such as prompt injection and LINDDUN for customer privacy.

Key takeaways

  • Scope first; identify and value assets, threats (adversarial, accidental, structural, environmental) and vulnerabilities. Inherent risk before controls, residual after.
  • Qualitative (scales, quick) vs quantitative (money, supports cost-benefit). SLE = AV × EF; ALE = SLE × ARO; safeguard value = ALE before − ALE after − annual cost. FAIR for rigorous estimates.
  • Responses: accept, avoid, mitigate, share, transfer. The owner accepts residual risk. Cyber insurance: first-party (own losses) and third-party (claims); does not transfer accountability; OFAC discourages ransom payments.
  • Controls: categories and functions; NIST RMF Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize, Monitor; SP 800-53A assessments (security and privacy); SOC 2 Type 2 for service providers; NIST Privacy Framework.
  • Monitor and report internally and externally. Maturity: CMMI 0-5, C2M2 MIL1-3, CMMC for defense contractors (in contracts since November 2025). CSF tiers are not maturity levels.
  • Frameworks: ISO 31000, ISO/IEC 27005, NIST RMF/SP 800-30/CSF, COBIT, SABSA, PCI DSS targeted risk analysis, HIPAA risk analysis, OCTAVE.
  • Threat modeling: STRIDE, PASTA, DREAD (subjective), LINDDUN (privacy), VAST (agile scale), attack trees, ATT&CK and ATLAS; do it early and repeat; feed the risk register.
