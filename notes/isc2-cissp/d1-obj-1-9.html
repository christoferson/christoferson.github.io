1.9: Risk management concepts

The full risk cycle a CISSP runs: finding threats and vulnerabilities, analyzing and rating risk, choosing responses including insurance, selecting and assessing controls, monitoring and reporting, improving maturity, and the frameworks that structure it all.

Overview

Objective 1.9

1.9 Understand and apply risk management concepts. Sub-topics: threat and vulnerability identification; risk analysis, assessment and scope; risk response and treatment (including cyber insurance and mitigation strategies); types of controls; security and privacy control assessments; continuous monitoring and measurement; internal and external reporting; continuous improvement (maturity modeling); risk frameworks (ISO, NIST, COBIT, SABSA, PCI, HIPAA).

Risk vocabulary (risk, threat, vulnerability, likelihood, impact, inherent and residual risk) is introduced in CC 1.2; threat modeling is on the 1.10 page. This page covers how a CISSP runs risk management. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its board has set a low appetite for risks to customer data and a moderate appetite for risks to internal analytics.

Threat and vulnerability identification

Objective 1.9Finding what could harm the organization and the weaknesses that would let it.

NIST defines risk as "a measure of the extent to which an entity is threatened by a potential circumstance or event", typically a function of the adverse impact and the likelihood of occurrence (SP 800-30). Identifying risk means identifying its parts:

  • Assets: what is valuable (data, systems, processes, people, reputation), from the inventory and the BIA (objective 1.7).
  • Threat sources: "a malicious person with harmful intent or an unintended or unavoidable situation (such as a natural disaster, technical failure, or human error)" (NIST IR 8286). Adversarial sources are described by capability, intent and targeting; non-adversarial ones by frequency.
  • Threat events: what a threat source might do, such as phishing for credentials, ransomware, a cloud region outage, or an employee emailing data to the wrong person.
  • Vulnerabilities: a "weakness in an information system, system security procedures, internal controls, or implementation that could be exploited or triggered by a threat source" (SP 800-53). SP 800-30 also considers predisposing conditions, such as a system being internet-facing, that make events more likely.

Sources for identification: threat intelligence and sector sharing groups, past incidents and near misses, vulnerability scans and penetration tests, audit findings, threat modeling of systems (objective 1.10), supplier assessments, and workshops with process owners. CSF 2.0's ID.RA outcomes cover identifying and recording vulnerabilities, receiving threat intelligence, and identifying internal and external threats.

Risk analysis, assessment and scope

Objective 1.9Setting the scope of an assessment, and rating risks with qualitative, semi-quantitative or quantitative methods.

NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 describes four steps: prepare (purpose, scope, assumptions, sources, model), conduct (identify threat sources and events, vulnerabilities and predisposing conditions, determine likelihood, impact and risk), communicate the results, and maintain the assessment. Assessments happen at three levels: organization, mission or business process, and system.

Scope decides what the assessment covers and therefore what it can say: which systems, locations, processes, data, suppliers and time period; which threats are included; and what is explicitly out. A narrow scope gives a precise answer to a narrow question; findings must state the scope so leadership does not assume more coverage than exists.

Three ways to rate risk:

  • Qualitative: categories such as very low to very high. Quick and easy to communicate; SP 800-30 notes it "supports communicating risk results to decision makers", but different experts "could produce significantly different" results.
  • Semi-quantitative: scores or bins (for example 0–100) that allow ranking and aggregation while staying approximate.
  • Quantitative: money values and frequencies; it "most effectively supports cost-benefit analyses", but needs data that is often missing.

The classic quantitative formulas (commonly taught; NIST's withdrawn FIPS 65 used the same idea):

  • Single loss expectancy (SLE) = asset value (AV) × exposure factor (EF, the fraction lost in one event).
  • Annualized loss expectancy (ALE) = SLE × annualized rate of occurrence (ARO).
  • Value of a safeguard = ALE before − ALE after − annual cost of the safeguard. A positive value means the control is worth it.
Cost-benefit: endpoint detection for store back-office PCs 
Asset value (store systems, sales, recovery)  $2,000,000
Exposure factor (ransomware event)             25%
SLE  = 2,000,000 x 0.25                       = $500,000
ARO before EDR  = 0.4  (once every 2.5 years)
ALE before      = 500,000 x 0.4               = $200,000
ARO after EDR   = 0.1
ALE after       = 500,000 x 0.1               =  $50,000
Annual cost of EDR (licences + monitoring)    =  $90,000
Value = 200,000 - 50,000 - 90,000             =  $60,000 per year
# positive: the control pays for itself

The numbers are only as good as the estimates. The FAIR model, published as Open Group standards (the Open FAIR risk taxonomy and risk analysis standards), estimates loss event frequency and loss magnitude as ranges and expresses cyber risk in financial terms, "a language that the business understands". Results go into a risk register: "a repository of risk information, including the data understood about risks over time" (IR 8286), with an owner, rating, response and status for each risk.

Risk response, treatment and insurance

Objective 1.9Choosing to accept, avoid, mitigate, share or transfer each risk, within the organization's appetite.

NIST SP 800-39 lists the responses: "risk acceptance; risk avoidance; risk mitigation; risk sharing; risk transfer; or a combination." The risk owner chooses, within the risk appetite set by senior leadership ("the types and amount of risk, on a broad level, [an organization] is willing to accept in its pursuit of value") and the narrower risk tolerance for specific objectives.

  • Avoid: stop the activity, such as not storing card numbers at all by using tokenization.
  • Mitigate (reduce): add or improve controls to lower likelihood or impact. Mitigation strategies include prevention, detection and faster recovery, layered so no single control is relied on.
  • Share: spread part of the risk, for example with a cloud provider under shared responsibility.
  • Transfer: shift the financial consequences, typically through insurance or contracts. SP 800-39 notes transfer "reduces neither the likelihood of harmful events occurring nor the consequences".
  • Accept: a documented decision by an authorized owner to bear the risk, often with a review date. Ignoring a risk is not acceptance.

Cyber insurance, per guidance from the FTC and the National Association of Insurance Commissioners, comes in two parts: first-party coverage for the organization's own losses (forensics, notification, data restoration, business interruption, extortion, crisis communications) and third-party coverage for claims by others (customers, partners, regulators). Most general liability policies "do not cover cyber risks", and cyber policies are "highly customized", so read exclusions, limits, waiting periods and requirements such as approved incident response firms. Insurers commonly ask for controls such as MFA, EDR and offline backups as a condition of cover (market practice, not a rule).

Insurance does not make ransom payments safe. The US Treasury's OFAC "continues to strongly discourage the payment of cyber ransom or extortion demands" and warns that facilitating payments to sanctioned parties may bring sanctions enforcement, with significant mitigation for prompt reporting to law enforcement. A policy that covers extortion does not override sanctions law.

Whatever remains after treatment is residual risk, which the owner must accept or treat further. At the retail group, the risk of card data theft is mitigated (segmentation, tokenization, monitoring), partly transferred (cyber insurance), and the residual risk is accepted each year by the CFO as risk owner.

Types of controls

Objective 1.9Classifying controls by how they are implemented and what they do, so a treatment plan covers every stage of an attack.

Controls are classified two ways. By category (how they are implemented): administrative or managerial (policies, training, screening), technical or logical (encryption, access control, firewalls), and physical (locks, guards, fences). By function (what they do):

  • Preventive: stop an incident from happening (MFA, firewalls, separation of duties).
  • Deterrent: discourage an attacker (warning banners, visible cameras, sanctions policy).
  • Detective: discover an incident in progress or after the fact (IDS, log review, audits).
  • Corrective: limit damage and fix the problem (isolating a host, patching, restoring a file).
  • Recovery: restore operations after a major incident (backups, DR sites); often treated as part of corrective.
  • Compensating: an alternative that gives "equivalent or comparable protection" when the normal control cannot be used (SP 800-53), such as extra monitoring for a legacy system that cannot use MFA.
  • Directive: tell people what to do (policies, signs, procedures).

A good treatment plan combines functions and categories: a preventive technical control (MFA) backed by a detective one (alerting on unusual logins), a corrective one (automatic session revocation) and an administrative one (training). CSF 2.0's functions (Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) express the same idea at program level.

One control, two answers. Many controls fit more than one function: a camera both deters and detects; a security guard can prevent, deter and detect. Exam questions usually ask for the primary purpose in the scenario given.

Control assessments

Objective 1.9Checking that security and privacy controls are in place, working as intended and producing the desired results.

A control that exists on paper may not work. NIST's Risk Management Framework assesses whether controls are implemented correctly, "operating as intended, and producing the desired results", using SP 800-53A Rev. 5, which provides assessment procedures for every security and privacy control (its methods are commonly summarized as examine, interview and test). The results feed the authorization decision: a senior official decides whether the residual risk is acceptable to operate the system.

  • Security control assessments check the protection of confidentiality, integrity and availability.
  • Privacy control assessments check that personal data is processed as the law and privacy notices say, and that privacy risks to individuals are managed. The NIST Privacy Framework (v1.0, with a v1.1 draft aligned to CSF 2.0) structures these.
  • Who assesses: self-assessment by owners, independent internal assessors, internal audit, or external parties such as a PCI qualified security assessor or a SOC 2 auditor; independence rises with the stakes.

Assessment methods and test types are covered in Domain 6 (objectives 6.1 and 6.2).

Continuous monitoring and measurement

Objective 1.9Keeping risk information current between assessments, and measuring whether controls and responses work.

Risk changes daily as systems, threats and the business change. Continuous monitoring is "maintaining ongoing awareness to support organizational risk decisions" (NIST; SP 800-137 describes an information security continuous monitoring program). It combines automated data (configuration compliance, vulnerability scans, security events, asset inventory) with periodic manual checks, and triggers reassessment when something significant changes.

Measurement turns monitoring into decisions: key risk indicators warn when a risk approaches tolerance, key performance indicators track whether treatments are working (objective 6.3). The RMF's final step, Monitor, keeps authorizations valid by tracking control effectiveness and changes over time.

Reporting

Objective 1.9Communicating risk to the people inside and outside the organization who need to act on it.

  • Internal: risk owners get detailed registers and action plans; executives and the board get the top risks, trends against appetite, major changes and decisions needed, in business terms. NIST IR 8286 describes rolling system and program risk registers up to an enterprise risk register and dashboard.
  • External: regulators (breach notifications, supervisory returns), investors (US public companies describe cybersecurity risk management and board oversight in annual reports under SEC rules), customers and partners (assurance reports, questionnaires), insurers (applications and renewals), and auditors.

Good reporting is accurate, consistent over time, honest about uncertainty and scope, and tailored to the reader. Overstating controls in external reports or questionnaires creates legal exposure; understating risks to the board deprives it of the chance to decide.

Continuous improvement and maturity

Objective 1.9Measuring how mature risk and security practices are, and planning improvement.

Maturity models describe how repeatable and well-managed practices are, giving a target and a path:

  • CMMI (ISACA's CMMI Institute): maturity levels 0 Incomplete, 1 Initial ("unpredictable and reactive"), 2 Managed, 3 Defined, 4 Quantitatively Managed, 5 Optimizing (continuous improvement).
  • C2M2 (US Department of Energy, v2.1): over 350 practices in ten domains, rated at maturity indicator levels MIL1 Initiated, MIL2 Performed and MIL3 Managed; widely used in energy and other critical infrastructure.
  • CMMC (US Department of Defense): certifies that defense contractors protect federal contract information and controlled unclassified information; its requirements entered DoD contracts from November 10, 2025.

CSF tiers are not maturity levels. NIST CSF 2.0's tiers (Partial, Risk Informed, Repeatable, Adaptive) describe how rigorous an organization's cybersecurity risk governance and management are. NIST says they are not a maturity model for individual controls; progress on outcomes is shown by comparing current and target profiles.

Improvement also comes from lessons learned after incidents and exercises, audit findings, and periodic reassessment of the risk management process itself.

Risk frameworks

Objective 1.9Choosing a risk management framework or method that suits the organization and its obligations.

  • NIST Risk Management Framework (SP 800-37 Rev. 2): seven steps: Prepare, Categorize (FIPS 199), Select (SP 800-53 controls), Implement, Assess (SP 800-53A), Authorize (a senior official accepts the risk) and Monitor. Mandatory for US federal systems; widely adapted elsewhere. SP 800-39 sets the organization-wide process (frame, assess, respond, monitor) and SP 800-30 the assessment method.
  • ISO: ISO 31000 gives general risk management guidelines for any organization; ISO/IEC 27005 applies risk management to information security within an ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS.
  • COBIT: ISACA's governance framework, which places IT and security risk within enterprise governance and management objectives.
  • SABSA: derives security architecture from business risk and opportunity, so controls trace to business attributes (objective 1.3).
  • PCI DSS: v4.0.1 uses targeted risk analyses, for example to justify how often certain requirements are performed in the cardholder data environment.
  • HIPAA: the Security Rule requires covered entities and business associates to conduct a risk analysis of risks to electronic protected health information and manage the risks found.
  • Methods: FAIR for quantitative analysis; OCTAVE (Carnegie Mellon's SEI, with the streamlined OCTAVE Allegro) for organization-wide, asset-focused self-directed assessment.

Frameworks are combined: the retail group uses ISO/IEC 27005 inside its ISMS, FAIR for the few risks the board wants in money terms, and PCI DSS's targeted risk analyses for the card environment.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying risk management reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the expensive control
Situation
A data loss prevention suite costs $250,000 a year. Analysis puts the ALE for customer data leakage at $180,000 before and $60,000 after deployment.
Best answer
The safeguard value is 180,000 − 60,000 − 250,000 = −$130,000, so on these numbers it costs more than it saves. Look for a cheaper control, reconsider the estimates, or let the owner accept or transfer the risk.
Why
Controls should cost less than the risk they reduce, unless law, contract or appetite requires them regardless.
Scenario 2: insurance as the answer
Situation
A manager proposes buying more cyber insurance instead of fixing unpatched internet-facing servers, because "the insurer will pay".
Best response
Insurance transfers some financial impact but does not reduce likelihood or harm to customers, may be voided by poor controls, and does not cover everything (such as reputation). Patch the servers (mitigate) and keep insurance for residual risk.
Why
Transfer complements mitigation; due care still requires reasonable controls for known weaknesses.
Scenario 3: who accepts the risk
Situation
A legacy warehouse system cannot be patched. The security team writes "risk accepted" in the register to close the finding.
Best response
Escalate to the system's business owner, who decides with the facts: compensating controls (isolation, monitoring) and formal acceptance of what remains, with a review date and a replacement plan.
Why
Only the risk owner with authority can accept risk; the security team advises and records the decision.

Key takeaways

  • Identify: assets, threat sources (adversarial and non-adversarial), threat events, vulnerabilities and predisposing conditions, from intelligence, incidents, scans, threat models and workshops.
  • Assess (SP 800-30): prepare (scope), conduct, communicate, maintain; qualitative, semi-quantitative, quantitative. SLE = AV × EF; ALE = SLE × ARO; safeguard value = ALE before − ALE after − cost. FAIR for ranges in money terms; record in a risk register.
  • Respond (SP 800-39): accept, avoid, mitigate, share, transfer, within appetite and tolerance. Insurance (first- and third-party) transfers money, not harm; OFAC discourages ransom payments; residual risk accepted by the owner.
  • Controls: administrative, technical, physical; preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, recovery, compensating, directive; combine them.
  • Assess controls: SP 800-53A for security and privacy controls; results support the authorization decision; independence grows with stakes.
  • Monitor, report, improve: continuous monitoring (SP 800-137), KRIs and KPIs, internal and external reporting in the reader's terms; CMMI, C2M2, CMMC; CSF tiers are not maturity levels.
  • Frameworks: NIST RMF (seven steps), ISO 31000 and 27005, COBIT, SABSA, PCI DSS risk analyses, HIPAA risk analysis, FAIR, OCTAVE.
