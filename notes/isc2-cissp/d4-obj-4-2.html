Overview

Objective 4.2

4.2 Secure network components.

Firewall types and IDS are introduced in CC 4.1, and facility power and cabling rooms on the 3.9 page. This page is about running the components themselves securely. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Each store has a firewall, two switches and Wi-Fi access points; 40 stores still run a firewall model whose vendor support ends next year.

Operating network infrastructure

Objective 4.2Keeping network devices available, supported and replaced before they become unpatchable.

  • Redundant power: dual power supplies on separate circuits, UPS for network gear as well as servers, and generators where uptime matters. A core switch on a single power supply is a single point of failure.
  • Redundant devices and paths: paired firewalls and core switches with failover, diverse network links from different carriers, and tested failover (an untested standby often fails when needed).
  • Warranty and support: hardware replacement times, software updates and access to vendor engineers are part of the contract. Spares for critical parts cover the gap until a replacement arrives.
  • End of support: SP 800-53 SA-22 says to "replace system components when support for the components is no longer available", or arrange alternative support. Edge devices (firewalls, VPN gateways, routers) are the riskiest because they face the internet and are favorite targets.
  • Secure configuration: change default credentials, disable unused services and ports, manage out of band, back up configurations, keep firmware current and send logs to central monitoring.

CISA BOD 26-02. CISA's Binding Operational Directive 26-02, "Mitigating Risk From End-of-Support Edge Devices" (February 5, 2026), requires US federal agencies to inventory listed end-of-support edge devices within 3 months, decommission all identified ones within 18 months, and within 24 months set up continuous discovery of edge devices so new ones are replaced before their end-of-support date. It is a model for any organization: the retail group has budgeted to replace its 40 store firewalls before support ends.

Transmission media

Objective 4.2Protecting cables and radio links from tapping and damage, and keeping signals clean enough to carry data reliably.

  • Twisted pair copper (Ethernet): cheap and common; it emits electromagnetic signals, picks up interference and is relatively easy to tap. Distance is limited (about 100 meters per run).
  • Coaxial cable: better shielded than twisted pair, used for cable broadband and some legacy networks.
  • Fiber optic: light rather than electricity, so it is immune to electromagnetic interference, emits no electrical signal and covers long distances. Tapping it is harder and more likely to be noticed, though not impossible. Use it for backbones and between buildings.
  • Wireless: radio leaves the building, so anyone nearby can listen: encryption (WPA3) is essential, and antenna placement and power should limit coverage outside.

Physical security of media: run cables in conduits or trays through controlled spaces, lock wiring closets, disable unused wall ports (or put them in an unused VLAN), and inspect for unknown devices. SP 800-53 PE-4 covers access control for transmission lines. Emanations are "unintentional signals that, if intercepted and analyzed, would disclose the information" being processed; TEMPEST is the study and control of such emanations, and PE-19 requires protection "from information leakage due to electromagnetic signals emanations" where the risk justifies it, through shielding, distance (control zones) or fiber.

Signal propagation quality: signals weaken over distance (attenuation), pick up noise and electromagnetic or radio-frequency interference from motors, lights and other equipment, and leak between pairs (crosstalk). The result is errors, retransmissions and outages, which are availability problems. Use the right cable category, keep within distance limits, separate data cables from power cables, and monitor error rates.

Network access control

Objective 4.2Checking who and what connects before giving network access, on wired, wireless and virtual networks.

Network access control (NAC) allows "access based on a user's credentials" and the results of health checks on the device (NIST). The usual foundation is IEEE 802.1X (current revision 802.1X-2020), port-based network access control:

  1. SupplicantThe device (or its user) asks to connect and presents credentials, ideally a device certificate (EAP-TLS).
  2. AuthenticatorThe switch or wireless access point keeps the port closed except for authentication traffic and relays the request.
  3. Authentication serverUsually RADIUS: checks the credentials and the device's posture against policy and tells the authenticator what to allow.
  4. EnforcementThe port opens onto the right VLAN or with the right access list: full access, a restricted network, or a quarantine network for remediation.
  • Pre-admission vs post-admission: check before joining (posture: patch level, encryption, EDR running), and keep checking after, removing devices that become unhealthy or behave badly.
  • Agent-based vs agentless: an agent gives detailed posture; agentless profiling (by traffic and fingerprints) covers printers, cameras and IoT devices that cannot run one. MAC address authentication is weak because addresses can be spoofed, so treat it as a last resort, on restricted VLANs.
  • Physical and virtual solutions: NAC can run on dedicated appliances or as virtual appliances and cloud services, and the same idea applies in virtual and cloud networks through security groups and identity-based policies that decide which workloads may connect.

At the retail group, staff laptops join store networks with device certificates, tills and payment terminals are profiled and placed on the payment VLAN, and an unknown device plugged into a back-office port lands in a quarantine VLAN with an alert.

Endpoint security

Objective 4.2Protecting each host on its own, so security does not depend on the network around it.

  • Anti-malware: SP 800-53 SI-3 calls for "signature-based, non-signature-based" malicious code protection; signatures catch known malware, behavior analysis the rest.
  • Endpoint detection and response (EDR): records process, file, network and user activity on each host, detects suspicious behavior, and lets responders isolate the host and investigate remotely. XDR (extended detection and response) correlates endpoint data with network, email, identity and cloud data. (NIST lists both terms without definitions; these are the common meanings.)
  • Host-based firewall (SC-7(12)) and host-based IDS/IPS (SI-4(23)): filter and monitor traffic on the host itself, which still works when the device is on a hotel or home network.
  • Application allowlisting: a list of "applications and application components that are authorized for use" (SP 800-167), enforced as "deny-all, permit-by-exception" (CM-7(5)). Strong against malware and ransomware, but needs maintenance as software changes.
  • Hardening and patching: secure baselines, removal of unneeded software and services, no local administrator rights, full-disk encryption, and fast patching.
  • Mobile device management for phones and tablets: enforce screen locks, encryption and updates, separate work and personal data, and wipe lost devices.

Endpoints are the new perimeter. With remote work, cloud services and zero trust, the device itself must be trusted (healthy, managed, monitored) before it gets access. Endpoint posture is an input to NAC and zero trust policy decisions, which ties this page to micro-segmentation.

Network devices and how to harden them

Objective 4.2Knowing what each network component does for security, and securing the components themselves.

  • Switches (layer 2): VLANs, port security, 802.1X, DHCP snooping and dynamic ARP inspection.
  • Routers (layer 3): route between networks, apply access control lists, and filter routes; protect routing protocols with authentication.
  • Firewalls: stateful filtering between zones; next-generation firewalls add application and user awareness and intrusion prevention (objective 7.7).
  • Proxies and gateways: forward proxies and secure web gateways control outbound web use; reverse proxies and load balancers terminate TLS and protect applications; email gateways filter messages.
  • Wireless access points and controllers: enforce WPA3 and 802.1X, separate SSIDs, and detect rogue devices.
  • VPN concentrators and remote access gateways: high-value, internet-facing targets that need fast patching and close monitoring (objective 4.3).

Hardening applies to all of them, because a compromised network device can see and change everything that passes through it:

  1. Secure managementOut-of-band management network, SSH and HTTPS only, no Telnet or plain HTTP, SNMPv3 instead of older versions.
  2. Strong administrationNamed accounts through AAA (TACACS+ or RADIUS) with MFA; no shared or default credentials; command logging.
  3. Minimal servicesDisable unused services, ports and protocols; apply secure configuration baselines.
  4. Firmware and supportSigned firmware from the vendor, prompt patching, and replacement before end of support.
  5. Configuration controlConfigurations backed up, versioned and compared against the baseline; changes through change management.
  6. LoggingDevice logs and flow data sent to the SIEM, with alerts on configuration changes outside change control.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the unknown device
Situation
An unknown laptop plugged into a back-office port at a store gets full network access.
Best response
Enable 802.1X NAC so unauthenticated devices land in a quarantine VLAN, disable unused ports, and alert on new devices.
Why
NAC checks devices before granting access; physical ports are an easy entry point.
Scenario 2: the old firewall
Situation
Forty store firewalls reach end of support next year, and budget is tight.
Best response
Plan and fund replacement before the date; meanwhile monitor them closely and limit their exposure, with a time-limited, owner-approved exception if needed.
Why
Unsupported edge devices are prime targets; CISA BOD 26-02 shows the expected urgency.
Scenario 3: the copper link between buildings
Situation
Two distribution center buildings are linked by a long copper cable that suffers interference and occasional outages.
Best response
Replace it with fiber, which covers the distance, is immune to electromagnetic interference and is harder to tap.
Why
Media choice affects both availability and confidentiality.
Scenario 4: the router change
Situation
A router configuration changed overnight with no change ticket, opening a new route to the payment network.
Best response
Treat it as a possible incident: revert to the approved configuration, investigate who made the change and how, and add alerts on out-of-process changes.
Why
Unauthorized configuration changes to network devices are a sign of compromise or process failure.

Key takeaways

  • Infrastructure: redundant power, devices and paths with tested failover; warranty and support contracts; replace unsupported components (SA-22); harden and manage out of band. BOD 26-02: inventory, replace and continuously discover end-of-support edge devices.
  • Media: copper emits and can be tapped; fiber resists interference and tapping; wireless reaches outside, so encrypt. Protect cabling physically (PE-4); emanations, TEMPEST and PE-19.
  • Signal quality: attenuation, noise, EMI and RFI, crosstalk cause errors and outages; respect distance limits and separate data from power.
  • NAC: 802.1X supplicant, authenticator, RADIUS server; posture checks before and after admission; quarantine VLANs; agentless profiling for IoT; MAC authentication is weak.
  • Endpoints: anti-malware (SI-3), EDR and XDR, host firewall and HIDS, application allowlisting (deny-all, permit-by-exception), hardening, encryption, MDM.
  • Devices: switches, routers, firewalls, proxies and gateways, wireless controllers, VPN gateways; harden with out-of-band management, SSH and SNMPv3, AAA with MFA, minimal services, signed firmware, configuration control and logging.
