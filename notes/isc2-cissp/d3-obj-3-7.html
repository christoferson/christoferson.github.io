Cryptanalytic and implementation attacks

Objective 3.7Telling attacks on the mathematics apart from attacks on how cryptography is built and used.

Classic cryptanalytic attacks are defined by what the attacker has:

AttackAttacker hasDefense
Brute forceCiphertext or a hash, and time: tries every key or passwordLong keys; slow, salted password hashing; lockouts
Ciphertext onlyOnly ciphertextModern algorithms resist it; it is the weakest attack position
Known plaintextSome plaintext and its matching ciphertextAlgorithms resistant to key recovery from pairs
Chosen plaintextCan get plaintext of their choice encryptedRandomized encryption (proper IVs and nonces)
Chosen ciphertextCan get ciphertext of their choice decrypted, or learn whether it decryptsAuthenticated encryption; uniform error messages
Frequency analysisCiphertext from a simple substitution cipher: counts letter patternsAny modern cipher; works only on classical ciphers

Most real breaks are implementation attacks: they target how the algorithm is built or run rather than its mathematics.

  • Side-channel attacks: "enabled by the leakage of information from a physical cryptosystem" (NIST), such as power use, electromagnetic emissions or sound.
  • Timing attacks: a side channel in which differences in how long operations take reveal key bits or whether a guess was partly right. Defense: constant-time code.
  • Fault injection: deliberately causing errors (voltage or clock glitches, lasers, heat) so the device skips a check or produces faulty output that reveals the key. Defenses: tamper-responsive hardware and checking results before releasing them.
  • Others: weak random numbers, reused nonces, padding oracles, and keys left in memory or code.

Attacks on authentication and systems

Objective 3.7Recognizing attacks that steal or abuse credentials and keys, or hold data hostage, and how to stop them.

  • Adversary-in-the-middle (MITRE ATT&CK T1557): the attacker positions themselves between two parties to read, change or replay traffic, for example through ARP poisoning, DHCP spoofing, name-resolution poisoning or an evil-twin Wi-Fi access point. AitM phishing kits proxy a real login page and steal session cookies, which defeats one-time codes. Defenses: TLS with certificate validation, mutual authentication, phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2), and switch protections.
  • Pass the hash (T1550.002): attackers "pass the hash" using stolen password hashes to move laterally, authenticating without ever knowing the password (a weakness of NTLM). Defenses: limit privileged logons to hardened admin workstations, unique local admin passwords, credential protection features, and moving off NTLM.
  • Kerberos exploitation (T1558): Kerberoasting requests service tickets and cracks them offline to recover service account passwords (weakest with RC4 encryption); a golden ticket is forged with the domain's ticket-signing (KRBTGT) key and grants access anywhere; a silver ticket is forged with one service's key. Defenses: long random service account passwords or managed service accounts, AES only, protecting domain controllers, and resetting the KRBTGT key twice after a compromise.
  • Ransomware: encrypts (and often steals) data and demands payment. Attackers use cryptography as the weapon, and go after backups first. Defenses: offline, immutable and tested backups, segmentation, EDR, patching and MFA (CC 5.3).
  • Zero-day attacks: "an attack that exploits a previously unknown hardware, firmware, or software vulnerability" (CNSSI 4009). No patch exists, so defense in depth, least privilege, behavior-based detection and fast patching once a fix arrives matter. CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog is meant as "an input to" patch prioritization.
  • AI threats: adversarial AI (inputs crafted to make a model misclassify or misbehave, including prompt injection), model and data poisoning (NIST AI 100-2: an adversary controls some training data, or the model itself, which can hit integrity or availability) and deepfakes (synthetic voice or video used to impersonate executives). Defenses: data provenance and validation, adversarial testing, monitoring model behavior, and call-back verification for payment or access requests, however convincing the voice.

Spot the root cause. Pass the hash and golden tickets are not cryptographic breaks: the attacker steals or forges a credential. The fixes are credential hygiene and protecting the systems that hold the secrets, not stronger algorithms.

Defending against cryptanalytic attacks

Objective 3.7Building defenses that address the realistic ways cryptography is broken.

Because modern algorithms rarely fall to pure mathematics, defenses focus on everything around them:

  • Use strong, current algorithms and key lengths, retire weak ones on schedule (objective 3.6), and plan the post-quantum migration.
  • Use validated implementations: FIPS 140-3 modules and well-maintained libraries, with constant-time code and protections against side channels and fault injection in hardware.
  • Protect keys and credentials: hardware storage, no hard-coded secrets, short-lived tokens, and credential protection on endpoints so hashes and tickets cannot be stolen easily.
  • Authenticate channels properly: certificate validation, mutual authentication and phishing-resistant MFA defeat most adversary-in-the-middle attacks.
  • Limit the value of a break: forward secrecy in TLS (a stolen server key does not decrypt past sessions), key separation by purpose, and segmentation.
  • Detect misuse: alerts on unusual Kerberos ticket requests, logons with reused hashes, certificate anomalies and mass encryption activity.
  • Prepare to recover: offline and immutable backups for ransomware, and procedures to revoke and replace keys and certificates quickly after a compromise.

For AI threats, add data provenance and validation for training data, adversarial testing of models, monitoring for drift and abuse, and human verification of high-value requests that arrive by voice or video.

Ransomware, zero-days and AI threats in depth

Objective 3.7Understanding the three newest items in the outline well enough to plan defenses.

Ransomware turns cryptography against its owner: attackers encrypt systems and data, and usually steal data first so they can threaten to publish it (double extortion). Typical entry points named in CISA's #StopRansomware guidance include exposed remote desktop services, phishing, and exploitation of unpatched internet-facing systems; attackers then escalate privileges, disable security tools, and target backups before encrypting. Defenses work at each stage: remove exposure and patch, phishing-resistant MFA, least privilege and segmentation, EDR, offline and immutable backups that are tested, and an incident plan that includes legal advice on payments, since the US Treasury's OFAC strongly discourages paying and warns about sanctions (objective 1.9).

Zero-day attacks exploit "a previously unknown hardware, firmware, or software vulnerability", so no patch exists when they start. Once a fix is published the flaw becomes an "n-day", and attackers race to exploit systems that are slow to patch, which is why known exploited vulnerabilities get the shortest deadlines (objective 7.8). Because prevention by patching is impossible at first, defenses rely on reducing attack surface, least privilege, exploit mitigations (objective 3.4), behavior-based detection, segmentation that limits spread, temporary mitigations such as WAF rules or disabling a feature, and fast patching once a fix arrives.

AI threats fall into three groups:

  • Attacks on AI systems: adversarial examples that cause wrong outputs (evasion), data and model poisoning that plants errors or backdoors (NIST notes poisoning can hit integrity or availability), prompt injection that hijacks a model's instructions, and model extraction or training-data extraction that steals the model or the data behind it.
  • AI used by attackers: convincing phishing at scale, faster vulnerability discovery and exploit development, and automated reconnaissance.
  • Deepfakes: synthetic voice and video used to impersonate executives or bypass weak identity checks, as in fraudulent payment requests.

MITRE ATLAS catalogs techniques against AI systems, and NIST AI 100-2 classifies them by attacker goal; both are useful when threat modeling an AI service (objective 1.10).

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the timing difference
Situation
A tester notices that the API rejects wrong passwords faster when the first characters are wrong.
Best response
Fix the comparison to run in constant time, and add rate limiting and lockout to the endpoint.
Why
Timing differences are a side channel that can reveal secrets character by character.
Scenario 2: the golden ticket
Situation
Investigators find Kerberos tickets with impossible lifetimes for accounts across the domain after a breach of a domain controller.
Best response
Treat the domain as compromised: remove the attacker's access, reset the KRBTGT key twice, rebuild affected domain controllers, and review privileged accounts.
Why
Forged tickets mean the ticket-signing key was stolen; only replacing that key invalidates them.
Scenario 3: the fake bank site
Situation
Staff on hotel Wi-Fi report a certificate warning on the banking site and some clicked through.
Best response
Treat it as a possible adversary-in-the-middle attack: reset affected credentials, train staff never to bypass certificate warnings, and require phishing-resistant MFA and the corporate VPN.
Why
Certificate validation is what defeats interception; clicking through removes it.
Scenario 4: the CFO's voice
Situation
A finance clerk receives a call that sounds exactly like the CFO, urgently requesting a supplier payment to a new account.
Best response
Verify through a known channel (call back on the number in the directory) and follow the dual-approval process before paying.
Why
Deepfake voices defeat recognition; process controls and out-of-band verification do not depend on it.

Key takeaways

  • Classic attacks by attacker knowledge: brute force, ciphertext only (weakest position), known plaintext, chosen plaintext, chosen ciphertext; frequency analysis breaks only classical ciphers.
  • Implementation attacks are the common real breaks: side channels (power, emissions), timing, fault injection, weak randomness, nonce reuse, padding oracles, keys in memory or code.
  • Credential and protocol attacks: adversary-in-the-middle (T1557), pass the hash (T1550.002), Kerberoasting and golden or silver tickets (T1558); these steal or forge credentials rather than break algorithms.
  • Ransomware uses cryptography as a weapon; zero-days exploit unknown flaws; AI threats include adversarial inputs, poisoning and deepfakes.
  • Defenses: strong current algorithms, validated constant-time implementations, protected keys and credentials, authenticated channels, forward secrecy, detection of misuse, and tested recovery.
