Overview

Objective 3.8

3.8 Apply security principles to site and facility design.

Physical control types are introduced in CC 1.4; the specific controls for rooms, utilities, fire and power are on the 3.9 page; daily physical security operations on the 7.14 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its own data center sits on a business park near a river; every store has a small network cabinet in the back office.

Security principles in site design

Objective 3.8Applying the same design principles to buildings as to systems: layers, least privilege, and planning for failure.

Familiar principles carry over to the physical world: defense in depth (perimeter, grounds, building, floor, room, rack), least privilege (people can enter only the zones they need), separation of duties, fail securely (but doors on escape routes must fail safe so people can get out: life safety always comes first), and zoning, with the most sensitive areas at the center of the building, away from exterior walls, lobbies and loading bays.

Site selection weighs natural hazards (floods, earthquakes, storms, wildfires), crime rates, access for emergency services, distance from hazards such as airports, chemical plants or rail lines, and the availability of power and network providers from different routes.

Crime prevention through environmental design (CPTED) is, in the International CPTED Association's words, "a multi-disciplinary approach to crime prevention that uses urban and architectural design" and the management of the environment. Its classic principles:

  • Natural surveillance: lighting, landscaping and clear sight lines so intruders are easily seen.
  • Natural access control: paths, fences, planters and building layout that steer people to controlled entrances.
  • Territorial reinforcement: clear boundaries and signs that make it obvious a space is owned and watched.
  • Maintenance: a cared-for site signals that people are paying attention; broken lights and fences signal the opposite.

Site selection and threat assessment

Objective 3.8Choosing and assessing locations by the threats they face and the services they depend on.

Facility security starts before construction or lease, with an all-hazards assessment of the location:

  • Natural hazards: flood plains, earthquake zones, storm and wildfire exposure, extreme heat; history and climate projections both matter.
  • Human-made and environmental hazards: nearby chemical plants, rail lines and pipelines, airports, crime rates, civil unrest, and neighbors whose activities attract attention.
  • Utilities and services: diverse power feeds from different substations, at least two network carriers entering by separate routes, water supply, and response times for fire, police and medical services.
  • Access and visibility: transport for staff and deliveries, but a low profile for sensitive facilities (no signs announcing a data center), and space for setbacks and vehicle barriers.
  • Shared buildings: in multi-tenant offices or colocation data centers, the landlord or provider controls the perimeter, lobby and often power and cooling; contracts must cover their security, access logs and incident notification.

SP 800-53 PE-18 (location of system components) asks organizations to position equipment where physical and environmental hazards and unauthorized access are least likely to reach it, and PE-17 covers security at alternate work sites, including homes. The assessment is repeated when the threat picture changes, such as new flood maps or a change in local crime.

For a new distribution center, the retail group rejected the cheapest site because it sat in a 100-year flood zone with a single power feed, choosing one with two substations and higher ground.

Zones, layers and facility layout

Objective 3.8Laying out a facility so that sensitive areas are hard to reach and every layer delays and detects an intruder.

Physical security works in concentric layers, each meant to deter, detect, delay and allow a response:

  1. PerimeterFences, gates, vehicle barriers, lighting and cameras at the property line.
  2. Grounds and approachClear sight lines, controlled parking, separation of deliveries from visitors.
  3. Building envelopeFew entrances, hardened doors and windows, controlled loading docks, alarmed emergency exits.
  4. Interior zonesPublic, staff-only, restricted and highly restricted areas, each needing more authorization than the last.
  5. Rooms and racksServer rooms, wiring closets, media stores and evidence rooms, with locked racks and cages inside.

Layout principles: put the most sensitive rooms deep inside, away from exterior walls, lobbies, loading docks and the top or bottom floors (roof leaks, flooding); route visitors so they never pass through restricted areas; separate the mail and delivery room, which receives unknown packages; keep utility entry points (power, water, network) protected and monitored; and make sure walls of secure rooms run from true floor to true ceiling. Natural controls (CPTED) and mechanical controls (locks, badges, alarms) work together, and every layer should produce an alert when breached so that response arrives before the intruder reaches the next one.

Life safety constrains the layout. Fire codes require exits and evacuation routes that stay usable; secure doors on escape routes must let people out even when power or access control fails. Designs that trap people to protect assets are always wrong.

Designing different kinds of site

Objective 3.8Applying the same principles to data centers, stores, offices and remote workers.

  • Data centers: low profile, single controlled entrance with an access control vestibule, no windows, layered zones down to locked racks, redundant power and cooling, fire suppression suited to equipment, and full monitoring.
  • Offices: public reception separated from staff areas, badge-controlled floors, clear desk policies, and secure rooms for sensitive work; visitors escorted.
  • Retail stores: the network cabinet and cash office in a locked back area, cameras covering tills and entrances, staff-only doors alarmed, and payment terminals inspected for tampering.
  • Remote and home workers: no physical perimeter to rely on, so controls shift to the device (encryption, screen locks), secure remote access, and rules on printing and private workspaces (PE-17).
  • Edge and unattended sites: kiosks, cell sites and small cabinets need tamper-evident, locked enclosures, alarms and remote monitoring, since no one is present.

The same questions apply everywhere: what is valuable here, who needs to reach it, what threatens it, and which layers will detect and delay an intruder long enough for someone to respond?

Physical security in the security program

Objective 3.8Treating physical and cyber security as one program, with shared risk assessment, ownership and measurement.

Physical and logical security protect the same assets and increasingly use the same technology: badge systems, cameras, alarms and building controls are networked computers, and an attacker who walks into a wiring closet bypasses the firewall. Good programs bring them together:

  • One risk assessment: physical threats (theft, intrusion, sabotage, natural hazards) are assessed alongside cyber threats for the same assets, and treated by the same risk owners.
  • Clear ownership: facilities, physical security and IT security agree who owns which controls, especially for shared systems such as badge readers and cameras, which need patching, segmentation and monitoring like any IT system.
  • Integrated access management: joiners, movers and leavers update badges and accounts together; physical access to sensitive zones is reviewed with logical access.
  • Shared monitoring and response: alarms, camera events and logical security alerts reach the same operations center, so a badge used in two cities at once, or a door forced open next to the server room, is correlated and acted on.
  • Testing: physical penetration tests and social engineering tests (tailgating, pretexting at reception) check the layers as a whole (objective 6.2).
  • Measurement: alarm response times, door-held and forced-door events, badge reconciliation results and test findings reported with other security metrics.

Design choices are cost-benefit decisions like any control: a guard force is flexible but expensive; cameras deter and record but need someone to watch or review; locks are cheap but only as good as key control. Combining natural controls (CPTED, layout), mechanical controls and people usually gives the best protection for the cost.

Designing for security and life safety together

Objective 3.8Resolving conflicts between keeping intruders out and letting people out.

  • Escape routes: doors on exit paths must open from inside without special knowledge, even if that weakens access control; use alarms on emergency exits rather than locks.
  • Fail-safe versus fail-secure: fail-safe locks open on power loss (for people), fail-secure locks stay locked (for assets); choose per door based on what is behind it and fire codes.
  • Codes and authorities: building and fire codes set minimum requirements; involve fire authorities and safety engineers early in design.
  • Accessibility: controls must allow people with disabilities to enter, move and evacuate safely.

Where the two conflict, life safety wins, and security is achieved by other layers such as monitoring, alarms and inner zones.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the ground-floor server room
Situation
A new office design puts the server room on the ground floor next to the loading dock, behind a glass wall "so visitors can see our technology".
Best response
Move it to an interior room on a middle floor with solid walls, no windows and controlled access; showcase technology elsewhere.
Why
Sensitive rooms belong deep inside, away from exterior walls, loading areas and public view.
Scenario 2: the locked fire exit
Situation
To stop tailgating through a rear fire exit, a manager proposes chaining it shut outside business hours.
Best response
Keep the exit usable; add an alarm that sounds when it opens, a camera, and a delayed-egress device only if fire codes allow.
Why
Life safety always wins; exits must remain usable, so detect misuse instead of blocking escape.
Scenario 3: the colocation contract
Situation
The retail group moves its secondary data center into a colocation facility. The draft contract says nothing about physical access logs.
Best response
Add requirements for access control, logs and their availability, visitor escort, incident notification, and the right to audit or receive attestation reports.
Why
The provider controls the outer layers; security responsibilities for them must be written into the contract.
Scenario 4: the CPTED fix
Situation
Staff report people loitering in a dark, overgrown corner of the distribution center car park at night.
Best response
Improve lighting and sight lines (trim landscaping), add signage and camera coverage, and maintain the area.
Why
CPTED: natural surveillance, territorial reinforcement and maintenance deter crime cheaply.
Scenario 5: the locked fire exit
Situation
To stop stock theft, a store manager chains a rear fire exit shut.
Best response
Remove the chain immediately and use an alarmed exit device and cameras instead.
Why
Life safety always takes priority over asset protection.

Key takeaways

  • Principles: defense in depth, least privilege and zoning, fail securely for assets but fail-safe for people, most sensitive areas at the center.
  • CPTED: natural surveillance, natural access control, territorial reinforcement, maintenance.
  • Site selection: all-hazards assessment (natural, human-made, utilities, emergency services, visibility), shared-building contracts; PE-17 and PE-18.
  • Layers: perimeter, grounds, envelope, interior zones, rooms and racks; each deters, detects and delays so response arrives in time; true floor-to-ceiling walls; protected utility entries.
  • Life safety constrains every design: exits stay usable.
  • Site types: data centers, offices, stores, remote workers and unattended sites apply the same questions differently.
