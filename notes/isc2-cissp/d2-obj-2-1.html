2.1: Identifying and classifying information and assets

Deciding how sensitive and how critical each piece of information and each asset is, so that protection is applied consistently and in proportion: the schemes, the process, who decides, and how systems inherit a rating from the data they hold.

Overview

Objective 2.1

2.1 Identify and classify information and assets. Sub-topics: data classification; asset classification.

Classification basics, labeling and masking are in CC 5.1. Handling rules for each class are on the 2.2 page, and ownership and inventories on the 2.3 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its information ranges from published prices to card data, customer profiles, product designs and pricing algorithms.

Why classify

Objective 2.1Understanding what classification achieves and what it depends on.

NIST describes data classification as "the process an organization uses to characterize its data assets using persistent labels so those assets can be managed properly" (draft IR 8496). Without it, an organization either protects everything at the highest level (expensive and unworkable) or protects nothing consistently. Classification lets each level carry a defined set of controls, so people and systems know how to handle data without deciding case by case.

It depends on identification first: you cannot classify data you do not know you have. CSF 2.0 expects "inventories of data and corresponding metadata for designated data types" (ID.AM-07) and assets prioritized "based on classification, criticality, resources, and impact" (ID.AM-05). Data discovery tools that scan file shares, databases, cloud storage and SaaS for sensitive patterns help find data no one recorded, such as card numbers in an old spreadsheet.

Classification schemes

Objective 2.1Knowing government and commercial schemes and how to design a workable one.

US government: classified national security information has three levels under Executive Order 13526, defined by the damage unauthorized disclosure could cause to national security: Top Secret ("exceptionally grave damage"), Secret ("serious damage") and Confidential ("damage"). The order says no other terms may be used for classified information, and when in doubt the lower level applies. Separately, Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) covers sensitive but unclassified information that law or policy requires to be protected; it replaced older labels such as "For Official Use Only", has categories in a public registry (for example privacy), and must be protected by non-federal organizations under NIST SP 800-171.

Commercial organizations choose their own scheme; there is no official standard. A typical four-level scheme:

  • Public: approved for anyone (published prices, press releases).
  • Internal: for staff; limited harm if disclosed (internal procedures, org charts).
  • Confidential: significant harm if disclosed (customer data, contracts, financial forecasts).
  • Restricted: severe harm (card data, authentication secrets, unreleased results, trade secrets).

Good schemes have few levels (three to five), plain names, clear criteria with examples, and controls defined per level. Some add categories or caveats alongside levels, such as "personal data" or "legal privilege", because different laws apply to them even at the same sensitivity.

The classification process

Objective 2.1Running classification as an owned, repeatable process from creation to declassification.

  1. Set policy and schemeSenior management approves the levels, criteria and roles.
  2. IdentifyFind information and assets through inventories and discovery.
  3. ClassifyThe owner decides the level using the criteria: value, sensitivity, legal and contractual obligations, and impact of disclosure, change or loss.
  4. LabelApply human-readable markings and machine-readable labels; NIST notes "a label is a metadata attribute that represents a data classification."
  5. ProtectApply the controls defined for the level (objective 2.2).
  6. ReviewReclassify when value or sensitivity changes; declassify or downgrade when appropriate (quarterly results become public after announcement).

NIST defines the information owner as the "official with statutory or operational authority for specified information and responsibility for establishing the controls for its generation, collection, processing, dissemination, and disposal." Classification criteria commonly considered:

  • Value to the organization and to competitors or attackers.
  • Legal, regulatory and contractual requirements (GDPR, PCI DSS, NDAs).
  • Impact on confidentiality, integrity and availability if compromised.
  • Age and lifetime: some information loses sensitivity over time.
  • Aggregation: combined data can be more sensitive than its parts, so a data set that joins customer names, purchase histories and locations may need a higher level than each source.

The owner classifies; the custodian protects. If a question asks who determines the classification of a data set, the answer is the data owner (a business manager), not IT, the security team or the custodian. The security team defines the scheme and the controls for each level; the custodian implements them.

Over-classification is a real problem: if everything is "Restricted", people stop taking the label seriously and controls become unworkable. Default to the lowest level that fits, with clear examples, and audit samples periodically.

Classifying assets and systems

Objective 2.1Rating systems and other assets by the most sensitive information they hold and by how critical they are.

Assets take their classification from the information they store, process or transmit, and from how critical they are to operations. In the US federal approach, FIPS 199 rates each information type as low, moderate or high impact for confidentiality, integrity and availability, and a system takes the high-water mark: the highest rating for each objective across all the information it holds (objective 1.2). A laptop that holds one restricted file is a restricted asset; a server hosting both public web pages and customer records is rated by the customer records.

Criticality is a second dimension. A store's till may hold mostly internal data, but it is business-critical because sales stop without it. Sensitivity drives confidentiality controls (encryption, access control); criticality drives availability controls (redundancy, recovery priority from the BIA, objective 1.7). Many organizations record both for every asset.

Asset classes beyond systems: physical assets (buildings, equipment, media), intangible assets (intellectual property, brand, licenses), and people and suppliers who hold critical knowledge or access. The retail group's asset register gives each item a sensitivity class, a criticality tier and an owner, which together decide its controls, its patch priority and where it sits in recovery plans.

Classifying AI data and outputs

Objective 2.1Extending classification to training data, prompts, models and AI-generated output.

  • Training and fine-tuning data carries the classification of its sources; personal data keeps its legal obligations.
  • Models and weights are valuable intellectual property and can leak training data, so classify them at least at the level of their most sensitive training data.
  • Outputs: joint guidance from CISA, NSA and FBI says "the output of AI systems should be classified at the same level as the input data."
  • Prompts and logs often contain whatever users pasted in, so they need classification and retention rules too.

Labels help enforce this: data loss prevention and AI gateways can block content labeled Restricted from being sent to AI tools that are not approved for it.

Labeling and marking in practice

Objective 2.1Applying labels people and systems can read, keeping them correct as data moves, and avoiding common classification failures.

A classification is only useful if it travels with the information. Two kinds of label work together:

  • Human-readable markings: headers and footers on documents, banners on screens and email subject tags, labels on folders, drives and tapes. US CUI rules, for example, make a banner marking of "CUI" or "CONTROLLED" mandatory on CUI documents.
  • Machine-readable labels: metadata attached to files, emails, database columns or cloud objects, which tools use to enforce rules: encrypting a document labeled Restricted, blocking it from leaving the organization, or watermarking it when printed.

Practical rules that keep labels accurate:

  • Label at creation, with a sensible default (often Internal) so nothing is unlabeled; ask users to raise the level when needed rather than to remember to add one.
  • Inheritance: derived content (an extract, a summary, an AI output) takes at least the level of its most sensitive source, and labels follow copies into new locations.
  • Automatic classification: content inspection that recognizes card numbers, national identifiers or project code names can suggest or apply labels; combine it with user judgment, since patterns miss context.
  • Downgrading: only the owner or an authorized person may lower a label, and the change is logged.
  • Structured data: classify databases at the column or field level, so a table with one restricted column can still be queried safely for the rest.

Common failures to watch for: schemes with too many levels that nobody can tell apart; everything marked Confidential "to be safe"; labels that are never reviewed, so last year's sensitive plans stay restricted forever; copies in email, chat and personal storage that lose their labels; and classification treated as a one-off project instead of part of everyday work. Periodic sampling by the data owners and the security team, comparing labels with actual content, shows how well the scheme is working.

At the retail group, documents and emails carry sensitivity labels that default to Internal; the DLP engine raises any file containing card numbers to Restricted and encrypts it automatically, and each quarter data owners review a sample of their labeled files.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying classification reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: who decides
Situation
A new data set of supplier pricing agreements is created. The database administrator asks what classification to apply.
Best answer
The data owner (the head of procurement) classifies it using the scheme's criteria; the administrator then applies the controls for that level.
Why
Owners classify and are accountable; custodians implement.
Scenario 2: the combined report
Situation
Analysts combine store-level sales (Internal) with loyalty member locations (Confidential) to produce a report predicting which stores will close.
Best answer
Classify the report on its own merits, likely Restricted: the combination reveals unannounced decisions and personal data together.
Why
Aggregation can make the whole more sensitive than any part; the derived report needs its own classification.
Scenario 3: the mixed server
Situation
A server hosts the public product catalog and, on another partition, the customer returns database. The team wants to rate it "low" because most of its traffic is public.
Best answer
Rate the server by the high-water mark of its information, the customer data; or separate the workloads so the public catalog can run with lighter controls.
Why
A system takes the highest impact level of the information it holds, not the most common.
Scenario 4: the published results
Situation
Last quarter's financial results, classified Restricted while being prepared, were announced publicly this morning. The finance folder is still labeled Restricted and its access list is tiny.
Best response
The data owner reviews and downgrades the published figures to Public (keeping working papers and forecasts at their own level), and the change is recorded.
Why
Classification should follow current sensitivity; only the owner may downgrade, and over-classified data makes controls harder to apply where they matter.

Key takeaways

  • Classification characterizes data with persistent labels so it is protected consistently and in proportion; it depends on knowing what data exists (inventories, discovery).
  • Schemes: US government Top Secret, Secret, Confidential (EO 13526) plus CUI (SP 800-171 for non-federal); commercial schemes are the organization's own (public, internal, confidential, restricted); few, clear levels plus categories.
  • Process: policy, identify, owner classifies by value, law, impact, age and aggregation, label, protect, review and declassify. Avoid over-classification.
  • Assets inherit the high-water mark of their information (FIPS 199) and add criticality; sensitivity drives protection, criticality drives recovery priority.
  • AI: training data, models, prompts and outputs are classified; outputs at least at the level of their inputs.
