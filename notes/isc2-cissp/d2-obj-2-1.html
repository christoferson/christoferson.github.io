2.1–2.3: Classifying, handling and provisioning assets

Deciding how valuable and sensitive each piece of information and each asset is, setting the rules for handling it physically and digitally from creation to destruction, and keeping owned, inventoried assets under control as they are issued and used.

Overview

Objectives 2.1, 2.2 and 2.3

2.1 Identify and classify information and assets. 2.2 Establish information and asset handling requirements. 2.3 Provision information and assets securely.

Asset security starts with knowing what you have and how much it matters. The detail behind most of this page is in the CC guide: classification, labeling, masking, sanitization and encryption in CC 5.1, and inventories and the asset life cycle in CC 5.4. This page draws them together the way CISSP questions do: who decides, which rule applies, and in what order. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its assets range from store tills and card data to its brand, its product designs and its pricing algorithms.

Classifying data

Objective 2.1Assigning information to sensitivity levels, by its owner, so it receives consistent protection.

Classification puts information into a small number of levels by the harm its disclosure, alteration or loss would cause. The data owner, a business manager, decides the level; the security team defines the scheme and the controls for each level. Two families of scheme:

  • Government: in the US, classified information has three levels (Top Secret, "exceptionally grave damage" to national security; Secret, "serious damage"; Confidential, "damage") under Executive Order 13526, plus Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) for sensitive unclassified data.
  • Commercial: organizations choose their own, typically four levels such as public, internal, confidential and restricted. There is no official commercial standard.

Good classification practice: keep levels few and clearly defined, classify at creation, label in ways both people and systems can read, review classifications periodically (data often becomes less sensitive over time, such as quarterly results once published), and set a rule for combined data, which takes the highest level of its parts or higher, because aggregation can reveal more than any single piece.

The owner classifies; the custodian protects. If a question asks who determines the classification of a data set, the answer is the data owner, not IT, the security team or the custodian. The custodian implements the controls the classification requires.

Classifying assets and systems

Objective 2.1Rating systems and other assets by the most sensitive information they hold and by how critical they are to the business.

Assets take their classification from the information they store or process, and from how critical they are to operations. In the US federal approach, FIPS 199 rates each information type as low, moderate or high impact for confidentiality, integrity and availability, and a system takes the high-water mark: the highest rating for each objective across all the information it holds (CC 1.1). A laptop that holds one restricted file is a restricted asset.

Criticality adds a second dimension: a store's till system may hold only internal data most of the time, but it is business-critical because sales stop without it. The retail group records both a sensitivity class and a criticality rating for each asset, which drive different controls: encryption and access control for sensitivity, redundancy and recovery priority for criticality (the BIA in objective 1.7).

Physical and digital handling

Objective 2.2Writing handling rules for each classification level that cover marking, storage, transport, sharing and use, on paper and in electronic form.

Handling requirements translate each classification into practical rules. SP 800-53 MP-3 requires marking media with "distribution limitations, handling caveats, and applicable security markings", AC-16 covers machine-readable labels, and the media protection family also covers storing media securely in controlled areas and protecting and accounting for it during transport. A handling matrix makes the rules usable:

Handling rules: Restricted (retail group)
Marking
"RESTRICTED" in header and footer; metadata label applied automatically
Paper storage
Locked cabinet in an access-controlled room; clear desk at all times
Electronic storage
Approved encrypted repositories only; no personal devices or personal cloud
Transport
Paper by tracked courier in sealed envelopes; removable media encrypted and logged out and back in
Sharing
Named individuals only; external sharing needs owner approval and an agreement
Printing
Secure print release; collect immediately
AI tools
Not to be entered into any AI service
Destruction
Cross-cut shredding for paper; purge or destroy for media (below)

Data loss prevention tools enforce the electronic rules, and physical controls such as clear desk, secure printing and visitor escorts enforce the paper ones. Handling rules apply through the whole life cycle, including backups and archived copies, which must be protected at the same level as the original.

Secure media destruction and encryption

Objective 2.2Choosing the right sanitization method for each medium and level, and protecting data with encryption at rest and in transit.

NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 (September 2025) defines three levels, explained in CC 5.1: clear (logical overwrite or reset, for reuse inside the organization), purge (recovery infeasible even in a laboratory, including cryptographic erase, for media leaving the organization's control) and destroy (purge-level, and the media can no longer be used: shredding, disintegration, pulverizing, melting, incineration). The choice depends on the classification and on where the media goes next. Paper is destroyed by cross-cut shredding, pulping or burning.

Watch the traps: overwriting SSDs does not reliably remove data; degaussing works only on magnetic media and does not count as destroy; and cryptographic erase is only safe if no copy of the key exists elsewhere. Every sanitized item should have a record (a certificate of sanitization), and SP 800-53 MP-6 requires sanitizing media "prior to disposal, release out of organizational control, or release for reuse."

Encryption protects data in its other states:

  • At rest: full-disk encryption on laptops and servers, database or field-level encryption for the most sensitive data, encrypted backups. Keys are kept separately, ideally in a hardware security module.
  • In transit (in motion): TLS for web and APIs, VPNs or IPsec between sites, encrypted email where needed (objective 4.3).
  • In use: confidential computing keeps data encrypted in memory while it is processed, an emerging option for sensitive workloads such as AI.

Algorithms, key management and the move to post-quantum cryptography are covered under objective 3.6.

Ownership, inventory and provisioning

Objective 2.3Giving every asset an owner and an inventory record, and issuing information and equipment in a secure, controlled state.

Every asset needs a named owner who is accountable for it: deciding its classification, who may use it and when it is retired. Assets are both:

  • Tangible: hardware, devices, media, buildings and equipment.
  • Intangible: data, software and licenses, intellectual property, cloud services, and reputation.

The inventory records them: CSF 2.0 expects inventories of hardware, of "software, services, and systems", of supplier services, and of "data and corresponding metadata" (ID.AM-01, -02, -04, -07), and SP 800-53 CM-8 requires a component inventory that "accurately reflects the system" and is updated at every installation and removal (CC 5.4). Asset management keeps that inventory true across the life cycle, with automated discovery to find what was never recorded.

Provisioning securely means assets are issued in a known, protected state and to the right person:

  1. Request and approveThe owner or manager approves the need; the asset is assigned to a named user in the inventory.
  2. Build to baselineDevices are built from a hardened image, enrolled in management (encryption, endpoint protection, patching) and checked before issue.
  3. Grant access by roleAccess to information follows the classification and least privilege (CC 3.1).
  4. Track and recoverThe asset is tracked while in use, and recovered, sanitized and reissued or disposed of when the user leaves or the asset is retired.

At the retail group, a new store manager's laptop arrives encrypted, enrolled in device management and tagged to them in the inventory before their first day; access to store financial data is granted by role, and the laptop is collected and sanitized on the day they leave.

Further reading

  • NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2, Guidelines for Media Sanitization (NIST, September 2025).
  • FIPS 199, Standards for Security Categorization of Federal Information and Information Systems (NIST, 2004).
  • Controlled Unclassified Information (US National Archives): the CUI program and registry.

Key takeaways

  • Data classification: few clear levels, set by the data owner at creation and reviewed over time. Government: Top Secret, Secret, Confidential, plus CUI. Commercial: the organization's own scheme (public, internal, confidential, restricted). Aggregated data may need a higher level.
  • Asset classification follows the most sensitive data held (FIPS 199 high-water mark) plus business criticality; sensitivity drives protection, criticality drives recovery priority.
  • Handling: per-level rules for marking (MP-3, AC-16), storage, transport, sharing, printing, AI use and destruction, on paper and electronically, including backups.
  • Destruction (SP 800-88 Rev. 2): clear, purge, destroy, chosen by classification and destination; no SSD overwrites, degaussing is not destroy, keep certificates. Encryption at rest, in transit and, increasingly, in use.
  • Provisioning: every asset has an owner and an inventory record (tangible and intangible); issue from a hardened baseline, grant access by role, track, recover and sanitize.
