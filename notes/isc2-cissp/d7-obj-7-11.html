Overview

Objective 7.11

7.11 Implement Disaster Recovery (DR) processes. Sub-topics: response; personnel; communications (methods, prioritization); assessment; restoration; training and awareness; lessons learned; metrics (baseline, mean time to resolve or recover (MTTR), mean time between failures (MTBF)).

Recovery strategies and sites are on the 7.10 page; testing the plans on the 7.12 page; incident handling on the 7.6 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its DR plan fails card payments over between cloud regions and restores store and back-office systems from backups, run by a DR team led by the head of IT operations.

The disaster recovery process

Objective 7.11Running recovery through clear roles, communications, assessment and restoration, and measuring it.

A disaster recovery plan is "a written plan for recovering one or more information systems at an alternate facility in response to a major hardware or software failure or destruction of facilities" (SP 800-34 Rev. 1). The outline's elements:

  1. ResponseDetect and declare the disaster against defined criteria, activate the plan and the DR team, and protect people first.
  2. PersonnelNamed roles with alternates (no single expert), contact lists kept offline, staff safety and welfare, and the authority to spend and decide.
  3. CommunicationsMethods that do not depend on the failed systems (phone trees, out-of-band messaging, satellite phones), and prioritization: emergency services and staff first, then leadership, then customers, regulators, suppliers and media through approved spokespeople.
  4. AssessmentDetermine the damage and expected outage, then choose to recover in place or fail over to the alternate site.
  5. RestorationRecover systems in BIA priority order to a known state (CP-10), verify data integrity, then later reconstitute and fail back to the primary site in a controlled way.
  6. Training and awarenessEveryone with a role knows it and has practiced it; all staff know how to get information during a disaster.
  7. Lessons learnedReview after every activation and test; update plans, contacts and runbooks.

Metrics show whether recovery capability is good enough:

  • Baseline: normal performance and recovery times measured beforehand, so you know what "recovered" means.
  • Mean time to resolve or recover (MTTR): the average time from failure to restored service; compare it with the RTO.
  • Mean time between failures (MTBF): the average operating time between failures of a component or system; a reliability measure that helps plan redundancy and replacement.
  • Achieved RTO and RPO in tests and real events, and the time since the last successful test of each plan.

Declaring a disaster

Objective 7.11Deciding quickly and clearly when normal operations end and the DR plan begins.

  • Criteria: the plan defines when to declare, such as a site unusable for more than a set number of hours, loss of a critical service beyond its RTO, or a destructive cyberattack.
  • Authority: named people (and alternates) can declare a disaster, approve spending and order failover; waiting for a meeting of everyone wastes the recovery window.
  • Escalation path: incidents that grow (an outage that will not be fixed in time, ransomware spreading) escalate from incident management to DR and crisis management.
  • Early decision: failover takes time and failback has costs, so the decision balances the expected outage against the effort; declaring late often means missing the RTO.
  • Notification: once declared, the DR team, leadership, the alternate site or provider, and key suppliers are notified through the out-of-band call tree.

At the retail group, the head of IT operations or either deputy can declare a disaster; card payment failover between regions is automatic, while failover of the data center workloads needs a declaration.

Recovering securely

Objective 7.11Keeping security controls in place when systems are recovered under pressure.

  • Clean before restore: after a cyberattack, find the intrusion route and persistence first, and scan backups and images before restoring, or the attacker returns with the data.
  • Same controls at the alternate site: firewalls, access control, logging and monitoring must work at the recovery site or cloud environment, and be tested there.
  • Emergency access: break-glass accounts and temporary privileges granted for recovery are logged, time-limited and removed afterwards.
  • Credentials: reset passwords, keys and tokens that may be compromised, starting with privileged and service accounts.
  • Data integrity: verify restored data (checksums, reconciliation with transaction records) and decide how to re-enter or recover transactions lost between the last backup and the failure.
  • Physical security: alternate work sites and temporary staff need access control and supervision too.

Speed does not suspend security. Recovery under pressure tempts teams to disable controls or grant broad access "just for now". Plans should state which controls may be relaxed, by whose authority, and how they are restored.

Reconstitution and failback

Objective 7.11Returning to normal operations without a second outage.

SP 800-34 describes the final phase as reconstitution: restoring the primary site or building a new one, testing it, and moving operations back.

  1. Repair or replaceRestore the primary facility and systems, or build replacements, to the approved baselines.
  2. TestVerify systems, security controls and data at the primary site before moving users back.
  3. Synchronize dataCopy changes made at the alternate site back to the primary, with integrity checks.
  4. Fail backMove operations back in a planned window, with rollback possible, as a controlled change.
  5. DeactivateReturn the alternate site to standby, remove temporary accounts and access, and replenish supplies.

Failback is often riskier than failover because it is planned less and tested less. Include it in DR tests.

DR teams and runbooks

Objective 7.11Organizing people and documentation so recovery does not depend on memory.

  • Teams: a DR coordinator, damage assessment, recovery teams by platform (network, identity, servers, databases, applications), communications, and liaison with business units and suppliers.
  • Alternates: at least two trained people for every role, since a disaster may make some staff unavailable.
  • Runbooks: step-by-step recovery procedures for each system, with prerequisites, order, commands, validation checks and contacts, kept current through change management.
  • Offline copies: plans and runbooks stored where they can be reached if the main systems, email and file shares are down.
  • Automation: scripted or infrastructure-as-code recovery reduces errors and time, but the scripts and their credentials must also be recoverable.

Communications in a disaster

Objective 7.11Choosing methods that survive the disaster and putting messages in the right order.

Methods must not depend on the systems that failed:

  • Call trees and mass notification services that send texts, calls and app alerts, with acknowledgment tracking so you know who has not responded.
  • Out-of-band channels for the recovery team (a separate messaging service, phone bridges), especially if corporate email or chat is down or compromised.
  • Satellite phones or radios for sites where public networks may fail.
  • Staff hotlines and a public status page hosted outside the affected environment.

Prioritization puts life safety first: emergency services and staff welfare, then the DR and crisis teams and leadership, then customers, regulators, suppliers and partners, and the media through approved spokespeople. Regulators and contracts may set deadlines, so legal and compliance contacts are early on the list.

Prepared templates (staff updates, customer notices, holding statements) save time and reduce errors. Updates go out on a regular rhythm, even when there is little new to say, because silence creates rumor.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: who decides
Situation
A flood closes the data center; the IT manager waits four hours for the CEO to approve failover.
Best response
Define declaration criteria and give named people and alternates the authority to declare and fail over.
Why
Clear authority in the plan prevents delays that break the RTO.
Scenario 2: restoring the attacker
Situation
After ransomware, servers are restored from backups made after the attacker gained access, and are encrypted again within a day.
Best response
Identify the intrusion route and persistence first, scan and verify backups, restore from clean points or rebuild, and reset credentials.
Why
Recovery after an attack must not reintroduce the attacker.
Scenario 3: the open recovery site
Situation
At the alternate site, firewall rules were opened wide to get systems working quickly, and never tightened.
Best response
Restore the approved security baseline at the alternate site, and test security controls there in future DR tests.
Why
Security controls must operate in recovery mode too.
Scenario 4: the failed failback
Situation
Moving back to the primary site after a disaster causes a second, longer outage.
Best response
Plan and test failback as a controlled change with data synchronization, testing and a rollback option.
Why
Reconstitution is part of DR and must be planned and tested.

Key takeaways

  • DR process: response, personnel, communications (out of band, prioritized), assessment, restoration in BIA order to a known state, training, lessons learned.
  • Metrics: baseline, MTTR compared with RTO, MTBF, achieved RTO and RPO, time since last test.
  • Declaring: defined criteria, named authority with alternates, escalation from incidents, early decisions, out-of-band notification.
  • Secure recovery: clean before restoring, same controls at the alternate site, controlled emergency access, credential resets, data integrity checks.
  • Reconstitution: repair, test, synchronize, fail back as a controlled change, deactivate; teams with alternates, current offline runbooks, recoverable automation.
