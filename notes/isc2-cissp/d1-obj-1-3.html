Overview

Objective 1.3

1.3 Evaluate, apply, and sustain security governance principles. Sub-topics: alignment with business strategy, goals, mission and objectives; organizational processes (acquisitions, divestitures, governance committees); roles and responsibilities; security control frameworks (ISO, NIST, COBIT, SABSA, PCI DSS, FedRAMP); due care and due diligence.

CISSP questions are often answered from a manager's seat: the right answer usually serves business objectives and follows the governance process, rather than being the cleverest technical fix. The basics of governance and GRC are in CC 1.3 and CC 2.1; policy documents are on the 1.6 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is listed on a US stock exchange, is buying a smaller online competitor, and plans to sell its travel-insurance unit.

What security governance is

Objective 1.3Understanding governance as leadership's direction and oversight of security, distinct from day-to-day management.

NIST defines information security governance as "the process of establishing and maintaining a framework and supporting management structure and processes to provide assurance that information security strategies are aligned with and support business objectives, are consistent with applicable laws and regulations" and support internal controls and accountability (SP 800-100). Two distinctions follow:

  • Governance vs management: governance directs and oversees (sets objectives, risk appetite, policy and accountability, and checks results); management plans and runs the program within that direction. The board governs; the CISO and teams manage.
  • Accountability sits at the top: NIST CSF 2.0 states that "organizational leadership is responsible and accountable for cybersecurity risk and fosters a culture that is risk-aware, ethical, and continually improving" (GV.RR-01).

The outline's three verbs map to a cycle. Evaluate: assess the current state against objectives, obligations and risk. Apply: set direction, structures, policy and resources. Sustain: monitor, report, and improve so governance keeps working as the business and threats change (section 8). CSF 2.0 gathers the governance outcomes in its Govern function: organizational context (GV.OC), risk management strategy (GV.RM), roles, responsibilities and authorities (GV.RR), policy (GV.PO), oversight (GV.OV) and supply chain risk management (GV.SC).

Aligning security with the business

Objective 1.3Making the security program serve the organization's mission, strategy, goals and objectives.

Alignment means security priorities, spending and rules are derived from what the organization is trying to achieve. CSF 2.0 puts it first: "The organizational mission is understood and informs cybersecurity risk management" (GV.OC-01); stakeholders' needs and expectations are understood (GV.OC-02); "Risk management objectives are established and agreed to by organizational stakeholders" (GV.RM-01); "Risk appetite and risk tolerance statements are established, communicated, and maintained" (GV.RM-02); and "Cybersecurity risk management activities and outcomes are included in enterprise risk management processes" (GV.RM-03). It even asks organizations to characterize "strategic opportunities (i.e., positive risks)" (GV.RM-07): security can enable new business, such as entering a regulated market, not only restrain it.

The hierarchy runs from mission (why the organization exists) and strategy (how it will win) to goals (broad aims) and objectives (specific, measurable targets). Security planning mirrors it at three horizons:

  • Strategic plan (three to five years): the security strategy, tied to the business strategy and approved by leadership.
  • Tactical plans (about a year): projects and budgets that deliver the strategy, such as phishing-resistant MFA for all staff.
  • Operational plans (weeks and months): running controls, patching, monitoring and response.

NIST IR 8286 Rev. 1 (December 2025) explains how to integrate cybersecurity with enterprise risk management: practitioners should "understand enterprise strategic objectives when making risk decisions", and system-level risk registers roll up into an enterprise view that leadership can compare with other business risks.

Signs of good alignment: every major security initiative has a business sponsor and a link to an objective; risks are reported in business terms (revenue, customers, regulatory exposure), not CVE counts; and security is involved early in business initiatives. At the retail group, the plan to double online sales makes protecting the e-commerce platform and customer data the top security objective for three years, and each security project is justified against it.

Think like the CISO. When a question offers a technical fix and a governance step (align with business objectives, get management approval, follow the policy), the governance step is usually the one wanted. Senior management owns the risk; the security function advises and implements.

Boards and governance committees

Objective 1.3Using governance bodies to set direction, make risk decisions above any one department, and oversee results.

  • Board of directors: oversees risk, including cybersecurity, sets risk appetite and holds executives accountable, often through an audit committee or a risk committee. It needs regular, understandable reporting: top risks, trends against appetite, major incidents and audit findings.
  • Executive or security steering committee: senior managers from the business, IT, security, legal, privacy and HR who set priorities, approve policy and budgets, and resolve conflicts between departments.
  • Risk executive (function): NIST's term for "an individual or group within an organization that helps to ensure that" system-level risk decisions are viewed organization-wide, in line with strategic goals and risk tolerance (SP 800-39).
  • Specialist boards: change advisory board, architecture review board, AI review board, data governance council; each with a charter, membership and decision rights.

For US public companies, SEC rules adopted in July 2023 require the annual report (Regulation S-K Item 106) to describe "the board of directors' oversight of risks from cybersecurity threats" and "management's role and expertise in assessing and managing material risks", and require material incidents to be disclosed on Form 8-K generally within four business days after the company determines the incident is material. Board oversight of cybersecurity is therefore a disclosed, scrutinized activity, not an informal one.

Good committees have a written charter, the right seniority, regular meetings, minutes recording decisions and risk acceptances, and clear escalation from management to the board. At the retail group, the CISO reports quarterly to the board's audit and risk committee; a monthly security steering committee chaired by the COO approves policy and the security roadmap.

Acquisitions and divestitures

Objective 1.3Managing security when the organization buys, merges with or sells another business.

Mergers, acquisitions and divestitures change what the organization must protect, who has access and which obligations apply, often within weeks. NIST and CISA publish no guidance specifically for them, so the steps below are common practice; NIST's supply chain guide (SP 800-161 Rev. 1) helps with the related work of assessing suppliers.

  1. Due diligence before signingAssess the target's security program, policies, past breaches and incidents (including undisclosed ones), compliance status (such as PCI DSS or GDPR enforcement), technical debt, end-of-support systems and key third parties. Findings affect the price and the contract (warranties and indemnities).
  2. Day-one planDecide which incident response plan applies, who can approve access, and how to reach the target's security staff from the first day.
  3. Controlled integrationTreat the acquired network as untrusted until it meets the buyer's baseline; connect through monitored, restricted gateways; merge or federate identities deliberately; align policies; and retire duplicate systems.
  4. PeopleRetain key staff, communicate clearly, and watch for insider risk during uncertainty.

Divestitures run the other way: decide which data, systems and staff leave and which stay; separate or sanitize shared data and media; remove the departing unit's access and change shared credentials and keys it knew; agree transitional service agreements for services the buyer still needs from you for a time, with security terms; and keep records required for legal retention.

For the online competitor it is buying, the retail group reviewed the target's breach history and PCI assessment before signing, found an unpatched e-commerce platform, negotiated a price reduction, and connected the two networks only through a monitored gateway until the target passed the group's baseline. For the insurance unit it is selling, it is splitting the customer database and will cut the unit's access to group systems when the transitional agreement ends.

Roles and responsibilities

Objective 1.3Knowing who is accountable for security decisions, who carries them out, and who checks them independently.

  • Board and senior management: ultimately accountable; set direction and risk appetite, fund the program, and accept residual risk.
  • CISO: runs the security program, advises leadership and reports on risk. In US federal agencies the equivalent, the senior agency information security officer, is "responsible for carrying out the Chief Information Officer responsibilities under FISMA"; NIST notes other organizations may say CISO. Many organizations have the CISO report outside IT, to the CEO, COO or a risk executive, so security concerns are not filtered by the people they affect; no official source prescribes the reporting line.
  • CIO and CTO: run technology; responsible for operating it securely.
  • Privacy officer or data protection officer: privacy compliance; a DPO is required in some cases under GDPR and must be independent.
  • Data and system owners: business managers who classify their data, decide who gets access and accept risk for their systems.
  • Custodians and administrators: implement the owners' decisions (backups, access settings, encryption).
  • Users: follow policy, protect what they use, report incidents.
  • Auditors: independently assess whether controls work, reporting to the board's audit committee.

The IIA's Three Lines Model (restated in a 2026 position statement) organizes these roles: the first line, operational management, "owns and manages risks"; the second line (risk, compliance and often security functions) provides "specialized expertise, support, monitoring, and challenge"; the third line, internal audit, provides independent and objective assurance to the board. Responsibilities for each process can be recorded in a RACI chart (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed), with exactly one accountable person.

Accountability cannot be delegated. An owner can delegate the work of protecting data to a custodian or a cloud provider, but remains accountable. The same holds for senior management: if a question asks who is ultimately responsible for security, the answer is senior management, not the CISO or IT.

Security control frameworks

Objective 1.3Choosing, combining and mapping control frameworks to suit the organization's industry, obligations and needs.

A control framework gives a tested structure instead of inventing controls from scratch. The outline names several; most organizations use more than one:

FrameworkWhat it isTypical use
ISO/IEC 27001:2022Requirements for an information security management system (ISMS); certifiableProving a managed security program to customers worldwide
ISO/IEC 27002:2022Guidance on the controls, commonly summarized as 93 controls in four themes: organizational, people, physical and technologicalChoosing and implementing ISMS controls
NIST CSF 2.0 and SP 800-53Outcomes in six functions (CSF); a detailed control catalog with baselines (SP 800-53)Any sector (CSF); US federal systems must use SP 800-53
COBIT 2019ISACA's framework for governance and management of enterprise ITLinking IT and security governance to business goals; audit
SABSA"A proven methodology for developing business-driven, risk and opportunity focused Security Architectures"Deriving security architecture from business requirements
PCI DSS v4.0.1Payment card industry requirements for cardholder data; a contractual standard, not a lawAnyone storing, processing or transmitting card data
FedRAMPUS government security assessment and authorization for cloud services, being modernized as "FedRAMP 20x"Cloud providers selling to US federal agencies

Points CISSP questions draw on:

  • ISO/IEC 27001 certifies the management system (scope, risk assessment, statement of applicability, internal audit, management review, improvement), not that every control is perfect. Certificates to the 2013 edition expired on October 31, 2025.
  • NIST CSF 2.0 "does not prescribe how outcomes should be achieved". It describes outcomes in six functions (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover), uses current and target profiles to plan improvement, and tiers (Partial, Risk Informed, Repeatable, Adaptive) to describe how rigorous risk governance is. Tiers are not maturity levels for individual controls.
  • SABSA (owned by The SABSA Institute) is commonly described as six layers (contextual, conceptual, logical, physical, component and operational) asking what, why, how, who, where and when at each, so every control traces back to a business requirement. The Open Group published a guide with The SABSA Institute in 2022 on integrating it with the TOGAF enterprise architecture framework.
  • PCI DSS is enforced through contracts with the card brands and acquiring banks; failing it brings fines and loss of the ability to take cards, not prosecution.
  • FedRAMP 20x aims to move "beyond traditional compliance to focus on the security decisions that matter most", with automated validation against key security indicators.
  • Related frameworks: COSO's internal control framework (behind SOX compliance) and the AI management standard ISO/IEC 42001.

Choosing and combining: start from obligations (laws, contracts, customers), then the organization's size and maturity; pick one framework as the backbone and map the others to it so one control satisfies several requirements. Machine-readable formats such as NIST's OSCAL help automate the mapping. The retail group certifies its card environment to PCI DSS, runs its ISMS to ISO/IEC 27001 because European partners ask for the certificate, and reports to its board using NIST CSF 2.0 current and target profiles.

Due care and due diligence

Objective 1.3Telling the duty to act reasonably from the work of finding out what is needed, and knowing why both matter when things go wrong.

Due care is the ordinary, reasonable care that a reasonable person would take in the circumstances; failing it is the core of negligence. Due diligence is the reasonable investigation and attention needed before and while acting, such as checking a supplier's security or reviewing a company's records before buying it (definitions from Cornell's Legal Information Institute; NIST's glossary has no entry). A common way to remember the difference:

  • Due diligence is knowing: assessing risks, researching requirements, reviewing suppliers and acquisition targets, monitoring that controls work.
  • Due care is doing: putting reasonable controls in place, following policy, fixing what the diligence found.

Courts and regulators judge after the fact whether an organization acted as a prudent organization in its position would have (the prudent person standard). A negligence claim needs a duty, a breach of that duty, a causal link and harm. The US Federal Trade Commission has brought many cases against companies that failed to take "reasonable security measures", and the SEC and EU regulators look for the same evidence: risk assessments, policies, records of decisions, and fixes. Documentation is what proves due care and diligence afterwards.

Examples at the retail group: assessing a new payment processor before signing (diligence) and requiring encryption and breach notice in the contract (care); a quarterly vulnerability review (diligence) and patching critical internet-facing systems within the policy deadline (care); auditing that leavers lose access (diligence) and disabling accounts on the last day (care).

Diligence without care is not enough. A risk assessment that finds unpatched internet-facing servers is due diligence; leaving them unpatched is a failure of due care, and arguably worse than not knowing, because the organization knew. Exam questions often pair the two.

Sustaining governance

Objective 1.3Keeping governance effective over time through measurement, review and improvement.

The 2027 outline adds "sustain" to this objective. Governance decays: strategies go stale, committees stop meeting, policies drift from practice. Mechanisms that keep it alive:

  • Oversight (CSF GV.OV): leadership reviews the results of the risk management strategy and adjusts it.
  • Metrics and reporting: KPIs and KRIs against risk appetite, reported to the board (objective 6.3).
  • Management review and internal audit: the ISMS cycle in ISO/IEC 27001 requires both, feeding corrective action and continual improvement.
  • Profiles: comparing the CSF current profile with the target profile shows progress and gaps year by year.
  • Triggers: revisit governance after major changes, such as acquisitions, new regulations, major incidents or new technology like AI.
  • Culture: leadership visibly following the rules, and staff rewarded for raising risks.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying governance principles to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the unfunded control
Situation
Your risk assessment shows the e-commerce platform needs a web application firewall. The CIO says there is no budget this year.
Best response
Present the risk in business terms to the steering committee or the risk owner, with options and costs, and let them decide to fund, transfer or formally accept the risk; record the decision.
Why
Risk decisions belong to management, informed by security. Buying the tool without approval, or silently dropping it, both bypass governance.
Scenario 2: integration pressure
Situation
The day after closing the acquisition, the business asks you to connect the acquired company's network directly to the group network so staff can share files.
Best response
Provide a controlled, monitored connection for the needed services only, and plan full integration after the acquired network has been assessed against the group baseline.
Why
Until assessed, the acquired network is untrusted; a direct connection imports any compromise or weakness it has. Refusing all connectivity ignores the business objective.
Scenario 3: who answers to the regulator
Situation
After a breach, a regulator asks who in the company was responsible for the decision not to encrypt a customer database. The CISO had recommended encryption; the business owner deferred it.
Best answer
The business owner (and ultimately senior management) was accountable for accepting the risk. The CISO's documented recommendation and the recorded risk acceptance show the governance process worked as designed, even though the decision was poor.
Why
Owners and senior management accept risk; the security function advises. Documented decisions are the evidence of due diligence and of who made them.

Key takeaways

  • Governance (SP 800-100) directs and oversees so security supports business objectives and complies with law; management runs the program. Leadership is accountable (CSF GV.RR-01). Evaluate, apply, sustain.
  • Alignment: mission, strategy, goals, objectives drive strategic, tactical and operational plans; CSF GV.OC and GV.RM; IR 8286 Rev. 1 links security risk to enterprise risk. Choose the governance answer over the technical one.
  • Committees: board (audit or risk committee) oversees; steering committee prioritizes and approves; risk executive function keeps an organization-wide view; SEC Item 106 requires disclosing board oversight; charters and minutes.
  • M&A: due diligence before signing, day-one plan, controlled integration, people risk. Divestitures: separate data, sanitize, remove access, rotate keys, transitional service agreements.
  • Roles: senior management accountable; CISO runs the program; owners classify and accept risk; custodians implement; auditors assure. Three Lines Model; RACI with one accountable. Accountability cannot be delegated.
  • Frameworks: ISO/IEC 27001 (certifiable ISMS) and 27002 (controls), NIST CSF 2.0 (outcomes, profiles, tiers) and SP 800-53, COBIT, SABSA (traceable, layered), PCI DSS (contractual), FedRAMP (20x). Pick a backbone and map the rest.
  • Due diligence is knowing; due care is doing; prudent person standard; documentation proves both. Knowing and not acting is negligence.
