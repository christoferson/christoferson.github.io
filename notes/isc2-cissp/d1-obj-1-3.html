Overview

Objectives 1.3 and 1.6

1.3 Evaluate, apply, and sustain security governance principles. 1.6 Develop, document, and implement security policy, standards, procedures, and guidelines.

CISSP questions are often answered from a manager's seat: the right answer is usually the one that serves business objectives and follows policy, not the cleverest technical fix. The basics of governance, GRC and policy documents are in the CC guide (CC 1.3 and CC 2.1); this page covers the CISSP depth. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is listed on a US stock exchange and is buying a smaller online competitor.

Aligning security with the business

Objective 1.3Making the security program serve the organization's mission, strategy and objectives, rather than running as a separate technical function.

NIST defines information security governance as establishing a framework and management structure "to provide assurance that information security strategies are aligned with and support business objectives" and comply with laws (SP 800-100). NIST CSF 2.0 makes this the first Govern outcome: "The organizational mission is understood and informs cybersecurity risk management" (GV.OC-01), followed by "Risk management objectives are established and agreed to by organizational stakeholders" (GV.RM-01) and "Cybersecurity risk management activities and outcomes are included in enterprise risk management processes" (GV.RM-03). CSF 2.0 even asks organizations to characterize "strategic opportunities (i.e., positive risks)" (GV.RM-07): security can enable the business, not only restrain it.

In practice alignment works through planning at three horizons:

  • Strategic (three to five years): the security strategy, tied to the business strategy and approved by leadership.
  • Tactical (about a year): projects and budgets that deliver the strategy, such as rolling out phishing-resistant MFA.
  • Operational (day to day): running controls, patching, monitoring and responding.

NIST's guide to integrating cybersecurity with enterprise risk management (IR 8286 Rev. 1, December 2025) asks practitioners to "understand enterprise strategic objectives when making risk decisions" and rolls system-level risk registers up to an enterprise view. At the retail group, the business plan to double online sales makes protecting the e-commerce platform and customer data the top security objective for the next three years, and every security project is justified against it.

Think like the CISO. When a question offers a technical fix and a governance step (align with the business, get management approval, follow the policy), the governance answer is usually the one wanted. Senior management owns the risk; the security function advises and implements.

Committees, acquisitions and divestitures

Objective 1.3Using governance bodies to make and oversee security decisions, and managing security when organizations combine or split.

Governance committees make risk decisions above any one department. The board, often through an audit or risk committee, oversees; a security or IT steering committee of senior managers sets priorities and approves policy. NIST describes a risk executive (function), "an individual or group within an organization that helps to ensure that" risk is viewed organization-wide in line with strategic goals and risk tolerance (SP 800-39). For US public companies, SEC rules since 2023 require the annual report to describe "the board of directors' oversight of risks from cybersecurity threats" and management's role and expertise.

Acquisitions and divestitures change what the organization must protect overnight. NIST and CISA publish no specific guidance for them, so the following is common practice. NIST's supply chain guide (SP 800-161 Rev. 1) helps with the related task of assessing suppliers.

  • Before an acquisition: security due diligence on the target, covering its policies, past breaches and incidents, compliance status (for example PCI DSS), technical debt and third parties. Unknown breaches become the buyer's problem.
  • Integration: connect networks only after assessment, and treat the acquired network as untrusted until it meets the buyer's baseline. Align policies, merge or separate identities, and agree which incident response plan applies on day one.
  • Divestiture: decide which data leaves and which stays, sanitize what must not leave (CC 5.1), remove access and shared accounts, and change credentials and keys the departing unit knew.

For the online competitor it is buying, the retail group's security team reviews the target's breach history and PCI assessment before signing, and then connects the two networks only through a monitored gateway until the target passes the group's baseline.

Roles and responsibilities

Objective 1.3Knowing who is accountable for security decisions, who carries them out, and who checks them independently.

  • Board and senior management: ultimately accountable for security; set direction and risk appetite, fund the program, and accept residual risk.
  • CISO: runs the security program. In US federal agencies the equivalent, the senior agency information security officer, carries out the CIO's security responsibilities under FISMA. Many organizations have the CISO report outside IT, to the CEO or a risk executive, so security concerns are not filtered by the people they affect; no official source prescribes the reporting line.
  • Data and system owners: business managers who classify their data, decide who gets access and accept risk for their systems.
  • Custodians: usually IT, who implement the owners' decisions: backups, access settings, encryption.
  • Users: follow policy and report incidents.
  • Auditors: independently assess whether controls work, reporting to the board's audit committee. This is the third line in the IIA Three Lines Model (CC 2.1).

Accountability cannot be delegated. An owner can delegate the work of protecting data to a custodian or a cloud provider, but remains accountable. The same holds for senior management: if a question asks who is ultimately responsible for security, the answer is senior management, not the CISO or IT.

Security control frameworks

Objective 1.3Choosing and combining control frameworks to suit the organization's industry, obligations and needs.

A control framework gives a tested structure instead of inventing controls from scratch. The outline names several; most organizations use more than one and map between them:

SABSA is commonly described as a six-layer model, from the business context down through conceptual, logical, physical and component architecture to operations, asking what, why, how, who, where and when at each layer. Its point is traceability: every control can be traced back to a business requirement. The Open Group published a guide with The SABSA Institute in 2022 on integrating SABSA with the TOGAF enterprise architecture framework.

The retail group certifies its card processing environment to PCI DSS, runs its ISMS to ISO/IEC 27001 because European partners ask for the certificate, and reports to its board using NIST CSF 2.0 profiles.

Due care and due diligence

Objective 1.3Telling the duty to act reasonably from the work of finding out what is needed, and knowing why both matter when things go wrong.

Due care is the ordinary, reasonable care that a reasonable person would take in the circumstances; failing it is the core of negligence. Due diligence is the reasonable investigation and attention needed before and while acting, such as checking a supplier's security or reviewing a company's records before buying it (definitions from Cornell's Legal Information Institute; NIST's glossary has no entry). A common way to remember the difference:

  • Due diligence is knowing: assessing risks, researching requirements, reviewing suppliers and acquisition targets, monitoring that controls work.
  • Due care is doing: putting reasonable controls in place, following policy, fixing what the diligence found.

Courts and regulators judge after the fact whether an organization acted as a prudent organization in its position would have. A negligence claim needs a duty, a breach of it, a causal link and harm. The US Federal Trade Commission has brought many cases against companies that failed to take "reasonable security measures."

Diligence without care is not enough. A risk assessment that finds unpatched internet-facing servers is due diligence; leaving them unpatched is a failure of due care, and arguably worse than not knowing, because the organization knew. Exam questions often pair the two.

Policies, standards, procedures and guidelines

Objective 1.6Developing, approving and maintaining the documents that turn governance decisions into rules, from high-level policy to step-by-step procedures.

The document types and their definitions are in CC 1.3. At CISSP level, the focus is on building and running the set:

  1. PolicyManagement's statement of intent: what and why, technology-neutral, mandatory, approved by senior management and reviewed regularly. NIST describes program policy (the overall security program), issue-specific policy (such as email, remote work or AI use) and system-specific policy (SP 800-12).
  2. StandardsMandatory specific requirements that support a policy, such as a minimum password length or required encryption algorithms.
  3. BaselinesThe minimum configuration for a type of system, often from a hardening guide.
  4. ProceduresMandatory step-by-step instructions for a task, such as how to grant access or respond to a lost laptop.
  5. GuidelinesRecommended practice where flexibility is acceptable; not mandatory.

Good practice for developing them: involve the business owners and legal, keep policy short and stable so detail lives in standards and procedures that can change faster, give every document an owner and a review date, record exceptions with a risk owner and an expiry, and make staff acknowledge key policies. CSF 2.0 expects policy to be "established, communicated, and enforced" (GV.PO).

Key takeaways

  • Alignment: governance makes security support business objectives (SP 800-100; CSF GV.OC-01, GV.RM-01/03). Plan at strategic, tactical and operational horizons. Choose the governance answer over the technical one.
  • Committees: the board oversees (SEC rules require describing board oversight), a steering committee prioritizes, NIST's risk executive function keeps an organization-wide view. Acquisitions: due diligence first, connect only after assessment. Divestitures: separate data, sanitize, remove access, rotate keys.
  • Roles: senior management is ultimately accountable; the CISO runs the program; owners classify and accept risk; custodians implement; auditors assure independently. Accountability cannot be delegated.
  • Frameworks: ISO/IEC 27001 (certifiable ISMS) and 27002 (controls), NIST CSF and SP 800-53, COBIT (IT governance), SABSA (business-driven architecture), PCI DSS (card data, contractual), FedRAMP (US government cloud).
  • Due diligence is knowing (investigating); due care is doing (acting reasonably). Knowing and not acting is negligence.
  • Documents: policy (what and why, mandatory) → standards (specific, mandatory) → baselines → procedures (how, mandatory) → guidelines (recommended). Owners, review dates, exceptions with expiry, acknowledgment.
