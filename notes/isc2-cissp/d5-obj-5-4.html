Overview

Objective 5.4

5.4 Implement and manage authorization mechanisms. Sub-topics: role-based access control (RBAC); rule-based access control (RuBAC); mandatory access control (MAC); discretionary access control (DAC); attribute-based access control (ABAC); risk-adaptable access control (RAdAC); access policy enforcement (policy decision point, policy enforcement point).

Access control models are introduced in CC 3.2; the security models behind MAC are on the 3.2 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It has 4,000 workforce identities, 9,000 service accounts and API keys, 12 million customer accounts, and a growing number of AI agents.

Authorization models

Objective 5.4Choosing the access control model that fits the data, the organization and the risk.

  • Discretionary access control (DAC): "leaves a certain amount of access control to the discretion of the object's owner" (NIST SP 800-192), usually through ACLs and owner, group and other permissions. Flexible but "inherently weak", because users can pass on copies.
  • Mandatory access control (MAC): "access control policy decisions are made by a central authority, not by the individual owner of an object", by comparing the object's sensitivity label with the subject's clearance (and need-to-know categories). Users cannot change it. Used for classified systems; Bell-LaPadula is its model (objective 3.2).
  • Role-based access control (RBAC): permissions attach to roles and users get roles. NIST levels: core, hierarchical (roles inherit), and static and dynamic separation of duties. The standard enterprise model, but ad hoc roles lead to "role explosion".
  • Rule-based access control (RuBAC): access decided by administrator-set rules that apply to everyone, such as firewall rules by address and port or "no logons outside business hours". NIST notes there is "no commonly understood definition or formally defined standard" for it.
  • Attribute-based access control (ABAC, SP 800-162): decisions from subject attributes, object attributes, environment conditions ("current time, day of the week, location of a user, or the current threat level") and policy. Very fine-grained and avoids role explosion, but attributes must be accurate and policies are harder to audit.
  • Risk-adaptable access control (RAdAC): "a form of access control that uses an authorization policy that takes into account operational need, risk, and heuristics" (CNSSI 4009). Access is decided at the moment from identity, need and current risk, measured by factors such as "the strength of the authentication method" and location. Modern conditional access and zero trust policy engines work this way.

Spotting the model in a question. Owner decides: DAC. Labels and clearances: MAC. Job function: RBAC. Same rule for everyone (time, address): RuBAC. Combination of attributes and context: ABAC. Real-time risk and mission need, possibly overriding the usual policy: RAdAC.

Access policy enforcement

Objective 5.4Separating where access decisions are made from where they are enforced.

NIST's ABAC and zero trust architectures split authorization into components:

  1. Policy enforcement point (PEP)Intercepts the request at the resource (a gateway, proxy, application or API) and "enforces policy decisions".
  2. Policy decision point (PDP)"Computes access decisions" by evaluating the policy against the request; in SP 800-207 it is the policy engine plus the policy administrator.
  3. Policy information point (PIP)Supplies attributes and context: directory data, device posture, threat intelligence, location.
  4. Policy administration point (PAP)Where policies are written and managed.

Centralizing decisions in a PDP makes policy consistent, auditable and easy to change; distributing PEPs puts enforcement close to every resource. SP 800-207 grants access "on a per-session basis" with the least privilege needed. Policies themselves should be managed like code, with review, testing and version control.

At the retail group, the cloud IdP's conditional access engine acts as the PDP for SaaS (it allows store managers into the HR system only from managed devices and blocks sign-ins from unexpected countries), while an API gateway acts as the PEP for internal APIs, asking a central policy service for each decision.

Choosing and combining models

Objective 5.4Selecting an authorization approach that fits the data, the organization and the regulation.

Real systems combine models. A typical enterprise uses RBAC for most application access, ABAC conditions on top (location, device, data classification), rule-based controls at network boundaries, DAC in collaboration tools where owners share their own files, and MAC-like labels for the most sensitive data. Selection depends on:

  • Sensitivity and regulation: classified or highly regulated data favors centrally enforced, label-based control.
  • Scale and change: RBAC is easy to review but suffers role explosion; ABAC handles many combinations with fewer rules but needs accurate attributes.
  • Context: zero trust and RAdAC-style decisions use real-time signals, which requires good telemetry and a policy engine.
  • Auditability: the organization must be able to answer "who can access this, and why?" quickly.

Whatever the model, apply least privilege, separation of duties (mutually exclusive roles, dual approval) and deny by default.

Managing authorization over time

Objective 5.4Keeping entitlements and policies correct as people, data and systems change.

  • Entitlement catalog: every role, group and permission described in business terms with an owner.
  • Policy as code: authorization policies stored in version control, reviewed and tested before deployment, so changes are traceable.
  • Separation of duties rules: toxic combinations (create supplier plus approve payment) defined and blocked or flagged at request time.
  • Reviews and analytics: periodic certifications plus analysis of unused permissions to shrink access toward least privilege.
  • Cloud permissions: cloud IAM policies are a frequent source of excessive access; tools that analyze effective permissions find over-privileged roles (CNAPP and entitlement management).
  • Logging decisions: record allow and deny decisions at the PDP for investigations and tuning.

Authorization in applications and APIs

Objective 5.4Enforcing authorization correctly inside applications, where most access decisions are actually made.

  • Check on the server, every time: every request is authorized server-side, including checks that the caller may access this specific object (broken object-level authorization is the top API risk in OWASP's list).
  • Tokens and scopes: OAuth access tokens carry scopes and claims; APIs validate the token and check that the scope and claims allow the action, rather than trusting the client.
  • Centralize the logic: a shared authorization service or library (acting as the PDP) avoids each team writing its own checks inconsistently; applications act as PEPs.
  • Relationship-based rules: many applications decide access by relationships ("the customer who placed this order", "members of this project"); model these explicitly and test them.
  • Least privilege for applications: each service has only the database and API permissions it needs, so a compromised service cannot reach everything.
  • Test authorization: misuse cases and automated tests that try to access other users' data and admin functions (objective 6.2).

Most real breaches of access control happen here, not in the identity provider: a correct sign-in followed by a missing or wrong authorization check.

Authorization for data and AI systems

Objective 5.4Applying authorization below the application level, down to rows, fields and retrieved documents.

  • Row- and column-level security: databases and analytics platforms restrict which rows (for example, only a store manager's own store) and columns (no card numbers) each role can query.
  • Dynamic masking: sensitive fields shown masked to most users and in full only to authorized roles.
  • Data platform policies: tag-based policies on data lakes apply the same rule to every table carrying a sensitivity tag, an attribute-based approach that scales.
  • Retrieval for AI: AI assistants that search documents (retrieval-augmented generation) must apply the user's own permissions at retrieval time, so the model never sees what the user could not open.
  • AI agents: agents acting on behalf of users get delegated, scoped and time-limited permissions, with human approval for high-impact actions, not a broad service account.

At the retail group, the analytics platform uses one tag-based policy for all tables tagged "customer personal data": analysts see pseudonymized identifiers, and only the privacy-approved marketing role sees contact details.

Worked example: authorizing refunds

Objective 5.4Combining models to express a real business rule.

Rule: store staff may issue refunds up to 100, supervisors up to 1,000 for their own store during their shift, and larger refunds need a regional manager's approval. Nobody may approve a refund to their own card.

  • RBAC gives the base permission: the cashier, supervisor and regional manager roles.
  • ABAC adds conditions: the refund amount, the user's store matching the transaction's store, and the time matching the user's shift.
  • Separation of duties: the requester and the approver must be different people, and the card used must not belong to the employee.
  • Policy decision point: the payment service asks a central policy engine, which returns permit or deny with a reason; the point-of-sale app enforces it.
  • Audit: every decision is logged with the attributes used, so investigators can see why a refund was allowed.

Expressing the rule as policy rather than code scattered through applications means it can be reviewed by the business owner, tested, and changed in one place when limits change.

Common authorization mistakes

Objective 5.4Recognizing the errors that most often break access control.

  • Checking authorization in the user interface only, so direct API calls bypass it.
  • Trusting identifiers or roles sent by the client instead of looking them up on the server.
  • Defaulting to allow when a policy service is unreachable.
  • Granting broad roles "temporarily" and never removing them.
  • Different teams implementing the same rule differently in different services.

Access control matrix, ACLs and capabilities

Objective 5.4The classic way of representing who may do what to which object.

An access control matrix is a table with subjects as rows, objects as columns, and permitted operations in each cell. It is rarely stored as a whole; systems store it in one of two ways:

  • Access control list (ACL): a column of the matrix, stored with the object, listing which subjects may access it and how. File system permissions and firewall rules are ACLs. Easy to see who can access an object; hard to see everything one subject can access.
  • Capability table (or capability list): a row, stored with the subject, listing the objects it may access. Tokens and tickets work like capabilities. Easy to see what a subject can do; revoking access to one object is harder.

Most ACLs work on implicit deny: anything not explicitly allowed is refused. Rules are often evaluated in order, so a broad allow above a specific deny can open more than intended.

Nondiscretionary and other access control types

Objective 5.4Terms the exam uses alongside the main models.

  • Nondiscretionary: access set centrally by administrators rather than by owners; RBAC and rule-based access control are both nondiscretionary, and MAC is the strictest form.
  • MAC environments: hierarchical (levels such as Confidential, Secret, Top Secret), compartmentalized (categories or compartments with no ordering), and hybrid (levels with compartments inside them). Access needs both sufficient clearance and the compartment, which expresses need-to-know; these form a lattice of labels.
  • Content-dependent: access depends on the data itself, such as a database view that shows only rows for the user's region.
  • Context-dependent: access depends on circumstances such as time, location, device or the sequence of earlier actions.
  • Constrained interfaces: restricted menus, greyed-out functions and limited shells that only offer permitted actions.
  • XACML (eXtensible Access Control Markup Language): an OASIS standard policy language and request-response format for ABAC, built around the policy enforcement point and policy decision point described above.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: role explosion
Situation
The HR system has 900 roles for 4,000 staff, many differing by one permission.
Best response
Consolidate into a smaller set of business roles and express the variations as attributes or ABAC conditions (location, department).
Why
Role explosion makes RBAC unmanageable; ABAC handles combinations with fewer rules.
Scenario 2: the toxic combination
Situation
A finance user can both create new suppliers and approve payments to them.
Best response
Define the two roles as mutually exclusive (static separation of duties), remove one, and add a check at request time.
Why
Separation of duties prevents one person completing a fraud alone.
Scenario 3: risky sign-in
Situation
A store manager signs in to the HR system from an unmanaged device in another country at 3 a.m.
Best response
The policy engine should step up authentication or block access based on device posture and location, and alert.
Why
Risk-adaptive and attribute-based decisions use context, not just identity.
Scenario 4: who can see this?
Situation
An auditor asks which identities can read the customer loyalty database, including through groups, roles and cloud policies.
Best response
Use an entitlement and effective-permission analysis across directory groups, database roles and cloud IAM, and fix any unexplained paths.
Why
Authorization must be auditable; effective access often differs from intended access.
Scenario 5: the AI assistant shows salaries
Situation
An employee asks the new AI assistant about pay ranges and receives details from HR documents they cannot normally open.
Best response
Make retrieval apply the user's own permissions, fix oversharing on the HR documents, and review what else the assistant could reach.
Why
AI retrieval must respect the same authorization as direct access.

Key takeaways

  • Models: DAC (owner), MAC (labels, central), RBAC (roles), RuBAC (global rules), ABAC (attributes and environment), RAdAC (real-time risk and need).
  • Enforcement: PEP enforces, PDP decides, PIP informs, PAP manages policy; per-session, least-privilege decisions (SP 800-207).
  • Choosing: combine models by sensitivity, scale, context and auditability; least privilege, separation of duties, deny by default.
  • Managing: entitlement catalog, policy as code, toxic-combination rules, reviews and unused-permission analysis, cloud effective permissions, decision logs.
