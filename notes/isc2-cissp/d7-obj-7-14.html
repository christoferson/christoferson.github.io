7.14–7.15: Physical security and personnel safety

Running the physical controls that keep intruders out of sites and sensitive rooms, and protecting people: when they travel, through awareness of how they are targeted, in emergencies, and when they are forced to act under threat.

Overview

Objectives 7.14 and 7.15

7.14 Implement and manage physical security. 7.15 Address personnel safety and security concerns.

Site and facility design (CPTED, server rooms, fire, power) is on the 3.8–3.10 page, and physical control types in CC 1.4. This page covers operating physical security day to day and keeping people safe. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its buyers travel often to suppliers abroad, and its stores handle cash.

External physical security controls

Objective 7.14Deterring, delaying and detecting intruders before they reach the building.

Perimeter controls work in layers: deter, delay, detect, then respond.

  • Barriers: fences (height and anti-climb features matched to the threat), gates, and vehicle barriers; SP 800-53 PE-3(7) names "bollards, concrete slabs, jersey walls, and hydraulic active vehicle barriers".
  • Lighting: even, continuous lighting of entrances, parking and fence lines, which supports cameras and deters intruders (with emergency lighting for evacuation, PE-12).
  • Surveillance: cameras covering approaches and entrances, monitored live or recorded and retained, with clear signs where law requires them.
  • Guards and patrols: flexible and able to judge situations, but costly and fallible; often combined with dogs, patrols and alarm response contracts.
  • Intrusion detection: perimeter and door sensors, motion detectors and glass-break sensors, connected to monitored alarms.
  • Controlled entrances: a small number of staffed or badge-controlled entry points, with deliveries through a separate, controlled loading area.

Operating them means more than buying them: test alarms and cameras regularly, review footage after incidents, keep lighting working, respond to every alarm, and track false alarm rates so guards do not start ignoring them.

Internal physical security controls

Objective 7.14Controlling who goes where inside the building, and knowing when someone is where they should not be.

  • Physical access control (PE-3): badge readers, PINs and biometrics by zone, with authorizations checked, logs kept, and keys and combinations changed when lost or when holders leave. Access to sensitive zones is reviewed like logical access, and removed together with it on departure.
  • Access control vestibules (PE-3(8)): two interlocking doors that prevent "piggybacking or tailgating"; NIST replaced the older term "mantrap" with "vestibule" in 2020. Turnstiles and security doors serve the same purpose.
  • Visitor management: registration, identification, visible temporary badges, escorts in non-public areas, and badge return on exit.
  • Locks: mechanical (key, combination) and electronic; high-security locks for sensitive rooms; locked racks and cabinets inside data centers.
  • Monitoring (PE-6): "guards, video surveillance equipment (i.e., cameras), and sensor devices" inside the building, plus alerts on doors held or forced open.
  • Clean desk and secure areas: sensitive papers and devices locked away, screens locked, and no personal devices in the most sensitive areas.

Tailgating beats technology. The best badge system fails if staff hold doors open for people behind them. Vestibules or turnstiles, door-held alarms, and training staff to challenge or report unbadged people close the gap.

Travel

Objective 7.15Protecting people, devices and data when staff travel, especially abroad.

  • Before travel: check risk ratings and advice for the destination, register trips so the organization can find people in an emergency, brief travelers on local threats and laws (including laws on encryption and device inspection at borders).
  • Devices: for high-risk destinations, issue clean loaner laptops and phones with only the data needed, then wipe or rebuild them on return. Keep devices with you; hotel rooms and safes are not secure.
  • Connections: use the corporate VPN or a personal hotspot rather than hotel or public Wi-Fi; do not use public charging ports or accept gifted USB devices.
  • Behavior: be careful what is discussed in public places and on social media during the trip; watch for approaches that may be intelligence gathering.
  • Support: a 24-hour contact for medical, security or legal emergencies, and travel insurance with evacuation cover.

Security training and awareness

Objective 7.15Teaching people how they are targeted and what to do, so they protect themselves and the organization.

Awareness programs (designed on the 1.11–1.12 page) should cover the threats people face personally:

  • Insider threat: recognizing and reporting concerning behavior (unusual access requests, attempts to bypass controls, signs of coercion), through a confidential channel, in a culture that treats reporting as protective rather than punitive.
  • Social media impacts: oversharing job roles, travel plans, office photos and badge pictures gives attackers material for phishing, impersonation and physical intrusion; executives and their families can be targeted.
  • MFA fatigue (2FA fatigue, push bombing): attackers with a stolen password send repeated push prompts until the user approves one. Teach users never to approve a prompt they did not start, and to report it; technical fixes are number matching and phishing-resistant MFA.
  • Physical social engineering: tailgating, fake contractors and delivery staff, and lost USB drives left as bait.
  • Deepfake voice and video: verify unusual requests for payments, credentials or access through a known channel.

Emergency management and duress

Objective 7.15Keeping people safe in emergencies and giving them safe ways to signal and comply when threatened.

Emergency management covers fires, severe weather, medical emergencies, active threats and site evacuations. An emergency action plan sets: how emergencies are reported, alarms and warnings, evacuation routes and assembly points, how everyone is accounted for afterwards, shelter-in-place procedures, and roles such as floor wardens. Drills test it; emergency lighting, alarms and exit signs must work; and doors on escape routes must let people out even when power or access systems fail.

Duress is being forced, under threat, to act against the organization, for example a store manager made to open a safe or an administrator forced to log in:

  • Duress codes and alarms: a special PIN or code word that appears to work normally but silently raises an alarm; panic buttons for staff at counters and in cash rooms.
  • Policy: staff are told to comply and stay safe; no asset is worth an injury. Security then responds to the silent alarm.
  • Design: time-delay safes, two-person rules and limited cash on hand reduce what any one coerced person can give up, which also reduces the incentive to coerce them.

Life safety always wins. Whenever a question pits people's safety against protecting assets, data or evidence, safety comes first: evacuate, comply under duress, and let doors fail open on escape routes.

The retail group's store safes have time delays and a duress PIN linked to its monitoring center, and each store runs an evacuation drill twice a year with every staff member accounted for.

Key takeaways

  • External controls: deter, delay, detect, respond; barriers and vehicle barriers (PE-3(7)), lighting, cameras, guards, intrusion sensors, few controlled entrances; test and respond to alarms.
  • Internal controls: zoned badge access reviewed with logical access (PE-3), vestibules against tailgating (PE-3(8)), visitor management and escorts, locks and racks, monitoring (PE-6), clean desk.
  • Travel: destination briefings, trip registration, loaner devices wiped on return, VPN or hotspot, careful behavior, 24-hour support.
  • Awareness: insider threat reporting, social media oversharing, MFA fatigue (never approve unexpected prompts; number matching, phishing-resistant MFA), physical social engineering, deepfakes.
  • Emergencies: emergency action plans, evacuation and accounting for people, drills, fail-safe escape doors. Duress: duress codes and panic alarms, comply and stay safe, time delays and two-person rules.
