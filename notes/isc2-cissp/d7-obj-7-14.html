Overview

Objective 7.14

7.14 Implement and manage physical security. Sub-topics: external security controls; internal security controls.

Site and facility design (CPTED, server rooms, fire, power) is on the 3.8 and 3.9 pages, and physical control types in CC 1.4. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its 300 stores handle cash and card terminals, and its head office and data center use badge access and a monitoring center.

External physical security controls

Objective 7.14Deterring, delaying and detecting intruders before they reach the building.

Perimeter controls work in layers: deter, delay, detect, then respond.

  • Barriers: fences (height and anti-climb features matched to the threat), gates, and vehicle barriers; SP 800-53 PE-3(7) names "bollards, concrete slabs, jersey walls, and hydraulic active vehicle barriers".
  • Lighting: even, continuous lighting of entrances, parking and fence lines, which supports cameras and deters intruders (with emergency lighting for evacuation, PE-12).
  • Surveillance: cameras covering approaches and entrances, monitored live or recorded and retained, with clear signs where law requires them.
  • Guards and patrols: flexible and able to judge situations, but costly and fallible; often combined with dogs, patrols and alarm response contracts.
  • Intrusion detection: perimeter and door sensors, motion detectors and glass-break sensors, connected to monitored alarms.
  • Controlled entrances: a small number of staffed or badge-controlled entry points, with deliveries through a separate, controlled loading area.

Operating them means more than buying them: test alarms and cameras regularly, review footage after incidents, keep lighting working, respond to every alarm, and track false alarm rates so guards do not start ignoring them.

Internal physical security controls

Objective 7.14Controlling who goes where inside the building, and knowing when someone is where they should not be.

  • Physical access control (PE-3): badge readers, PINs and biometrics by zone, with authorizations checked, logs kept, and keys and combinations changed when lost or when holders leave. Access to sensitive zones is reviewed like logical access, and removed together with it on departure.
  • Access control vestibules (PE-3(8)): two interlocking doors that prevent "piggybacking or tailgating"; NIST replaced the older term "mantrap" with "vestibule" in 2020. Turnstiles and security doors serve the same purpose.
  • Visitor management: registration, identification, visible temporary badges, escorts in non-public areas, and badge return on exit.
  • Locks: mechanical (key, combination) and electronic; high-security locks for sensitive rooms; locked racks and cabinets inside data centers.
  • Monitoring (PE-6): "guards, video surveillance equipment (i.e., cameras), and sensor devices" inside the building, plus alerts on doors held or forced open.
  • Clean desk and secure areas: sensitive papers and devices locked away, screens locked, and no personal devices in the most sensitive areas.

Tailgating beats technology. The best badge system fails if staff hold doors open for people behind them. Vestibules or turnstiles, door-held alarms, and training staff to challenge or report unbadged people close the gap.

Operating physical security

Objective 7.14Running physical controls day to day so they keep working.

  • Monitoring center: alarms, door events and camera feeds from all sites go to a staffed center (in-house or contracted) with written response procedures for each alarm type.
  • Post orders: written instructions for guards: what to check, how to handle visitors and deliveries, when to call police, and how to record events.
  • Response: every alarm is acknowledged, investigated and closed with a note; repeated false alarms are fixed at the cause rather than ignored.
  • Maintenance and testing: cameras, readers, locks, sensors, lighting and backup power for security systems are inspected and tested on a schedule.
  • Access reviews: badge access to sensitive areas is reviewed periodically, and lost badges are disabled immediately.
  • Records: access logs, visitor logs and camera footage are retained for a defined period to support investigations, within privacy law.
  • Measures: alarm response times, false alarm rates, tailgating incidents, lost badges and inspection results.

Physical security staff and the SOC share information: a badge used at a store while its owner is signed in remotely from another country is a security event for both.

Data centers and sensitive rooms

Objective 7.14Layering controls around the rooms that hold the most valuable assets.

  • Zones: public areas, general offices, restricted areas and high-security rooms, each with stronger controls than the one outside it.
  • Entry: multi-factor access (badge plus PIN or biometric) to data halls, a vestibule or turnstile against tailgating, and a list of authorized people reviewed regularly.
  • Inside: locked racks and cages, cameras covering aisles and doors, and no unescorted visitors or contractors.
  • Two-person rules for the most sensitive areas, such as key management rooms or vaults.
  • Equipment movement: logged and authorized delivery and removal of equipment and media, with checks at exits.
  • Environmental monitoring: temperature, humidity, water and smoke sensors alerting the monitoring center (facility design is on the 3.9 page).

For colocation and cloud data centers, the organization relies on the provider's controls and their attestation reports, while still controlling who on its own staff is authorized to enter colocation space.

Stores, branches and remote sites

Objective 7.14Applying physical security to many small sites with few staff.

  • Cash and valuables: time-delay safes, limited cash on hand, cash offices with restricted access, and armored transport collections.
  • Payment terminals: inventory of devices, periodic inspection for tampering and skimmers (PCI DSS requires this for card-reading devices), and staff trained to spot and report tampering.
  • Network equipment: store servers and network devices in locked cabinets, not under counters or in storerooms open to all staff.
  • Opening and closing: two-person procedures where risk is high, alarm setting checked by the monitoring center, and procedures for staff arriving to find signs of forced entry.
  • Deliveries: separate receiving areas, delivery staff checked against schedules, and doors not propped open.
  • Unstaffed sites: remote cabinets and warehouses rely on intrusion detection, cameras and alarm response contracts.

At the retail group, monthly terminal inspections found skimming overlays on two self-checkout terminals in one region; inspections were made weekly in that region and tamper-evident seals were added.

Physical and logical security together

Objective 7.14Treating physical security systems as IT systems that also need protecting.

  • Connected systems: access control systems, cameras and alarm panels run on networks and software; they need patching, hardening, changed default passwords and their own network segment.
  • Shared identity: linking badge access to the HR and identity lifecycle means that one leaver process removes both physical and logical access.
  • Correlation: physical access logs and logical sign-in logs together can reveal impossible activity, such as a badge entry at headquarters while the same user signs in from abroad.
  • Cloud-managed systems: many camera and access control systems are now managed from the vendor's cloud, so assess the vendor's security and protect the administrator accounts like any other privileged account.
  • Privacy: camera footage and badge logs are personal data, subject to retention limits, access control and notice requirements.

Keys, locks and physical security testing

Objective 7.14Managing the oldest physical controls and checking that physical security works.

  • Key control: a register of every key and who holds it, restricted keyways that cannot be copied freely, and rekeying when master keys are lost or holders leave without returning them.
  • Combinations and codes: changed on a schedule and when people with knowledge leave.
  • Physical penetration tests: authorized testers try tailgating, badge cloning, social engineering of reception, and lock bypass, under rules of engagement and with an authorization letter (objective 6.1).
  • Walkthrough audits: checking that doors close and lock, cameras work, visitor badges are returned and clean desk rules are followed.

Visitor and contractor management in depth

Objective 7.14Handling the people who are on site but not on staff.

  • Pre-registration by a host, with identity checked on arrival against an official ID.
  • Badges that look clearly different from staff badges and show expiry.
  • Escort rules by zone, and a host responsible for the visitor at all times.
  • Contractors with longer-term access get sponsored badges with end dates, reviewed with other access.
  • Delivery and maintenance staff are checked against work orders before entering technical areas.

Cameras: placement, retention and privacy

Objective 7.14Getting useful video without collecting more than necessary.

  • Purpose first: decide what each camera is for (identifying people at entrances, watching cash handling, deterring theft in car parks), which sets its placement and resolution.
  • Coverage of key points: entrances and exits, cash offices, data center doors and aisles, loading bays.
  • Retention: keep footage long enough to investigate incidents (often weeks), then delete automatically unless needed as evidence.
  • Privacy: signs where required, no cameras in private areas, restricted access to footage, and handling of access requests from individuals under privacy law.
  • Security of the system: cameras and recorders patched, segmented and protected, since footage is sensitive data (physical and logical security).

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the friendly tailgater
Situation
A person in a delivery uniform follows staff through a badge-controlled door carrying boxes.
Best response
Install a vestibule or turnstile for sensitive areas, alarm on doors held open, and train staff to direct visitors to reception.
Why
Tailgating defeats badge systems; physical design and staff awareness close the gap.
Scenario 2: the ignored alarm
Situation
Guards at a warehouse stop responding to a door alarm that goes off falsely several times a week.
Best response
Fix the cause of the false alarms, and require every alarm to be investigated and recorded.
Why
Alarm fatigue makes detection useless; response must be reliable.
Scenario 3: the camera on the corporate network
Situation
Store cameras use default passwords and sit on the same network as the point-of-sale systems.
Best response
Change default credentials, patch the cameras, and move them to a separate segment with restricted access.
Why
Physical security devices are IT systems and an attack path.
Scenario 4: the leaver's badge
Situation
An employee who left last month still has a working badge to the head office.
Best response
Link badge deprovisioning to the HR leaver process alongside logical access, and review badge access regularly.
Why
Physical access follows the same lifecycle and review as logical access.
Scenario 5: the lost master key
Situation
A master key for a distribution center has been lost.
Best response
Rekey the affected locks, review the key register, and consider electronic access control for high-value areas.
Why
Lost keys compromise every lock they open; key control requires rekeying.

Key takeaways

  • External controls: deter, delay, detect, respond; barriers and vehicle barriers (PE-3(7)), lighting, cameras, guards, intrusion sensors, few controlled entrances; test and respond to alarms.
  • Internal controls: zoned badge access reviewed with logical access (PE-3), vestibules against tailgating (PE-3(8)), visitor management and escorts, locks and racks, monitoring (PE-6), clean desk.
  • Operations: monitoring center, post orders, every alarm answered, maintenance and testing, access reviews, retained records, measures.
  • Sensitive rooms and small sites: zones, multi-factor entry, racks and two-person rules; cash controls, terminal tamper inspections, locked network cabinets, opening and closing procedures.
  • Convergence: physical security systems are IT systems; shared identity lifecycle, correlated logs, cloud-managed systems, privacy of footage and logs.
