Overview

Objective 7.12

7.12 Test disaster recovery plans (DRP). Sub-topics: read-through/tabletop; walkthrough; simulation; parallel; full interruption; communications (internal and external).

The DR process is on the 7.11 page; organization-wide business continuity exercises on the 7.13 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It tests payment failover between regions every quarter and runs a full test of data center recovery once a year.

Testing DR plans

Objective 7.12Proving plans work through tests of increasing realism, and taking part in organization-wide continuity exercises.

SP 800-53 CP-4 lists "checklists, walk-through and tabletop exercises, simulations (parallel or full interrupt), and comprehensive exercises". In rising order of realism and disruption:

  1. Read-through (checklist)Each team reviews the plan for accuracy and completeness on its own.
  2. TabletopTeams meet and talk through a scenario and their decisions, without touching systems.
  3. WalkthroughA step-by-step rehearsal of the procedures with the people who would carry them out, sometimes at the recovery site.
  4. SimulationA realistic scenario is played out with actions taken, short of failing over production.
  5. Parallel testSystems are brought up at the alternate site and process real or copied workload, while production keeps running at the primary site.
  6. Full interruptionProduction is actually shut down at the primary site and run from the alternate site. The most realistic and the riskiest; needs senior approval and careful timing.

Communications are tested too: internal call trees, out-of-band channels, and external statements, holding messages and contact with regulators and key customers. Testing should match the system's criticality (NIST suggests tabletop for low-impact systems, functional tests including restores for moderate, full-scale tests including failover for high).

Business continuity exercises (objective 7.13) go beyond IT: business units practice working without systems, relocating staff, and making decisions under pressure. Security professionals take part to contribute security and IT recovery input, to make sure security controls survive in recovery mode (alternate sites and emergency access must still be secure), and to bring the lessons back into security plans. FEMA's HSEEP is a common exercise methodology.

Testing must not cause the disaster. A full interruption test that fails can become a real outage. Work up through less disruptive tests first, have a rollback plan, and get business approval. Questions asking for the "least disruptive" test that still exercises systems usually want parallel testing.

Planning a DR test

Objective 7.12Designing tests that prove something without causing an outage.

  • Objectives: what the test must prove, such as "the payment platform runs from the second region within 2 hours with no more than 5 minutes of data loss".
  • Scope: systems, sites, teams and suppliers involved, and what is out of scope.
  • Success criteria: measurable targets (achieved RTO and RPO, transactions processed, security controls working) agreed before the test.
  • Approvals and timing: business owners approve tests that touch production; avoid peak periods.
  • Safety: a rollback plan, stop conditions, and a way to tell test activity from a real incident (notify the SOC and suppliers).
  • Roles: participants who do the recovery, a controller who runs the test, and observers who record times and issues.
  • Realism: use the written runbooks, the people who would really do it (including alternates), and occasionally unannounced elements such as an unavailable key person.

Evaluating results and fixing gaps

Objective 7.12Turning a test into improvements rather than a certificate.

  • Measure against criteria: actual recovery times and data loss compared with RTO and RPO, and which steps took longest.
  • Record issues: missing steps in runbooks, wrong contact details, expired credentials, missing licenses, dependencies nobody knew about, and security controls that did not work.
  • After-action report: what was tested, results, issues and recommendations, shared with management and owners.
  • Improvement actions: each issue gets an owner and a date, and is tracked to closure.
  • Retest: failed elements are tested again after fixes; a test that failed is not a pass because the report was filed.
  • Update the plan: runbooks, contact lists and dependencies are corrected, and the plan's version and review date updated.

A test that finds problems is a successful test: the problems were found in a controlled setting instead of during a real disaster. At the retail group, the yearly data center test found that two warehouse applications depended on a file server not in the recovery plan; it was added and the next test met its RTO.

Testing in the cloud and resilience engineering

Objective 7.12Using automation to test recovery more often and more realistically.

  • Automated restore tests: scheduled jobs restore backups into isolated environments and check that the data and application work.
  • Failover drills: regularly fail over between zones or regions, so failover is routine rather than an annual event.
  • Game days: planned exercises in which teams simulate failures (a region outage, a deleted database) and practice response.
  • Fault injection and chaos engineering: deliberately introducing failures (stopping instances, adding latency, cutting network paths) in controlled experiments, starting small and in test environments, to find weaknesses before real failures do.
  • Blast radius control: experiments have stop conditions and limited scope, and are approved like changes.

Frequent automated tests do not replace plan-level tests with people, but they keep the technical recovery steps from decaying between them.

Testing communications

Objective 7.12Checking that internal and external communication plans work as well as the technology.

  • Internal: call tree and mass notification tests with timed acknowledgments; out-of-band channels tried by the people who would use them; contact lists checked for accuracy (people leave and numbers change).
  • Escalation: confirm that leadership, the crisis team and on-call staff can be reached at night and on weekends.
  • External: practice drafting and approving customer notices and holding statements; check that contacts for regulators, insurers, key suppliers and the alternate site provider are current; test the public status page hosted outside the main environment.
  • Test status reporting: during a test, participants report progress and issues to the controller on a schedule, as they would report to leadership in a real event.

Communication failures are among the most common findings in DR tests: outdated phone numbers, a call tree that stops at someone on holiday, or a status page that was hosted on the failed system.

Building a test program

Objective 7.12Deciding how often to test what, so every plan is proven on a cycle.

  • Frequency: at least yearly for each plan, more often for critical systems, and after major changes to systems, sites or suppliers. Many regulations and standards expect regular testing; for example, PCI DSS requires the incident response plan to be reviewed and tested at least once every 12 months.
  • Rotation: vary scenarios (site loss, ransomware, supplier failure, key staff unavailable) and test different parts of large plans over a cycle.
  • Progression: new or changed plans start with read-throughs and tabletops before simulation, parallel or full interruption tests.
  • Coverage: track which systems and plans were tested, when, at what level and with what result, so gaps in the program are visible.
  • Reporting: test results and open issues reported to management and auditors as evidence of recovery capability (objective 6.3).

Common DR test findings

Objective 7.12Knowing what tests usually reveal, so plans can be fixed first.

  • Outdated contact lists and call trees.
  • Missing or wrong steps in runbooks, and steps that only one person knows.
  • Dependencies not in the plan, such as file shares, licence servers or DNS records.
  • Expired credentials, certificates or licences at the recovery site.
  • Backups that restore but are inconsistent or too slow to meet the RTO.
  • Security controls (logging, firewall rules, access control) missing at the alternate site.
  • Failback never tested.

Testing supplier and SaaS recovery

Objective 7.12Checking recovery for services you do not run yourself.

  • Ask critical suppliers for recent DR test results and their achieved recovery times.
  • Test your own workarounds for a SaaS outage, such as offline procedures or data exports kept elsewhere.
  • Test restoring your data from SaaS backups or exports, since many providers do not restore individual customer data on request.
  • Include key suppliers in exercises where contracts allow.

Unannounced and surprise elements

Objective 7.12Testing readiness that does not depend on advance warning.

  • Unannounced call tree tests: measure how many people respond and how quickly, outside business hours.
  • No-notice tabletop starts: leaders are told the scenario when they arrive, rather than in advance.
  • Removing a key person: tell the primary expert to sit out and see whether the alternate can run the recovery.
  • Safety still applies: surprise tests never touch production without approval, and senior management knows in advance even if participants do not.

Announced tests show that a plan can work; unannounced elements show whether the organization is ready when it is not expecting it.

Test names in other material

Objective 7.12Matching the names used in different study sources.

  • The read-through is often called a checklist test.
  • The structured walkthrough in many sources is the same as a tabletop exercise: team members walk through the plan around a table against a scenario. Some sources use walkthrough for a more hands-on rehearsal, as described above.
  • Simulation tests are sometimes merged with walkthroughs in older material.
  • Full interruption is also called a full-scale or cutover test.

Whatever the names, the exam logic is the order of increasing realism and disruption, and choosing the least disruptive test that meets the goal.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: least disruptive real test
Situation
Management wants to confirm the alternate site can run the workload without risking production.
Best response
Run a parallel test: bring systems up at the alternate site with real or copied workload while production continues.
Why
Parallel tests exercise systems without interrupting production; full interruption is riskier.
Scenario 2: the paper plan
Situation
The DR plan has been reviewed every year but never tested.
Best response
Start with a tabletop and walkthrough, then progress to simulation and parallel tests for critical systems.
Why
Work up through tests of increasing realism; reviews alone give little assurance.
Scenario 3: the successful failure
Situation
A DR test misses its RTO by three hours because of a missing runbook step.
Best response
Record it in the after-action report, fix the runbook, assign an owner and retest.
Why
The purpose of testing is to find and fix gaps.
Scenario 4: the uninformed SOC
Situation
During a DR test, the SOC sees mass logins from the alternate site and starts an incident response that disrupts the test.
Best response
Notify the SOC and relevant suppliers before tests, with a way to confirm test activity, while still monitoring for real attacks.
Why
Test planning includes communication so tests are not mistaken for incidents.
Scenario 5: the SaaS outage
Situation
A critical SaaS service is down for a day; the business has no workaround and no copy of its data.
Best response
Plan and test workarounds and regular data exports for critical SaaS, and ask the supplier for recovery commitments.
Why
DR planning covers services the organization does not run.

Key takeaways

  • Tests: read-through, tabletop, walkthrough, simulation, parallel, full interruption, in rising realism and risk; test communications; match tests to criticality.
  • Planning: objectives, scope, measurable success criteria, approvals, rollback and stop conditions, roles, realism; tell the SOC and suppliers.
  • Evaluation: compare with RTO and RPO, record issues, after-action report, owned actions, retest, update the plan; finding problems is success.
  • Automation: scheduled restore tests, routine failover drills, game days, controlled chaos engineering with limited blast radius.
