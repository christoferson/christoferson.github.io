Overview

Objective 7.2

7.2 Conduct logging and monitoring activities. Sub-topics: intrusion detection and prevention systems; security information and event management; security orchestration, automation and response; continuous monitoring and tuning; egress monitoring; log management; threat intelligence (threat feeds, threat hunting); user and entity behavior analytics.

Logging and monitoring basics are in CC 5.2; investigations on the 7.1 page; incident management on the 7.6 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its security operations center (SOC) runs a cloud SIEM and SOAR, with analysts on two shifts and an on-call engineer at night.

Log management

Objective 7.2Generating, protecting, retaining and disposing of logs so they support detection and investigations.

Log management is "the process for generating, transmitting, storing, accessing, and disposing of log data" (NIST SP 800-92 Rev. 1 draft; the 2006 SP 800-92 is still the final version). SP 800-53's AU family sets the requirements:

  • What: events adequate for after-the-fact investigations (AU-2), with what, when, where, source, outcome and identity in each record (AU-3).
  • Time: timestamps in UTC or with an offset (AU-8), from synchronized clocks.
  • Protection: "protect audit information and audit logging tools from unauthorized access, modification, and deletion" (AU-9), ideally on a separate system (AU-9(2)) that the administrators of monitored systems cannot change.
  • Review and correlation: AU-6, including correlation across repositories.
  • Retention (AU-11): long enough for investigations and legal requirements. For US federal agencies, OMB M-26-14 (May 2026) replaced the earlier logging memo with a "risk-based, prioritized logging approach", logs "actively searchable for a minimum of 6 months" and retrievable for a year.

CISA's baseline goals add: centralize logs, restrict access to them, and alert when a critical log source stops sending.

Detection: IDPS, SIEM, SOAR and UEBA

Objective 7.2Combining detection tools so attacks are spotted quickly and handled consistently.

  • Intrusion detection and prevention systems (IDPS): network or host based; signature detection is effective for known threats, anomaly detection finds unknown ones at the cost of false positives. An IPS sits inline and blocks; an IDS alerts.
  • SIEM: "a program that provides centralized logging capabilities for a variety of log types" (SP 800-92), adding normalization, correlation rules, alerting, dashboards and search for investigations.
  • SOAR (security orchestration, automation and response): playbooks that enrich alerts, open tickets and take actions automatically (disable an account, isolate a host, block an address), with human approval for risky steps. CISA and ASD ACSC published joint SIEM and SOAR implementation guidance in 2025.
  • User and entity behavior analytics (UEBA): baselines normal behavior for each user, host and service account and flags deviations, such as a buyer downloading ten times their usual volume or a service account logging in interactively. Useful for insider threats and stolen credentials.
  • Continuous monitoring and tuning: continuous monitoring is "maintaining ongoing awareness to support organizational risk decisions" (NIST SP 800-137 sets out a program for it). Tuning removes noisy rules, adds detections for new threats and checks coverage against frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK; NIST's incident response guidance says to tune "to reduce false positives and false negatives to acceptable levels."

False negatives are the dangerous ones. False positives waste analysts' time and cause alert fatigue; false negatives mean an attack is missed. Tuning that only suppresses alerts can create false negatives, so test detections (with attack simulations) after tuning.

Egress monitoring, threat intelligence and hunting

Objective 7.2Watching what leaves the organization, using intelligence about attackers, and searching proactively for what tools missed.

  • Egress monitoring: watching outbound traffic and data for theft and command-and-control: DLP on endpoints, email, web and cloud for sensitive content; proxy and DNS logs for connections to unusual or newly registered domains; volume anomalies; and encrypted traffic inspection where policy allows. Steganography and encrypted archives can hide data, so watch for behavior as well as content.
  • Threat intelligence: threat feeds of indicators of compromise ("technical artifacts or observables that suggest that an attack is imminent or is currently underway") shared in standard formats such as STIX over TAXII, plus reports on adversaries' tactics, techniques and procedures. Intelligence is strategic (trends for leadership), operational (campaigns) or tactical (indicators). Indicators age quickly; behaviors last longer.
  • Threat hunting: analysts proactively search for attackers who evaded detection, starting from a hypothesis (for example, "an attacker is using legitimate admin tools to move laterally"), often based on intelligence or ATT&CK techniques. Successful hunts become new automated detections.

Building and testing detections

Objective 7.2Turning knowledge of attacker behavior into reliable alerts.

Detection engineering treats detections like software: each one has a purpose, logic, tests and an owner.

  1. Start from threatsPick the techniques most relevant to the organization, using threat intelligence and MITRE ATT&CK, and check which ones current rules cover.
  2. Confirm the dataMake sure the needed logs are collected, parsed and complete (a rule on process creation is useless if endpoints do not send those events).
  3. Write the logicSignatures, correlation rules, thresholds or behavioral models; vendor-neutral formats such as Sigma help share rules across tools.
  4. TestRun the technique safely (attack simulation, purple teaming) and confirm the alert fires, then measure false positives on real data.
  5. Document and maintainEach alert has a description, severity, response playbook and owner; rules are version-controlled and reviewed when systems or threats change.

Coverage is measured against the techniques that matter, not by the number of rules. At the retail group, a gap review found no detection for new administrator accounts created in the cloud identity provider; the new rule and its SOAR playbook went live after a purple team test triggered it twice.

Running security operations

Objective 7.2Organizing people, process and measures so alerts are handled well.

  • Triage: analysts validate alerts, enrich them (asset owner, user, threat intelligence), decide severity and either close, escalate or open an incident. SOAR automates the repetitive parts.
  • Tiers or pods: first-line analysts handle triage, senior analysts investigate and hunt, engineers maintain tools and detections; smaller teams combine roles or use a managed detection and response (MDR) provider.
  • Coverage: around-the-clock monitoring through shifts, on-call rotas or a provider; attacks often start outside business hours.
  • Measures: mean time to detect and to respond, dwell time (how long attackers were present before detection), alert volume and false positive rate per rule, and the share of incidents found internally rather than reported by outsiders.
  • Alert fatigue: too many low-value alerts lead analysts to miss real ones; tune, suppress duplicates, and route informational events away from the queue.
  • Feedback: incidents and hunts feed new detections, and analysts' notes feed tuning.

Logging in cloud and SaaS

Objective 7.2Making sure cloud and software-as-a-service activity is actually recorded and kept.

  • Control plane logs: records of API calls that create, change or delete resources and permissions. They are the most important cloud log, and providers usually record them by default but keep them only for a limited time unless you export them.
  • Data plane logs: access to storage objects, databases and functions is often not logged by default and may cost extra; enable it for sensitive data.
  • Identity logs: sign-ins, MFA events, token issuance and admin changes from the identity provider, which are central to detecting account takeover.
  • SaaS logs: audit logs vary by product and licence tier; check during procurement that the logs needed are included and can be exported (a CISA Secure by Demand question, objective 8.4).
  • Central and protected: send logs from every account and subscription to a separate logging account that workload administrators cannot change, with immutable storage and retention set by policy.

Cloud logs are also evidence: knowing which logs exist and how long they are kept, before an incident, decides what an investigation can prove.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the deleted logs
Situation
After a breach, investigators find that the attacker, using a stolen administrator account, deleted the server logs.
Best response
Send logs in real time to a separate, protected log system the administrators cannot alter, and alert when a source stops sending.
Why
AU-9 asks for logs protected from modification and deletion, ideally on a separate system.
Scenario 2: the clock problem
Situation
Correlating events between firewalls and servers fails because their times differ by several minutes.
Best response
Synchronize all systems to authoritative time sources and record timestamps in UTC or with an offset.
Why
Timelines need synchronized clocks (AU-8).
Scenario 3: 4,000 alerts a day
Situation
Analysts close most alerts without looking because the queue is too long.
Best response
Tune and retire noisy rules, automate enrichment and low-risk responses with SOAR, and test detections after tuning to avoid false negatives.
Why
Alert fatigue causes missed attacks; quality of detections beats quantity.
Scenario 4: the large upload
Situation
UEBA flags a buyer who uploaded ten times their usual data volume to a personal cloud storage site.
Best response
Investigate through the agreed process with HR and legal, preserve the evidence, and use DLP and egress controls to block such uploads.
Why
Behavior analytics and egress monitoring detect insider data theft; the response follows investigation rules.

Key takeaways

  • Logs (AU family, SP 800-92): what to log, UTC time, protected from admins, correlated, retained (M-26-14 for federal agencies); alert when logging stops.
  • Detection: IDPS, SIEM, SOAR playbooks, UEBA baselines, continuous monitoring (SP 800-137) and tuning; false negatives are the dangerous errors.
  • Egress, intelligence, hunting: DLP and outbound anomaly monitoring; feeds (IoCs, STIX/TAXII) and TTPs; hypothesis-driven hunts become detections.
  • Detection engineering: threats first (ATT&CK), confirm data, write, test with simulations, document and maintain; measure coverage of relevant techniques.
  • Operations: triage and enrichment, tiers or MDR, 24x7 coverage, MTTD, MTTR and dwell time, fight alert fatigue. Cloud: control plane, data plane, identity and SaaS logs, centralized and protected.
