Overview

Objective 7.4

7.4 Apply foundational security operations concepts. Sub-topics: defense in depth; need-to-know and least privilege; separation of duties (SoD) and responsibilities; privileged account management; job rotation.

The principles are introduced in CC 3.2; privileged access in identity management on the 5.5 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Its IT operations team of 60 runs the data center, store systems and cloud accounts in shifts.

Foundational security operations concepts

Objective 7.4Applying defense in depth, least privilege, separation of duties, privileged account controls and job rotation in daily operations.

  • Defense in depth: multiple coordinated layers of controls, technical, operational and administrative, so no single failure leads to compromise. In operations: hardened hosts, segmentation, monitoring, backups and trained staff, each covering the others' gaps.
  • Need-to-know and least privilege: least privilege limits what someone can do to "the minimum necessary to accomplish assigned tasks"; need-to-know limits which information they may see even within their clearance or role. Operations staff get only the tools and systems their shift requires.
  • Separation of duties and responsibilities: "no user should be given enough privileges to misuse the system on their own". Examples in operations: whoever writes code does not deploy it to production alone; security administrators do not also administer the audit logs; requesting and approving access are different people. Two-person control (dual authorization) covers the most critical actions.
  • Privileged account management: an inventory of every privileged account, separate admin accounts, no standing privilege (just-in-time elevation), credentials vaulted and rotated, sessions recorded, and break-glass accounts sealed, monitored and tested (objective 5.5).
  • Job rotation: moving people through roles periodically. It reduces dependence on one person, gives cross-training, and exposes fraud or errors that the previous holder was hiding. Mandatory vacations serve a similar detective purpose: someone else does the job for a while.

Collusion. Separation of duties stops one person acting alone, but two people can collude. Job rotation and mandatory vacations make long-running collusion harder to sustain and more likely to be found.

Secure administration in practice

Objective 7.4Turning the principles into the way administrators actually work.

  • Tiered administration: separate admin accounts for each tier (identity systems, servers, workstations), so a credential stolen from a workstation cannot be used on a domain controller.
  • Privileged access workstations: dedicated, hardened devices for administration, with no email or web browsing, so phishing and drive-by attacks cannot reach admin sessions.
  • Jump hosts and gateways: administrative access to servers and network devices goes through a controlled gateway that authenticates, records and limits sessions.
  • Runbooks and automation: routine tasks run through reviewed scripts or automation with narrow permissions, which reduces the number of people who need direct privileged access.
  • Shift handover: open incidents, changes in progress and elevated accesses are handed over in writing, so privileges granted for a task are removed when it ends.

At the retail group, store server support moved from shared local administrator passwords to named accounts elevated through PAM for a ticketed task, with passwords rotated after each use. The number of people with standing administrator rights on store servers fell from 140 to 12.

Monitoring and reviewing privileged activity

Objective 7.4Checking that privileged access is used as intended.

  • Log every elevation: who requested privilege, who approved it, for which system and how long, linked to a ticket.
  • Alert on high-risk actions: new administrator accounts, changes to security groups or audit settings, log clearing, use of break-glass accounts, and privileged sign-ins from unusual places or times.
  • Session recording: recordings of sensitive sessions are kept and reviewed on a sample basis and for every break-glass use, by someone other than the administrator.
  • Periodic review: privileged accounts and group memberships are certified more often than normal access (for example quarterly), and unused privileges are removed.
  • Protect the reviewers' data: administrators should not be able to alter their own logs or recordings (separation of duties again).

Privileged account monitoring is also an insider threat control: most insider sabotage cases involve people with elevated access, and monitoring that they know exists is itself a deterrent.

Personnel controls in operations

Objective 7.4Managing the people who hold operational power.

  • Screening: background checks proportionate to the role, repeated for privileged and sensitive roles where law allows (objective 1.8).
  • Agreements: acceptable use, confidentiality and privileged user agreements that state responsibilities and the monitoring that applies.
  • Two-person integrity: the most sensitive tasks (key ceremonies, root account use, production database deletion) require two authorized people present or approving.
  • Cross-training: at least two people can perform each critical task, which supports job rotation and avoids single points of failure in staff.
  • Prompt offboarding: privileged access is removed first and fastest when an administrator leaves or changes role; shared secrets they knew are rotated.
  • Insider risk program: HR, legal, security and management share concerns through a defined process that respects privacy.

Defense in depth in operations

Objective 7.4Seeing how layers work together against a real attack path.

Defense in depth is easiest to understand by following one attack through the layers. Take a phishing email aimed at a store manager:

  1. Email filteringBlocks most malicious messages and rewrites links for checking at click time.
  2. AwarenessA trained user spots and reports the message, and the SOC removes copies from other mailboxes.
  3. Phishing-resistant MFAEven if the user enters a password on a fake page, the stolen password alone does not work.
  4. Endpoint protectionIf an attachment is opened, EDR blocks or contains the malicious process.
  5. Least privilege and segmentationThe manager's account and the store network cannot reach payment systems or other stores.
  6. MonitoringUnusual sign-ins or lateral movement attempts raise alerts for the SOC.
  7. Backups and responseIf all else fails, the incident plan and immutable backups limit the damage.

Layers should be diverse and independent: two layers that fail for the same reason (both depend on the same directory, or the same vendor's detection engine) give less protection than they appear to. Operations teams check that each layer is actually working, because a layer that was switched off during troubleshooting and never re-enabled gives only the appearance of depth.

Least privilege for automation

Objective 7.4Applying the same principles to scripts, pipelines and service accounts.

Much privileged activity is now performed by automation rather than people, and automation accounts are often the most powerful and least watched:

  • Narrow permissions: each pipeline, script and service account has only the permissions for its task, scoped to specific resources rather than whole accounts.
  • Short-lived credentials: workload identities and federated tokens instead of long-lived keys stored in scripts or pipeline variables.
  • Separation in pipelines: the identity that builds code is not the one that deploys to production; production deployment needs an approval step.
  • Owners and reviews: every non-human identity has a human owner and is reviewed like privileged user accounts; unused ones are disabled.
  • Monitoring: interactive sign-ins by service accounts, use from unexpected locations, or actions outside their normal pattern are alerts.

Compromise of a build pipeline or a highly privileged automation account can affect every system it deploys to, so these accounts deserve the same protection as domain administrators.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the developer deploys
Situation
A developer can write code, approve their own pull request and deploy it to production.
Best response
Separate duties: require review by another person and deploy only through the pipeline with its approvals.
Why
Separation of duties stops one person introducing unreviewed changes to production.
Scenario 2: the fraud found on holiday
Situation
A payments clerk never takes leave; when finally forced to, the replacement finds unexplained refunds.
Best response
Enforce mandatory vacations and job rotation for sensitive roles, and investigate the refunds under the investigation process.
Why
Mandatory vacations and rotation are detective controls against fraud and collusion.
Scenario 3: email on the admin laptop
Situation
Domain administrators read email and browse the web on the same laptop they use to administer servers.
Best response
Provide privileged access workstations for administration and separate everyday accounts and devices.
Why
Separating admin activity from risky activity protects privileged credentials.
Scenario 4: the shared root password
Situation
Six engineers share one password for a critical cloud root account.
Best response
Vault the credential with MFA, restrict its use to break-glass with two-person approval, monitor every use, and give engineers named roles for daily work.
Why
Privileged account management requires accountability: named accounts, vaulting, monitoring.

Key takeaways

  • Defense in depth: coordinated technical, operational and administrative layers.
  • Need-to-know limits information; least privilege limits actions. Separation of duties stops one person acting alone; two-person control for critical actions.
  • Privileged accounts: inventory, separate accounts, JIT, vaulting, session recording, monitored break-glass. Job rotation and mandatory vacations detect fraud and counter collusion.
  • Secure administration: tiers, privileged access workstations, gateways, runbooks and automation, written handovers.
  • Monitoring and people: log and review elevation, alert on high-risk actions, record sessions; screening, agreements, cross-training, fast offboarding, insider risk program.
