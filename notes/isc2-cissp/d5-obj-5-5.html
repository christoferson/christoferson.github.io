Overview

Objective 5.5

5.5 Manage the identity and access provisioning lifecycle. Sub-topics: account access review (user, system, service); provisioning and deprovisioning (onboarding, offboarding and transfers); role definition and transition; privilege escalation (administrator groups, sudo, auditing, least privilege); service accounts management.

The identity life cycle is introduced in CC 3.1; the systems that implement it are on the 5.6 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Seasonal hiring adds 1,500 temporary store staff every autumn, all of whom leave in January.

Provisioning, transfers and deprovisioning

Objective 5.5Driving access from HR events so joiners get the right access on day one, movers lose what they no longer need, and leavers lose everything at once.

  1. Joiner (onboarding)HR creates the record; the identity system creates the account and grants "birthright" access for the role automatically (the federal ILM playbook: "'Day One' access is automated based on HR data"). Anything extra needs a request and the owner's approval.
  2. Mover (transfer)SP 800-53 PS-5 requires reviewing and confirming ongoing need when someone transfers. New role access is added and old role access removed, so people do not accumulate rights (privilege creep).
  3. Leaver (offboarding)PS-4 requires disabling access within a defined time and revoking credentials and authenticators; AC-2 aligns account management "with personnel termination and transfer processes". Disable first, delete later (keeping data and logs), and include badges, tokens, API keys, shared passwords and SaaS accounts. SCIM pushes the change to connected applications.

Role definition and transition: when people change roles, or roles themselves change after a reorganization, role definitions and memberships need updating together. Common traps are leaving the old role in place "for a handover" with no end date, and temporary acting assignments that become permanent. Give transitional access an expiry date and an owner.

Hostile or high-risk departures. For a dismissal or a privileged leaver, access is disabled at the moment the person is told, not at the end of the day. Shared or privileged credentials they knew are changed (AC-2 requires changing shared authenticators when someone leaves the group).

Account access reviews

Objective 5.5Regularly confirming that every user, system and service account still needs exactly the access it has.

AC-2 requires reviewing accounts at an organization-defined frequency; CISA's CPG 2.0 suggests quarterly reviews of access permissions and role assignments and disabling accounts inactive for a set period (its example: 30 days). Reviews cover three kinds of account:

  • User accounts: managers or data owners certify each person's access; anything not certified is removed automatically ("automated revocation if uncertified", in the federal playbook).
  • System accounts: built-in and administrator accounts on servers, databases and network devices, checked against the inventory and baselines.
  • Service accounts: confirmed by their named owner, with their permissions and secret age checked.

Reviews work only if the reviewer can understand what they are approving (meaningful entitlement names, not cryptic group codes) and if rubber-stamping is detected. Higher-risk access, such as privileged roles and access to card data, is reviewed more often. The output is evidence for auditors (Domain 6).

Privilege escalation

Objective 5.5Allowing administrator power only when needed, through controlled paths, and recording every use.

Legitimate privilege escalation is how administrators do their work; uncontrolled escalation is how attackers take over. Least privilege (AC-6) means everyday work runs without privilege and elevation is deliberate:

  • Separate accounts: administrators use a separate privileged account (AC-6(2)) and never read email or browse with it; CISA's top misconfigurations list includes "improper separation of user/administrator privilege".
  • Administrator groups: keep membership of domain, cloud and local admin groups small, reviewed and alerted on; any addition outside a change request is an incident signal.
  • sudo (Unix and Linux): grant specific commands to specific users or groups rather than full root; avoid rules that allow shells or editors that can escape to a root shell; require reauthentication; log every command centrally.
  • Just-in-time elevation: privileges granted for a task and a time window through PAM, then removed (objective 5.2).
  • Auditing: AC-6(9) requires logging "the execution of privileged functions", and AC-6(10) preventing non-privileged users from executing them. Send the logs where admins cannot change them, and review them.

Unauthorized escalation, by exploiting vulnerabilities, misconfigured permissions, or stolen admin credentials and tickets (objective 3.7), is detected by alerts on new admin group members, unusual sudo use and privileged logons from unexpected hosts.

Service accounts

Objective 5.5Managing the non-human accounts that applications, scripts and AI agents use, which often outnumber people.

  • Inventory and ownership: every service account, API key and workload identity has a named human owner and a documented purpose; the federal digital worker playbook adds a sponsor who is accountable and regular recertification.
  • Least privilege: only the permissions the service needs; service accounts should not be domain or cloud administrators; deny interactive logon.
  • Secrets: no passwords in code or scripts (SP 800-53 IA-5(7)); store them in a secrets manager, rotate automatically, or better, use managed identities and short-lived tokens with no stored secret. CISA's CPG 2.0: no universal passwords for non-person entity accounts.
  • Monitoring: service accounts behave predictably, so alert on unusual use (new hosts, interactive logons, off-pattern times). Long, random passwords also defeat Kerberoasting.
  • Deprovisioning: when the application is retired, the account, its keys and its permissions go too. Orphaned service accounts are common and attractive to attackers.

The retail group found 2,300 of its 9,000 service accounts had no owner; it disabled them in waves, re-enabling only those whose break revealed an owner, and moved its applications to managed identities in both clouds.

Identity governance and metrics

Objective 5.5Running the provisioning life cycle as a measured, governed program.

Identity governance and administration (IGA) brings provisioning, reviews, role management and policy enforcement into one program with clear ownership (often a joint IAM team across security, IT and HR). Measures that show whether it works:

  • Time to provision new starters and time to deprovision leavers (target: on the last day, immediately for hostile departures).
  • Orphaned and dormant accounts found by reconciliation, and how fast they are removed.
  • Review completion and the share of access revoked in reviews (near zero may mean rubber-stamping).
  • Privileged access: number of standing admin accounts, and use of just-in-time elevation.
  • Service accounts with owners, and secrets older than policy allows.
  • Separation of duties violations detected and resolved.

Reported to the security steering committee with other identity risks, these turn a technical process into a managed risk (objective 6.3).

Role definition and transition in depth

Objective 5.5Designing roles that stay accurate as the organization changes.

  • Role design: start from job functions, use role mining (analysis of the access people actually have and use) to propose roles, and have business owners approve them. Keep roles few and meaningful.
  • Role owners: each role has an owner who approves its permissions and reviews it periodically, separate from reviews of who holds it.
  • Reorganizations: when departments merge or split, update role definitions and memberships together, and remove roles that no longer match any job.
  • Temporary and acting roles: cover for a manager on leave or a project role is granted with an end date and reviewed when it ends.
  • Contractors and seasonal staff: separate role sets with narrower access and built-in expiry; sponsors are accountable for them.
  • Conflicting roles: separation of duties rules prevent combinations such as requester and approver of the same transaction.

At the retail group, the store manager role was split into store manager and cash office supervisor after an audit showed one role could both adjust till counts and approve the discrepancy report.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the hostile departure
Situation
A database administrator is dismissed for misconduct at 3 p.m.
Best response
Disable access, revoke sessions and credentials and collect devices at the moment the person is told, change shared secrets they knew, and review recent activity.
Why
Hostile terminations need immediate, coordinated revocation.
Scenario 2: the mover
Situation
An employee moved from payroll to marketing a year ago and still has payroll access.
Best response
Remove the old access, and fix the process so transfers trigger review and removal of previous role access (PS-5).
Why
Privilege creep comes from adding access on transfer without removing the old.
Scenario 3: sudo everything
Situation
Developers have "sudo ALL" on production servers "for troubleshooting".
Best response
Replace with specific, audited commands or just-in-time elevation through PAM, logging every privileged command centrally.
Why
Least privilege and auditing of privilege escalation (AC-6(9)).
Scenario 4: the shared service password
Situation
Twelve applications use the same database service account password, which has not changed in five years.
Best response
Give each application its own account with least privilege, store secrets in a vault with automatic rotation, or move to managed identities.
Why
Shared, static service credentials defeat accountability and are a prime target.

Key takeaways

  • Joiners, movers, leavers: HR drives automated birthright access; transfers review and remove old access (PS-5); leavers disabled promptly with all credentials revoked (PS-4, AC-2); disable before delete; SCIM propagates.
  • Reviews: users, systems and services, risk-based, with automatic removal if not certified.
  • Privilege escalation: separate admin accounts, small monitored admin groups, narrow sudo rules, JIT elevation, log privileged functions (AC-6(9)).
  • Service accounts: owner, purpose, least privilege, no interactive logon, secrets in a vault or none at all, monitoring, deprovisioning.
  • Governance: IGA program with metrics for provisioning and deprovisioning time, orphaned accounts, reviews, standing privilege and SoD violations.
