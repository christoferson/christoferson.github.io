Overview

Objective 3.10

3.10 Manage the information system lifecycle. Sub-topics: stakeholder needs and requirements; requirements analysis; secure architectural design; development/implementation; integration; verification and validation; continuous security review; transition/deployment; operations and maintenance/sustainment; retirement/secure disposal.

Secure design principles are on the 3.1 page; secure development in Domain 8. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is building a new order management platform and retiring an old reporting system.

The information system life cycle

Objective 3.10Building security into every stage of a system's life, from the first stakeholder conversation to disposal.

NIST SP 800-160 Vol. 1 Rev. 1 (2022), Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems, applies systems security engineering to the system life cycle processes of ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288. The stages in the outline follow those processes:

  1. Stakeholder needs and requirementsCapture what the business, users, regulators and customers need, including protection needs and acceptable risk.
  2. Requirements analysisTurn needs into specific, testable system security requirements, traced back to their source.
  3. Secure architectural designApply the design principles and models from objective 3.1, threat model the design, and choose controls (objective 3.3).
  4. Development and implementationBuild or buy the components using secure development practices (Domain 8).
  5. IntegrationAssemble components and check that the security of the whole holds, including interfaces and inherited controls.
  6. Verification and validationVerify that requirements are met, and validate that the system meets its intended use.
  7. Transition and deploymentMove into production in a secure configuration, with authorization to operate and handover of security documentation.
  8. Operations and maintenancePatch, monitor, manage changes and sustain the system's security as threats change.
  9. Retirement and secure disposalMigrate or archive data, sanitize media, revoke accounts and certificates, and update inventories (objective 2.5).

Continuous security review runs across all stages: reassessing risk, design and controls whenever requirements, threats or the system change, not only at go-live.

Verification vs validation. Verification is "confirmation, through the provision of objective evidence, that specified requirements have been fulfilled" (are we building the system right?). Validation confirms that the requirements for "a specific intended use or application have been fulfilled", that is, the stakeholders' real needs are met (are we building the right system?).

Requirements, design and build in depth

Objective 3.10Getting security right in the early stages, where changes are cheapest.

  • Stakeholder needs: business owners, users, operations, legal, privacy, regulators and customers each bring security and privacy needs; capture protection needs (what must be protected, how badly) and constraints (budget, schedule, technology).
  • Requirements analysis: turn needs into requirements that are specific, measurable and testable, separate functional from assurance requirements, resolve conflicts, and trace each to its source (objective 3.3).
  • Secure architectural design: apply design principles (objective 3.1), threat model each trust boundary, choose reference architectures, and hold design reviews with security architects before build. Design decisions and accepted risks are recorded.
  • Development and implementation: secure coding standards, code review, SAST and dependency scanning, secrets management and protected build pipelines (Domain 8); for purchased components, evaluated products and contractual requirements.
  • Integration: security of interfaces between components and with external systems, inherited controls confirmed, and end-to-end tests of security functions; integration is where assumptions made by different teams collide.

SP 800-53 SA-3 (system development life cycle) asks organizations to manage systems using a life cycle that incorporates security and privacy considerations and assigns roles and responsibilities throughout. The cost argument is simple: a flaw fixed at requirements or design costs a fraction of one fixed after release.

Sustaining and retiring systems securely

Objective 3.10Keeping security current through years of operation, and ending systems without leaving risk behind.

  • Transition: deploy from approved configurations, complete security testing and authorization to operate, hand over runbooks, monitoring and support contacts, and remove test accounts and data.
  • Operations and maintenance: patching and vulnerability management, monitoring and incident response, access reviews, backup and recovery tests, and change management for every modification (objectives 7.8 and 7.9).
  • Sustainment: keep components within vendor support, renew certificates and licenses, refresh threat models as the system and threats change, and budget for technology refresh.
  • Continuous security review: a security checkpoint at each stage gate, and reassessment triggered by significant change, new threats, incidents or audit findings, not only at go-live.
  • Retirement and secure disposal: decide what data to migrate, archive or destroy; sanitize media; revoke accounts, keys and certificates; remove DNS, firewall rules and integrations; update inventories (objective 2.5).

Verification and validation in practice

Objective 3.10Planning the checks that show a system meets its requirements and the real needs of its users.

Verification and validation run through the whole life cycle, not only at the end:

  • Verification methods (NIST lists inspection, analysis, demonstration and test): requirements and design reviews, code review and static analysis, unit and integration tests, security testing, and configuration audits against baselines.
  • Validation methods: user acceptance testing with real users and realistic data, pilots in a few stores before rollout, operational exercises, and review of whether the system actually reduces the risks it was meant to.
  • Independence: for high-risk systems, independent verification and validation by people not involved in building the system gives more assurance, as do external penetration tests and assessments.
  • Traceability: every requirement maps to at least one verification activity, and the results are evidence for authorization.
  • Regression: changes are re-verified, ideally by automated tests in the pipeline, so fixes do not break existing security functions.

A system can pass verification and still fail validation, for example when requirements missed how shared store devices are really used. Involving users and operators early in requirements and design is the best way to avoid that gap.

Life cycle models and security

Objective 3.10Fitting the same security activities into different development and acquisition approaches.

NIST's Risk Management Framework describes system development life cycle phases as initiation, development and acquisition, implementation, operation and maintenance, and disposal; ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 describes the same work as technical processes. Real projects follow different models, and security activities map onto each:

  • Sequential (waterfall): security gates at the end of each phase (requirements sign-off, design review, test, go-live authorization).
  • Iterative and agile: security requirements as backlog items, threat modeling per feature or epic, security checks in the definition of done, and periodic architecture reviews as the design evolves.
  • DevOps and continuous delivery: automated security tests and policy checks in the pipeline, with continuous monitoring and rapid patching in operation (objective 8.1).
  • Acquired systems and SaaS: the vendor runs much of the life cycle, so security shifts to requirements in the contract, evaluation of the vendor's practices, configuration, integration and ongoing supplier monitoring (objective 1.11).

Whatever the model, the outcomes are the same: requirements defined and traced, designs reviewed, components built or bought securely, security verified and validated, changes controlled, and systems retired cleanly.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: security at the end
Situation
A project invites the security team to review a new system two weeks before launch.
Best response
Do a focused risk review now, and change the life cycle so security joins at requirements and design, with gates at each stage.
Why
Security must be built in from the start; late reviews find expensive problems and leave little time to fix them.
Scenario 2: verification passed, users unhappy
Situation
All security requirements were verified in testing, but store staff cannot use the new MFA on shared tills and are bypassing it.
Best response
Treat it as a validation failure: revisit stakeholder needs for shared devices and redesign (for example badge-tap authentication).
Why
Verification checks requirements were met; validation checks the system meets real needs in its intended use.
Scenario 3: the forgotten system
Situation
An old reporting system was switched off, but its service account, API keys and firewall rules still exist.
Best response
Complete retirement: revoke credentials and keys, remove rules and DNS, sanitize storage, and update the inventory.
Why
Secure disposal covers access paths and records, not only the hardware.
Scenario 4: the major upgrade
Situation
A system in operation for four years is adding a public API for partners.
Best response
Trigger a security review: update the threat model, add requirements for the API, test it, and re-authorize if the risk changes significantly.
Why
Continuous security review is triggered by significant change, not just by schedule.

Key takeaways

  • Life cycle (SP 800-160, ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288, SA-3): needs, requirements, design, implementation, integration, verification and validation, transition, operations, disposal, with continuous security review throughout.
  • Early stages: capture protection needs; specific, testable, traced requirements; design principles, threat models and design reviews; secure build; integration tests of interfaces and inherited controls.
  • Verification vs validation: built right (requirements met) vs right thing built (real needs met).
  • Later stages: secure transition and authorization, operations and maintenance with change management, sustainment within vendor support, change-triggered reviews.
  • Retirement: data disposition, sanitization, revocation of accounts, keys and certificates, removal of rules and DNS, inventory updates.
