5.1–5.2: Access to assets and authentication strategy

Controlling who and what can reach every kind of asset through its whole life, and designing how people, devices and services prove who they are: from identity proofing to passkeys, sessions, single sign-on and just-in-time privilege.

Overview

Objectives 5.1 and 5.2

5.1 Manage physical and logical access to assets. 5.2 Design identification and authentication strategy.

The basics are in the CC guide: authentication factors and MFA in CC 1.1, and the identity life cycle and access reviews in CC 3.1. This page takes the design view. Federation protocols and authorization models are on the next page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It has 4,000 workforce identities, 9,000 service accounts and API keys, 12 million customer accounts, and a growing number of AI agents.

Controlling access to assets

Objective 5.1Running one access life cycle (provision, control, review, revoke) across every kind of asset, physical and logical.

The same four steps apply whatever the asset:

  1. ProvisionGrant access on an approved request, by role, with least privilege. HR events drive workforce access ("the HR/personnel system is the sole driver of identity creation and termination", in the federal ILM playbook).
  2. ControlEnforce it at the asset: authentication, authorization, physical locks and badges, network controls, and logging.
  3. ReviewOwners recertify access regularly (CISA's CPG 2.0 suggests quarterly for permissions and role assignments), removing what is no longer needed.
  4. RevokeRemove access promptly on termination, transfer or when it is no longer justified (SP 800-53 AC-2 and PS-4), including credentials, tokens, keys and badges.

What changes from asset to asset is who owns the decision and how it is enforced:

  • Information: the data owner approves access by classification and need-to-know; enforced by permissions, encryption and DLP.
  • Systems: administrators' and users' accounts on servers and platforms, with privileged access separated and monitored.
  • Devices: only enrolled, managed and healthy devices may connect (NAC, device certificates, MDM).
  • Facilities: badges, zones and visitor processes, aligned with logical access so a leaver's badge and accounts are removed together (CSF 2.0 PR.AA-06).
  • Applications: roles inside each application, ideally driven centrally through SSO and automated provisioning (SCIM).
  • Services: cloud and SaaS services and APIs, whose access lives in each provider's IAM and needs the same reviews.
  • Identities: the identities themselves are assets, including service accounts, workload identities and AI agents, each with an accountable owner.

Orphaned and forgotten access. A CISA advisory (AA24-046A) described attackers using a former employee's administrator account that "was not disabled immediately following the employee's departure". Revocation must cover every asset type, and especially privileged and non-human accounts, which are easily missed.

Groups, roles and AAA

Objective 5.2Organizing identities so access is granted to roles rather than individuals, and accounting for what they do.

  • Groups collect identities for administration (all store managers in the EU); roles are "the set of named duties or job functions within an organization" with their permissions. Granting access to roles keeps it consistent and reviewable; direct grants to individuals become exceptions to justify.
  • Role engineering: too few roles give people more than they need; too many become unmanageable ("role explosion"). Separation of duties is built in by making conflicting roles mutually exclusive.
  • AAA: authentication (proving identity), authorization (what the identity may do) and accounting (recording what it did). Accounting is the protocol term from RADIUS and Diameter; NIST speaks of accountability, which accounting logs make possible. RADIUS (RFC 2865) and TACACS+ are the classic AAA protocols for network devices; TACACS+ separates the three functions and encrypts the whole payload, which is why it is preferred for administering network devices.

Registration, proofing and establishing identity

Objective 5.2Making sure an identity belongs to a real, correct person, device or service before credentials are issued.

Identity proofing means to "collect, validate, and verify information about a subject"; enrollment then gives the proofed applicant a subscriber account and binds authenticators to it. NIST SP 800-63A-4 (July 2025) defines three identity assurance levels:

  • IAL1: the claimed identity is validated against evidence at a basic level.
  • IAL2: stronger evidence and verification, remote or in person; the usual level for workforce and sensitive customer accounts.
  • IAL3: in-person (or attended kiosk) proofing with a trained operator, for the highest-risk access.

Proofing must also cover recovery: attackers who cannot beat MFA call the help desk and ask for a reset. Account recovery should need the same assurance as enrollment. For devices and services, establishing identity means registering them in inventory, issuing device certificates or workload identities from a trusted source, and naming a human owner.

The retail group proofs new staff in person during onboarding at IAL2 and issues security keys there; help desk resets for privileged users need a video call with the manager and a check of the original documents.

Authentication strength and passwordless

Objective 5.2Choosing authenticators that match the risk, moving to phishing-resistant and passwordless methods.

NIST SP 800-63B-4 defines three authentication assurance levels: AAL1 allows single-factor; AAL2 requires "two distinct authentication factors", and verifiers must offer at least one phishing-resistant option; AAL3 requires an authenticator with "a non-exportable private key" that provides phishing resistance. SMS and voice codes should be a last resort.

Passwordless authentication removes the shared secret:

  • FIDO2 (the FIDO Alliance's standards) and WebAuthn (the W3C web API; Level 3 became a Recommendation in August 2026): the device creates a key pair for each site; the private key never leaves the authenticator, and the signature is bound to the site's origin, so a look-alike phishing site gets nothing usable.
  • Passkeys: "an authentication credential based on FIDO standards". Device-bound passkeys (security keys, some platform authenticators) never leave one device and suit AAL3; synced passkeys are backed up across a user's devices through a provider, which is convenient but means the sync account must be protected too.
  • Biometrics: NIST says "a biometric characteristic is not recognized as an authenticator by itself"; it should unlock a physical authenticator (such as a fingerprint on a phone holding a passkey) rather than be matched centrally. Biometric errors are false rejections (type I) and false acceptances (type II); the crossover error rate, where they are equal, compares systems.

Phishing-resistant is not the same as MFA. Push approvals, one-time codes and SMS are multi-factor but can be phished or relayed (adversary-in-the-middle kits, MFA fatigue). Only origin-bound methods (FIDO2/WebAuthn, smart cards with PKI) resist phishing. CISA guidance names hardware FIDO keys as most effective, with passkeys an acceptable alternative.

Sessions, SSO, credentials and just-in-time access

Objective 5.2Protecting what happens after login, reducing the number of credentials, and removing standing privilege.

  • Session management: after authentication, the session token is the credential. Generate it randomly, send it only over TLS with secure cookie settings, bind it to the client where possible, expire it on inactivity and after a maximum time (SP 800-63B-4 sets reauthentication limits per AAL), end it fully on logout, and reauthenticate before high-risk actions. Stolen session cookies bypass MFA entirely.
  • Single sign-on (SSO): "one account and its authenticators are used to access multiple applications". Fewer passwords and central control, including one place to disable a leaver, but the identity provider becomes a single point of failure and a prime target, so it needs the strongest authentication and monitoring.
  • Federated identity management (FIM): SSO across organizations, where an identity provider asserts a user's identity to relying parties it does not run (SAML, OpenID Connect; next page).
  • Credential management systems: password vaults (managers) for people, which NIST says verifiers should allow; enterprise secrets managers for service credentials and API keys; and privileged access management (PAM), which checks out, rotates and records the use of administrator credentials so no one knows standing admin passwords.
  • Just-in-time access (JIT): privileges are granted when needed, for a limited time and after approval, then removed automatically. CISA's zero trust maturity model puts "just-in-time and just-enough access" at the optimal stage, and its cloud baselines forbid permanent active assignments for highly privileged roles.
Authentication strategy (retail group)
Workforce
SSO for all applications; passkeys on managed devices; security keys for administrators and finance (AAL3)
Privileged
No standing admin rights; JIT elevation through PAM, approved and time-limited, sessions recorded
Devices
Device certificates from the internal CA, checked at sign-in and by NAC
Services and AI agents
Workload identities and short-lived tokens; secrets in a secrets manager, rotated automatically; a named owner for each
Customers
Passkeys offered to all loyalty accounts; risk-based step-up for payment changes
Recovery
Same assurance as enrollment; no help desk resets on caller ID alone

Key takeaways

  • Access life cycle for every asset type (information, systems, devices, facilities, applications, services, identities): provision, control, review, revoke. HR events drive it; revoke privileged and non-human access too.
  • Groups and roles make access consistent and reviewable; avoid role explosion; build in separation of duties. AAA: authentication, authorization, accounting (RADIUS, TACACS+).
  • Proofing (SP 800-63A-4): IAL1-3; recovery must match enrollment assurance; devices and services need registered identities and owners.
  • Authentication (SP 800-63B-4): AAL1-3; AAL3 needs a non-exportable key and phishing resistance. Passwordless: FIDO2, WebAuthn, passkeys (device-bound vs synced); biometrics unlock an authenticator; FRR, FAR, CER.
  • Sessions: random tokens, TLS, timeouts, reauthentication; stolen cookies bypass MFA. SSO centralizes control and risk; FIM crosses organizations.
  • Credentials: password vaults, secrets managers, PAM. JIT: time-limited, approved privilege with no standing admin rights.
