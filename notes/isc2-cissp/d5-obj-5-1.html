Overview

Objective 5.1

5.1 Manage physical and logical access to assets (e.g., provision, control, review, revoke). Sub-topics: information; systems; devices; facilities; applications; services; identities.

The basics are in CC 3.1; authentication design is on the 5.2 page; the provisioning life cycle in detail on the 5.5 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It has 4,000 workforce identities, 9,000 service accounts and API keys, 12 million customer accounts, and a growing number of AI agents.

Controlling access to assets

Objective 5.1Running one access life cycle (provision, control, review, revoke) across every kind of asset, physical and logical.

The same four steps apply whatever the asset:

  1. ProvisionGrant access on an approved request, by role, with least privilege. HR events drive workforce access ("the HR/personnel system is the sole driver of identity creation and termination", in the federal ILM playbook).
  2. ControlEnforce it at the asset: authentication, authorization, physical locks and badges, network controls, and logging.
  3. ReviewOwners recertify access regularly (CISA's CPG 2.0 suggests quarterly for permissions and role assignments), removing what is no longer needed.
  4. RevokeRemove access promptly on termination, transfer or when it is no longer justified (SP 800-53 AC-2 and PS-4), including credentials, tokens, keys and badges.

What changes from asset to asset is who owns the decision and how it is enforced:

  • Information: the data owner approves access by classification and need-to-know; enforced by permissions, encryption and DLP.
  • Systems: administrators' and users' accounts on servers and platforms, with privileged access separated and monitored.
  • Devices: only enrolled, managed and healthy devices may connect (NAC, device certificates, MDM).
  • Facilities: badges, zones and visitor processes, aligned with logical access so a leaver's badge and accounts are removed together (CSF 2.0 PR.AA-06).
  • Applications: roles inside each application, ideally driven centrally through SSO and automated provisioning (SCIM).
  • Services: cloud and SaaS services and APIs, whose access lives in each provider's IAM and needs the same reviews.
  • Identities: the identities themselves are assets, including service accounts, workload identities and AI agents, each with an accountable owner.

Orphaned and forgotten access. A CISA advisory (AA24-046A) described attackers using a former employee's administrator account that "was not disabled immediately following the employee's departure". Revocation must cover every asset type, and especially privileged and non-human accounts, which are easily missed.

Physical and logical access together

Objective 5.1Managing building and system access as one process, so neither leaves a gap.

Physical and logical access protect the same assets and should follow the same life cycle. Badge systems, door controllers and visitor systems are part of the identity estate: a leaver whose accounts are disabled but whose badge still works can walk into the server room, and a contractor with a badge but no accounts can still plug into a network port.

  • One source of truth: HR (and the contractor management system) drives both badge and account provisioning.
  • Zone-based authorization: physical zones (public, staff, restricted, data center) are granted by role, like logical permissions, and reviewed together.
  • Revocation together: termination disables accounts, badges, tokens and keys in one workflow; lost badges are reported and disabled like lost credentials.
  • Correlation: physical and logical logs combined can spot misuse, such as a remote VPN login while the same person's badge is used on site.

CSF 2.0 includes physical access management alongside logical access in its identity outcomes (PR.AA-06), and SP 800-53 PE-2 and PE-3 cover authorizations and enforcement of physical access (objective 7.14).

Provisioning and revoking at scale

Objective 5.1Automating the access life cycle so it stays correct for thousands of identities and assets.

  • Birthright access: a standard set granted automatically by role, location and employment type on day one (email, intranet, the store's till role).
  • Requested access: anything beyond birthright goes through a request, approval by the asset or data owner, and time limits where possible.
  • Automation: identity governance tools and SCIM provisioning create, change and remove accounts in connected applications from HR events, which is faster and more accurate than tickets.
  • Disconnected systems: applications that cannot be connected still need provisioning and revocation, through tracked tickets and periodic reconciliation of their account lists against HR.
  • Expiry by default: contractor, seasonal and project access is created with an end date.

Revocation must cover more than accounts: active sessions and refresh tokens, API keys, certificates, SSH keys, shared secrets the person knew, device enrollments, and physical items. Disabling an account in the directory does not end a session already established in a SaaS application unless tokens are revoked too.

Reviewing access to every asset type

Objective 5.1Confirming regularly that access to information, systems, devices, facilities, applications and services is still justified.

Access reviews (certifications) ask each owner to confirm who should keep access to what they own. Effective reviews are risk-based and specific:

  • Information and applications: data and application owners review users and roles, more often for sensitive data (card data, payroll).
  • Systems and privileged access: administrators and privileged groups reviewed most often; standing privilege questioned every time.
  • Devices: managed device inventories reconciled with users; unknown or unmanaged devices removed from access.
  • Facilities: badge access to restricted zones reviewed by zone owners.
  • Services and identities: service accounts, API keys and AI agents reviewed by their owners for need, permissions and secret age (objective 5.5).

Reviews fail when reviewers rubber-stamp long lists of cryptic entitlements. Provide meaningful descriptions, highlight what changed and what is risky (dormant accounts, toxic combinations that break separation of duties), and revoke automatically anything not confirmed.

Access for services and non-human identities

Objective 5.1Applying the access life cycle to APIs, workloads and AI agents as well as people.

Non-human identities (service accounts, workload identities, API clients, bots and AI agents) often outnumber people and are harder to see. They need the same four steps: provision with an owner and purpose, control with least privilege and short-lived credentials, review regularly, and revoke when the workload or integration ends. Cloud platforms make this easier with workload identities that receive short-lived tokens automatically instead of stored keys.

AI agents add a twist: they act on behalf of users or the organization, so their access should be scoped to the task, high-impact actions should require human approval, and their actions should be logged against both the agent's identity and the user it serves. The federal Digital Worker Identity Playbook adds an accountable sponsor for each automated "digital worker" and regular recertification.

Access control policy and owner duties

Objective 5.1Writing the rules for granting access and making owners responsible for applying them.

SP 800-53 AC-1 requires an access control policy and procedures; AC-2 adds the account management rules. A workable policy states:

  • Principles: least privilege, need-to-know, separation of duties, deny by default.
  • Who approves: the information or system owner for their assets, managers for their staff's need, and security for privileged access; nobody approves their own access.
  • How access is requested: through one tracked process with a business reason, never by email to an administrator.
  • Emergency access: a break-glass route with strong controls, logging and after-the-fact review.
  • Review and revocation: frequency by risk, triggers (transfer, termination, inactivity), and deadlines.
  • Logging: grants, changes, removals and their approvals recorded for audit.

Owners carry the decisions: they define who should have access, approve requests, certify access in reviews, and accept the risk of exceptions. Security designs the process and tooling, IT implements it, and audit checks that it works. At the retail group, each of 150 applications has a named owner whose quarterly certification of privileged and sensitive roles is tracked on the security scorecard.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the leaver's badge
Situation
A dismissed employee's accounts were disabled at once, but their badge still opened the warehouse door the next morning.
Best response
Link badge revocation to the HR termination event so accounts, badges and tokens are revoked together, and review badge lists against HR.
Why
Physical and logical access must follow one life cycle; revocation must cover every asset type.
Scenario 2: the still-active session
Situation
A contractor's directory account was disabled, but they kept using a SaaS app for two days through an existing session.
Best response
Revoke sessions and refresh tokens at the identity provider and in the SaaS app as part of offboarding, and shorten session lifetimes.
Why
Disabling an account does not end sessions already established elsewhere.
Scenario 3: rubber-stamp reviews
Situation
Managers approve 100% of access in quarterly reviews within minutes.
Best response
Make reviews risk-based (privileged and sensitive access first), show meaningful descriptions and changes, flag dormant and conflicting access, and auto-revoke anything not confirmed.
Why
Reviews only work if reviewers can understand and are prompted to challenge access.
Scenario 4: the orphaned API key
Situation
An API key for a decommissioned integration still has write access to the order database.
Best response
Revoke it, find other keys without active owners, and require an owner and expiry for every non-human credential.
Why
Non-human identities need the same provision, control, review and revoke cycle as people.

Key takeaways

  • Life cycle for every asset type: provision, control, review, revoke; HR events drive it.
  • Physical and logical together: one source of truth, zone-based authorization, joint revocation, correlated logs (CSF PR.AA-06, PE-2, PE-3).
  • At scale: birthright and requested access, SCIM automation, reconciliation of disconnected systems, expiry by default; revoke sessions, tokens, keys and badges.
  • Reviews: risk-based by asset type, meaningful entitlements, flags for dormant and toxic access, auto-revocation.
  • Non-human identities: owners, least privilege, short-lived credentials, reviews and revocation; AI agents scoped with human approval for high-impact actions.
