Overview

Objective 7.5

7.5 Apply resource protection. Sub-topics: media management; media protection techniques (archiving, encryption, disposal, reuse); data at rest, data in transit and data in use.

Handling, retention and sanitization of data are on the 2.2 page and data controls on the 2.6 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. Backups of store and payment systems go to tape offsite and to immutable cloud storage.

Media management and protection

Objective 7.5Tracking and protecting storage media from issue to disposal.

Media management covers every medium that holds organizational data: disks and SSDs, backup tapes, USB drives, mobile devices, paper, and cloud storage volumes and snapshots. The SP 800-53 MP family sets the controls: access (MP-2), marking (MP-3), storage (MP-4), transport (MP-5), sanitization (MP-6) and use (MP-7, such as restricting or blocking removable media).

  • Inventory and tracking: label media with their classification, log check-out and return, account for backup tapes in transit and at offsite storage.
  • Archiving: long-term storage under the retention schedule, in a format and on media that will still be readable when needed, protected against loss and environmental damage, with periodic test reads.
  • Encryption: encrypt removable media, backups and archives, with keys managed separately so a lost tape or drive is not a breach.
  • Disposal and reuse: sanitize by the method the classification and destination require (NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2: clear for reuse inside the organization, purge or destroy for media leaving it), with a record for each item (objective 2.2).

The retail group blocks USB storage on all store and office devices except for a few approved, encrypted drives, and its backup provider collects tapes in locked cases with each case's serial number logged at both ends.

Protecting data in each state

Objective 7.5Matching protection to whether data is stored, moving or being processed.

  • At rest: full-disk, database and file encryption, access control, backups protected at the same level as the original, and integrity protection such as write-once storage for logs and backups.
  • In transit: TLS, IPsec or SSH on every network path, including internal ones; certificate validation; no fallback to cleartext protocols.
  • In use: access control and least privilege in applications, masking sensitive fields on screen, endpoint DLP, memory protection, and confidential computing (trusted execution environments) for the most sensitive processing (objective 3.4).

Operations teams own much of this day to day: checking that encryption is actually on, that certificates are valid, that backups are encrypted and restorable, and that sensitive data has not appeared somewhere it should not be (discovery scans).

Protecting backups

Objective 7.5Making sure backups survive the events they are meant to recover from.

Ransomware groups look for backups first, so backups are a resource to protect, not just a copy.

  • Several copies in separate places: the widely used 3-2-1 practice keeps three copies on two kinds of media with one offsite; at least one copy should be offline or immutable (write-once, with a retention lock that administrators cannot shorten).
  • Separate administration: backup systems use separate accounts, MFA and ideally a separate identity domain, so compromising the main directory does not give control of backups.
  • Encryption: backups are encrypted, with keys held outside the system being backed up and themselves backed up.
  • Classification: backups carry the classification of the data in them and are handled, retained and destroyed accordingly.
  • Restore testing: regular restores of whole systems, including identity services, prove the backups are usable and measure recovery time (objective 6.3).
  • Monitoring: alerts on failed jobs, deleted backups, shortened retention and unusual changes to backup policies.

Protecting cloud storage and snapshots

Objective 7.5Applying media protection to storage you never physically see.

  • Block public access at the account or organization level, and allow exceptions only through review.
  • Snapshots and images: disk snapshots and machine images contain full copies of data; restrict who can create, copy and share them, and check that none are shared publicly or with unknown accounts.
  • Encryption and keys: encrypt volumes, objects and snapshots, using customer-managed keys where control or separation of duties requires it; deleting or disabling a key can make data unrecoverable (crypto-shredding), which is both a disposal method and a risk.
  • Lifecycle rules: expire objects and snapshots under the retention schedule so old copies do not accumulate.
  • Data discovery: scan storage for sensitive data in unexpected places, such as card numbers in log buckets.
  • Disposal: you cannot sanitize the provider's disks, so rely on encryption and key destruction, provider attestations of media sanitization, and contract terms (objective 2.2).

At the retail group, a discovery scan found a test bucket holding a copy of the loyalty database; it was deleted, and the pipeline now blocks production data from being copied into test accounts without masking.

Removable media and mobile devices

Objective 7.5Controlling the small media and devices that most often lose data.

  • Restrict by default: block USB mass storage through device control, and allow only approved, encrypted devices for named users with a business need (MP-7).
  • Encrypt: approved removable drives use hardware or software encryption with a password or key, so loss does not expose data.
  • Scan: media coming in from outside is scanned for malware, ideally at a dedicated kiosk, before use on internal systems; this matters most for isolated and OT networks.
  • Mobile devices: managed through mobile device management (MDM): required passcode and encryption, separation of work and personal data, remote wipe, and blocking of unmanaged devices from corporate data.
  • Paper and printouts: print release at the printer, locked shred bins, and clean desk rules for sensitive material.
  • Found media: staff are trained never to plug in unknown USB devices, which attackers sometimes leave deliberately in car parks or post to targets.

Archiving for the long term

Objective 7.5Keeping archived data readable, intact and findable for as long as it must be kept.

  • Retention-driven: archive under the retention schedule, and delete when the period ends unless a legal hold applies.
  • Readable formats: prefer open or widely supported formats, and keep the software or conversion tools needed to read proprietary ones.
  • Media refresh: tapes and disks degrade and drives become obsolete; migrate archives to new media on a planned cycle and test reads periodically.
  • Integrity: store hashes with archived data and verify them on migration and retrieval; write-once storage prevents alteration.
  • Keys: encrypted archives are only as durable as their keys; keep keys for as long as the data, with escrow or backup, and record which key protects which archive.
  • Catalog: an index of what is archived, where, from which system and date, so data can be found for legal requests and restored without searching every tape.

An archive that cannot be read, found or trusted fails both the business and legal obligations it exists for.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the objective to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the lost tape
Situation
A backup tape is missing after transport to the offsite store.
Best response
Follow the incident process; if the tape was encrypted with keys held separately, the breach risk is low, but still investigate and document.
Why
Encrypting removable media and backups means a lost item is not a data breach.
Scenario 2: the ransomware deletes backups
Situation
Attackers with domain administrator rights deleted the online backups before encrypting servers.
Best response
Keep immutable or offline copies and administer backups with separate accounts and MFA outside the main directory.
Why
Backups must be protected from the same attacker who compromises production.
Scenario 3: drives for donation
Situation
Old laptops with SSDs are to be donated to a charity.
Best response
Purge (for example, cryptographic erase or the drive's sanitize command) or destroy, verify, and keep a sanitization record for each device.
Why
Media leaving the organization needs purge or destroy under SP 800-88, with records.
Scenario 4: the public snapshot
Situation
A cloud disk snapshot of a database server was shared publicly by mistake.
Best response
Remove sharing immediately, treat it as a possible breach (check access logs), and enforce blocking of public sharing at the organization level.
Why
Snapshots are media; cloud guardrails stop accidental exposure.

Key takeaways

  • Media (MP family): label, track, store and transport securely, restrict removable media, archive readably, encrypt, sanitize to SP 800-88 Rev. 2 with records.
  • Data states: at rest (encryption, access control, WORM), in transit (TLS, IPsec, SSH everywhere), in use (least privilege, masking, DLP, confidential computing).
  • Backups: several copies, one offline or immutable, separate administration, encrypted, classified, restore-tested, monitored.
  • Cloud storage: block public access, control snapshots, encrypt with managed keys, lifecycle rules, discovery scans; disposal through crypto-shredding and attestations.
