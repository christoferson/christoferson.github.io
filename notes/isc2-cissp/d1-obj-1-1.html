Overview

Objectives 1.1 and 1.2

1.1 Understand, adhere to, and promote professional ethics. 1.2 Understand and apply security concepts.

CISSP is aimed at people who lead and design security programs, so it starts with the obligations that come with that trust, and with the vocabulary every later domain uses. These topics overlap with the ISC2 CC guide: if they are new to you, read CC 1.1 (the CIA triad, AAA and non-repudiation in depth) and 1.5 (the code's canons and complaints) first. This page adds what CISSP expects on top. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. A retail group with about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services for customers and staff. You are its security architect, reporting to the CISO.

The ISC2 Code of Ethics

Objective 1.1Applying the ISC2 code that every certified member and Associate must follow, and knowing how breaches of it are handled.

The outline calls it the "ISC2 Code of Professional Ethics"; ISC2's own website calls the same document the ISC2 Code of Ethics. Following it is a condition of certification for every member and Associate of ISC2. It has a preamble and four canons, which in summary (not ISC2's wording) say:

  1. Society firstProtect society, the common good, public trust and infrastructure.
  2. IntegrityAct honorably, honestly, justly, responsibly and legally.
  3. PrincipalsGive diligent and competent service to principals, the people you work for under an employer or contractor relationship.
  4. The professionAdvance and protect the profession.

The order matters when duties conflict: an employer's instruction does not outrank the duty to society or to act legally. Members who see another member breach the code are expected to report it through ISC2's complaint process; complaints are written and sworn, and the standing to complain depends on the canon (anyone for the first two, principals for the third, other professionals bound by a code for the fourth). A peer review can lead to loss of certification. The full wording is on isc2.org/ethics.

Two different ISC2 codes. In February 2026 ISC2 launched a separate Code of Professional Conduct, which "builds upon the existing ISC2 Code of Ethics" with practical guidance on conflicts of interest, confidentiality and emerging technologies such as AI. It is voluntary. The Code of Ethics, which the exam calls the Code of Professional Ethics, is the one that is mandatory and enforced.

Organizational codes of ethics

Objective 1.1Following and promoting an employer's code of ethics, and knowing where such codes come from and what they usually require.

Most organizations publish their own code of ethics or code of conduct, and a CISSP is expected to follow it, help write and enforce it, and set an example. Some are required by law. US public companies must disclose under SEC Regulation S-K Item 406 (from the Sarbanes-Oxley Act) whether they have a code of ethics for their senior executive and financial officers, defined as "written standards that are reasonably designed to deter wrongdoing and to promote" honest conduct including handling conflicts of interest, accurate disclosure, compliance with laws, "prompt internal reporting of violations" and "accountability for adherence to the code."

Older codes still appear in study material. The Internet Activities Board's RFC 1087, "Ethics and the Internet" (1989), called it unethical to seek unauthorized access to Internet resources, disrupt its intended use, waste resources, destroy the integrity of computer-based information, or compromise users' privacy. Other professional bodies, such as ISACA, publish their own codes for their members.

A typical security-related section of the retail group's code of conduct requires staff to:

  • use systems and data only for authorized business purposes, and report suspected misuse without fear of retaliation;
  • declare conflicts of interest, such as a family link to a security vendor under evaluation;
  • never accept gifts that could influence a purchasing decision;
  • protect customer and colleague data, and follow the AI use policy.

When the codes pull in different directions. If a manager asks you to hide a breach from customers to protect the share price, both codes point the same way: the ISC2 canons put society and acting legally before service to principals, and the company's own code requires reporting violations. Raise it internally first through the reporting channel, and know your legal obligations to report (objective 1.4).

Ethics in AI

Objective 1.1Applying recognized principles for the ethical use of AI, and knowing the main frameworks and laws behind them.

The 2027 outline adds ethics in AI to this objective. Security leaders are often the people asked whether an AI system is safe to deploy, so they need the shared principles. The main international frameworks agree on the core ideas:

  • OECD AI Principles (2019, revised May 2024, 47 adherents): inclusive growth and well-being; human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; and accountability.
  • UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021, adopted by all 193 member states), which UNESCO calls "the first global standard on AI ethics": principles including proportionality and do no harm, privacy, human oversight and determination, transparency and explainability, responsibility and accountability, and fairness and non-discrimination.
  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework: trustworthy AI is valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed. It is voluntary.
  • Standards: IEEE 7000-2021 sets a process for addressing ethical concerns during system design; ISO/IEC 42001 is the AI management system standard.

Law is catching up unevenly. The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) bans certain AI practices from February 2, 2025, and rules for general-purpose AI models apply from August 2, 2025. In July 2026 an EU "digital omnibus" regulation (2026/1744) delayed the obligations for high-risk systems to December 2, 2027 (systems such as those used in hiring or education) and August 2, 2028 (AI built into regulated products), and added a new ban on AI systems that create non-consensual sexual images, from December 2, 2026. In the US, federal policy since 2025 favors promoting AI innovation over new regulation, and the NIST framework remains voluntary.

At the retail group, the AI review board (security, privacy, legal and the business) checks each proposed AI use against these principles. A tool that ranked job applicants was redesigned so a person makes every decision and candidates can ask for an explanation, because hiring is a high-risk use under the EU act and a fairness concern everywhere.

Watch the dates. Many sources still say the EU AI Act's high-risk rules apply from August 2, 2026. That changed in 2026: check the current timeline on the European Commission's site before relying on any date.

The five pillars

Objective 1.2Applying the five core security properties, and telling authenticity, integrity and non-repudiation apart.

The CISSP outline names five properties, which ISC2 calls the five pillars of information security. The first three are the CIA triad, covered with NIST definitions in CC 1.1; the other two are where CISSP questions often probe:

PillarAssures thatTypical controls
ConfidentialityInformation is disclosed only to those authorizedAccess control, encryption, classification
IntegrityInformation and systems are not changed without authorizationHashes, change control, integrity monitoring
AvailabilitySystems and data are usable when neededRedundancy, backups, DDoS protection
AuthenticityData or a message is genuine and came from its claimed sourceMessage authentication codes, signatures, strong authentication
Non-repudiationThe originator cannot credibly deny an actionDigital signatures with protected private keys, tamper-evident logs

NIST defines authenticity in two senses: "the property that data originated from its purported source" (its cryptography and identity guides), and, in its risk and control guides, "the property of being genuine and being able to be verified and trusted; confidence in the validity of a transmission". Integrity says the content has not changed; authenticity says who it came from; non-repudiation adds proof that a third party can check, so the sender cannot deny it.

A MAC gives authenticity but not non-repudiation. A message authentication code uses a key shared by sender and receiver, so it proves the message came from one of them and was not altered, but either could have created it. Only an asymmetric digital signature, made with a private key that only the sender holds, supports non-repudiation (CC 1.1). In US law the FISMA definition of integrity includes "non-repudiation and authenticity", while the CISSP outline lists them as separate pillars.

At the retail group, payment messages between stores and the processing center carry MACs (authenticity and integrity within the company), while instructions to the bank to release large settlements are digitally signed by named officers (non-repudiation).

Key takeaways

  • The ISC2 Code of Ethics (the outline's "Code of Professional Ethics") is mandatory: four canons in order of priority (society; honorable and legal conduct; principals; the profession). Breaches are reported through a written complaint and peer review. The 2026 Code of Professional Conduct is separate and voluntary.
  • Organizational codes add the employer's rules (conflicts of interest, gifts, reporting misuse, data protection). SEC Item 406 requires public companies to disclose a code for senior financial officers; RFC 1087 is the classic Internet ethics statement. When codes conflict, society and the law come first.
  • AI ethics: OECD, UNESCO and the NIST AI RMF share fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, safety and human oversight. EU AI Act: bans from 2025; high-risk rules delayed in 2026 to December 2027 and August 2028.
  • Five pillars: confidentiality, integrity, availability, authenticity (genuine, from its claimed source) and non-repudiation (provable to a third party). MACs give authenticity, not non-repudiation; digital signatures give both.
