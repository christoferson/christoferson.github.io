Overview

Objective 1.1

1.1 Understand, adhere to, and promote professional ethics. Sub-topics: the ISC2 Code of Professional Ethics, organizational codes of ethics, and ethics in AI.

The objective has three verbs. Understand the codes, adhere to them in your own conduct, and promote them, which means helping write, teach and enforce ethical rules in the organization. If the canons and complaint process are new to you, CC 1.5 covers the basics; this page goes further. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. A retail group with about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services for customers and staff. You are its security architect, reporting to the CISO.

Why ethics comes first

Objective 1.1Understanding why the exam treats ethics as the foundation for every other domain.

Security professionals hold unusual power: administrator access to every system, knowledge of every weakness, sight of investigations and personal data, and influence over what leadership is told about risk. Customers, colleagues and the public cannot check how that power is used, so they rely on the profession's ethics. A security leader who quietly reads executives' email, downplays a breach to protect a bonus, or recommends a vendor because a friend works there does damage that no technical control prevents.

Ethics also shapes how CISSP questions are answered across all eight domains. Many "what should you do first" questions have a technically clever option and an option that protects people, follows the law and informs the right decision-maker; the second is almost always the intended answer. The canons give a consistent way to choose.

Three related ideas often appear together:

  • Ethics: principles of right conduct, which can ask more than the law requires.
  • Law: rules enforced by the state; acting legally is a minimum, not the whole of ethical behavior. ISC2's 2026 decision-making guide specifically warns against the "it's not illegal" justification.
  • Policy: the organization's own rules, which must themselves be lawful and ethical (objective 1.6 covers policy documents).

The ISC2 Code of Ethics

Objective 1.1Knowing what the ISC2 code is called, who it binds, and how it is structured.

The exam outline calls it the "ISC2 Code of Professional Ethics"; ISC2's own website calls the same document the ISC2 Code of Ethics. Treat the two names as one document. Every certified member and every Associate of ISC2 must follow it, and candidates agree to abide by it before sitting the exam (ISC2 candidate policy). Strict adherence is a condition of certification, and a knowing violation can lead to a peer review panel and the loss of certification.

The code is deliberately short and high level. It does not list rules for every situation and does not replace judgment. It has two parts:

  • The preamble, which in summary says that the safety and welfare of society, the common good, and duties to principals and to each other require members to hold, and be seen to hold, the highest ethical standards.
  • Four canons, the mandatory duties, summarized below in our own words. The full wording is on isc2.org/ethics; ISC2 does not permit its text to be reproduced, so read it there.
  1. Canon I: SocietyProtect society, the common good, the necessary public trust and confidence, and the infrastructure.
  2. Canon II: IntegrityAct honorably, honestly, justly, responsibly and legally.
  3. Canon III: PrincipalsProvide diligent and competent service to principals, the people you work for under an employer or contractor relationship.
  4. Canon IV: The professionAdvance and protect the profession.

The order is the priority. The canons are listed from the widest duty (society) to the narrowest (the profession). Exam questions use this order to resolve conflicts: an employer's instruction (Canon III) never justifies harming the public or acting illegally (Canons I and II). ISC2's current page does not spell out an "earlier canon wins" rule in those words, but the ordering and the preamble's emphasis on society are why study guides and exam questions treat it that way.

The four canons in practice

Objective 1.1Translating each canon into concrete behavior a security professional can be held to.

Canon I, society. Your duty extends beyond your employer to the people affected by its systems: customers whose data it holds, the public who depend on its services, and shared infrastructure. In practice:

  • Do not hide risks to the public, such as a flaw that exposes customers' card data, even if disclosure is embarrassing.
  • Do not build or tolerate capabilities designed to harm people, such as covert surveillance of customers beyond what the law and privacy notices allow.
  • Support public trust in security: share threat information through appropriate channels, and disclose vulnerabilities in other vendors' products responsibly (objective 6.4).

Canon II, integrity. Be honest about your own work and its limits, and act within the law:

  • Report test results and risks accurately; do not soften findings because the sponsor is senior.
  • Claim only qualifications and experience you have; do not let others take credit for your work, or take credit for theirs.
  • Stay inside your authorization: an approved penetration test does not authorize reading HR files you happen to reach.
  • Keep promises of confidentiality, and avoid even the appearance of impropriety, such as accepting gifts from vendors during a tender.

Canon III, principals. Serve those who employ or contract you diligently and competently:

  • Give your honest professional opinion, including unwelcome news; a principal who is told only what they want to hear is not being served.
  • Work within your competence: decline or seek help with tasks you are not qualified for, such as a forensic investigation that may go to court.
  • Protect the principal's confidential information, during and after the engagement.
  • Declare conflicts of interest, such as owning shares in a vendor you are evaluating.

Canon IV, the profession. Help the profession earn and keep trust:

  • Keep your knowledge current (continuing professional education is a condition of keeping the certification).
  • Mentor and share knowledge, and support only those who are qualified and ethical; do not recommend or endorse people you know to be unfit.
  • Do not bring the profession into disrepute, for example by misusing access or by public conduct that undermines trust in security professionals.
  • Report breaches of the code by other members (next section).

Reporting breaches and the complaint process

Objective 1.1Knowing when you must report another member, who can file a complaint, and what happens next.

Members who observe another member breaching the code are expected to report it through ISC2's ethics complaint procedure; failing to do so can itself be a breach of the code under Canon IV. The procedure, in summary:

  1. StandingOnly someone who claims to be injured by the conduct may complain, and who that can be depends on the canon: anyone in the public for Canons I and II; principals (employers or contractors) for Canon III; other certified or licensed professionals who also subscribe to a code of ethics for Canon IV.
  2. Written complaintA sworn, written affidavit that identifies the member and the canon breached, with supporting evidence.
  3. ReviewISC2's ethics committee does not investigate on its own; it acts on the evidence provided, and only if the complaint shows a case on its face. The respondent is notified and given time to reply, and is given the benefit of the doubt.
  4. DecisionISC2's board decides, and its decision is final. The sanction named in the procedure is revocation of certification.

Report through the right channel. A breach of the ISC2 code by a colleague may also break the employer's code or the law. Report it internally through the organization's channel (and to authorities where the law requires) as well as to ISC2; one does not replace the other. Do not investigate it yourself by accessing their accounts without authorization.

The Code of Professional Conduct

Objective 1.1Telling the voluntary 2026 conduct guidance apart from the mandatory code.

In February 2026 ISC2 launched a separate Code of Professional Conduct, developed with input from about 1,400 professionals. ISC2 says it "builds upon the existing ISC2 Code of Ethics". It applies to the whole profession, not only members, and it is voluntary. It gives practical guidance in two parts:

  • Ethics: integrity, including conflicts of interest and anti-bribery; confidentiality; respect for laws, including your own organization's code of conduct; and public safety, including emerging technologies such as AI.
  • Professional conduct: responsibility and accountability, with protecting human life as the primary concern; collaboration; competence; and raising concerns through hotlines, HR or compliance functions.

ISC2 followed it in April 2026 with an ethical decision-making guide: nine steps in three phases (understand the situation; evaluate options and decide; act and learn from the outcome).

Two ISC2 codes, one mandatory. If a question asks what certified members are required to follow, or what can lead to loss of certification, the answer is the Code of Ethics (the outline's Code of Professional Ethics). The Code of Professional Conduct is guidance.

Organizational codes of ethics

Objective 1.1Following, shaping and promoting the employer's code of ethics, and knowing where such codes come from.

Most organizations publish a code of ethics or code of conduct that applies to all staff. A CISSP is expected to follow it, to help write the parts on information use, security and AI, to train people on it, and to set an example. Some codes are required or shaped by law and by other bodies:

  • Securities law: US public companies must disclose under SEC Regulation S-K Item 406 (from section 406 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, adopted in 2003) whether they have a code of ethics for their principal executive, financial and accounting officers, and explain why not if they do not. Such a code is defined as "written standards that are reasonably designed to deter wrongdoing and to promote" honest and ethical conduct including handling conflicts of interest; "full, fair, accurate, timely, and understandable" disclosure; compliance with laws; "prompt internal reporting of violations"; and "accountability for adherence to the code."
  • Professional bodies: ISACA's Code of Professional Ethics binds its certification holders (including duties of objectivity, due diligence and professional care); ACM and IEEE publish codes for computing and engineering professionals. A person may be bound by several codes at once.
  • Classic Internet ethics: the Internet Activities Board's RFC 1087, "Ethics and the Internet" (1989), called it unethical and unacceptable to seek unauthorized access to Internet resources, disrupt the Internet's intended use, waste resources (people, capacity, computers), destroy the integrity of computer-based information, or compromise users' privacy, and called negligence in Internet-wide experiments irresponsible. Older study material still quotes it.

A good organizational code is specific enough to act on, applies to everyone including executives, offers a safe channel to report concerns without retaliation, and is backed by consequences. Security-related sections typically cover:

Code of conduct: information and technology section (retail group)
Acceptable use
Systems and data are used only for authorized business purposes; personal use within stated limits
Confidentiality
Customer, colleague and company information is protected and shared only as needed
Monitoring
Staff are told what is monitored and why; privileged users may not use their access to look at colleagues' data out of curiosity
Conflicts of interest
Declared in writing, such as a family link to a vendor under evaluation
Gifts and hospitality
Nothing that could influence a purchasing or audit decision; gifts above a set value declared
AI use
Only approved AI tools; no restricted data in public services; people remain accountable for AI-assisted decisions
Speaking up
Concerns reported to a manager, the ethics hotline or compliance, with protection from retaliation

Promoting the code is part of the security role: include it in onboarding and annual awareness training, make ethics part of privileged access agreements (administrators sign that they will not misuse access), and lead by example, for instance by declaring your own conflicts.

Ethics in AI

Objective 1.1Applying recognized principles for the ethical design and use of AI, and knowing the frameworks and laws behind them.

The 2027 outline adds ethics in AI to this objective. Security leaders are often asked whether an AI system is safe and appropriate to deploy, and AI raises ethical questions that classic security does not: decisions that are hard to explain, bias learned from data, and harm at scale. The main frameworks agree on the core principles:

  • OECD AI Principles (2019, revised May 2024; 47 adherents): inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being; human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; and accountability.
  • UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021, adopted by all 193 member states), which UNESCO calls "the first global standard on AI ethics": ten principles including proportionality and do no harm, safety and security, privacy and data protection, responsibility and accountability, transparency and explainability, human oversight and determination, and fairness and non-discrimination.
  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, 2023, being revised): trustworthy AI is valid and reliable; safe; secure and resilient; accountable and transparent; explainable and interpretable; privacy-enhanced; and fair, with harmful bias managed. NIST notes that "accountability presupposes transparency", and its bias guidance (SP 1270) describes systemic, computational and statistical, and human-cognitive bias. The framework is voluntary.
  • Standards: IEEE 7000-2021 sets a process for identifying ethical values and building them into system requirements and design; ISO/IEC 42001:2023 is the certifiable AI management system standard.

Law is catching up unevenly. The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) entered into force on August 1, 2024; banned practices and AI literacy obligations apply from February 2, 2025, and rules for general-purpose AI models from August 2, 2025. In July 2026 the EU's "digital omnibus" on AI (Regulation 2026/1744) delayed the high-risk obligations to December 2, 2027 (stand-alone high-risk uses such as hiring, education and biometrics) and August 2, 2028 (AI built into regulated products), and added a ban on systems that generate non-consensual intimate images or child sexual abuse material, from December 2, 2026. In the US, federal policy since 2025 favors promoting AI innovation over new regulation, and the NIST framework remains voluntary.

What these principles mean for a security leader in practice:

  • Transparency: people are told when they are dealing with AI, and decisions that affect them can be explained.
  • Human oversight: a person can review, override or stop consequential decisions; "the model decided" is never an excuse.
  • Fairness: training data and outputs are tested for bias against protected groups before and after deployment.
  • Privacy: personal data used for training has a lawful basis and is minimized; outputs do not leak it.
  • Security and safety: models are protected against poisoning, manipulation and misuse (objective 3.7).
  • Accountability: every AI system has a named owner, and its use is recorded and reviewable.

At the retail group, an AI review board (security, privacy, legal, HR and the business) assesses each proposed AI use. A tool that ranked job applicants was redesigned so that a person makes every decision and candidates can ask for an explanation, because hiring is a high-risk use under the EU act and a fairness concern everywhere. A proposal to analyze store CCTV for "suspicious behavior" was rejected as disproportionate.

Watch the dates. Many sources still say the EU AI Act's high-risk rules apply from August 2, 2026. That changed in July 2026. Check the European Commission's current timeline before relying on any date.

Resolving ethical conflicts

Objective 1.1Working through a conflict between duties in a defensible order.

Real dilemmas rarely announce themselves. A structured approach, consistent with ISC2's decision-making guide, keeps the response defensible:

  1. Establish the factsWhat exactly is being asked or has happened, and who is affected? Avoid acting on rumor.
  2. Identify the dutiesWhich canons, laws, contracts and organizational rules apply, and to whom do you owe what?
  3. Rank themSafety of people and the law first; then honesty; then service to principals; then the profession.
  4. Consider the optionsIncluding raising it with the person, escalating internally, using the ethics hotline, seeking legal advice, or declining the task.
  5. Act through proper channelsEscalate within the organization first unless that would be unlawful, futile or dangerous; report externally where the law requires.
  6. Document and learnRecord what you were asked, what you did and why; feed lessons into policy and training.

Exam pattern. When an option has you acting alone and secretly (deleting data, leaking to the press, hacking back), it is almost always wrong. When an option has you protecting people, following the law, and informing the right decision-maker through proper channels, it is almost always right.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying the canons and codes to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the delayed breach notice
Situation
You confirm that customer card data was stolen. The CFO asks you to hold the incident report until after next week's earnings call.
Best response
Explain the legal and contractual notification duties, decline to delay them, and escalate through the incident process to the CISO, legal counsel and, if needed, the audit committee.
Why
Canons I and II (society, acting legally) outrank Canon III (the CFO as principal). The company's own code requires prompt reporting of violations. Going straight to the press would skip proper channels.
Scenario 2: the friendly vendor
Situation
You lead the evaluation of three endpoint security products. One vendor's account manager is your former colleague and offers you conference tickets.
Best response
Decline the gift, declare the relationship in writing to your manager, and let the evaluation proceed with documented criteria, or step back from scoring that vendor.
Why
Canon II (honor, avoiding the appearance of impropriety) and Canon III (honest service to the principal), plus the organizational code on gifts and conflicts. Simply staying silent because you would be fair anyway does not meet the standard.
Scenario 3: the colleague's side business
Situation
You learn that a fellow ISC2-certified colleague runs paid "penetration tests" against small businesses without their written authorization.
Best response
Report it through your organization's channel if it involves company resources, and file a complaint through ISC2's ethics procedure, since unauthorized testing may be illegal and harms public trust in the profession.
Why
Members are expected to report breaches by other members (Canon IV), and the conduct engages Canons I and II. Confronting the colleague alone, or gathering evidence by accessing their systems, is not appropriate.

Key takeaways

  • The ISC2 Code of Ethics (the outline's "Code of Professional Ethics") binds every member and Associate: a preamble and four canons in priority order (society; honorable, honest and legal conduct; diligent, competent service to principals; advancing the profession). Paraphrase it; read the wording at isc2.org.
  • Applying the canons: disclose risks to the public, report honestly, stay within authorization and competence, declare conflicts, keep knowledge current, and do not endorse the unfit.
  • Complaints: members must report breaches by other members; standing depends on the canon; sworn written affidavit; the committee acts on evidence provided; the board's decision is final; the only sanction named is revocation.
  • The 2026 Code of Professional Conduct and its decision-making guide are voluntary guidance that build on the mandatory code.
  • Organizational codes: follow, shape and promote them; SEC Item 406 for senior financial officers; ISACA, ACM and IEEE codes; RFC 1087. Good codes are specific, apply to everyone, protect people who speak up, and have consequences.
  • AI ethics: OECD, UNESCO, NIST AI RMF, IEEE 7000, ISO/IEC 42001 share transparency, human oversight, fairness, privacy, safety and accountability. EU AI Act: bans from 2025; high-risk rules delayed to December 2027 and August 2028.
  • Conflicts: facts, duties, rank them (people and law first), consider options, act through proper channels, document.
