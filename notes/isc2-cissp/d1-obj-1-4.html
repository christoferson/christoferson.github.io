Overview

Objectives 1.4 and 1.5

1.4 Understand legal, regulatory, and compliance issues that pertain to information security in a holistic context. 1.5 Understand requirements for investigation types (i.e., administrative, criminal, civil, regulatory).

CISSP does not expect legal expertise, but it does expect security leaders to know which laws apply, when to call legal counsel, and how an investigation's purpose changes how evidence must be handled. Laws covered in the CC guide (GDPR fines and reach, HIPAA, SOX, the SEC rule, NIS2, DORA, US breach laws and the EU AI Act) are summarized in CC 1.3. The official outline is on isc2.org. This page is general information, not legal advice.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is opening an online store in China and uses a payroll provider in South Africa.

Cybercrime and data breach laws

Objective 1.4Recognizing what computer crime laws prohibit, how international cooperation works, and when a breach triggers legal duties.

The main US law is the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA, 18 U.S.C. 1030). It makes it a crime to access a computer without authorization or by exceeding authorized access to obtain protected information, to access a computer to defraud, to knowingly transmit code that intentionally causes damage, to traffic in passwords, and to extort with threats against computers. Most US computers count as "protected computers" because they are used in interstate commerce.

Van Buren narrowed "exceeds authorized access". In Van Buren v. United States (2021) the Supreme Court held that the phrase covers only getting information from parts of a computer that are "off-limits" to the person: a "gates-up-or-down inquiry." Misusing data you are allowed to access, such as a police officer searching a database for a bribe, is not a CFAA crime, though it may break other laws or policy. Older study material often treats insider misuse as a CFAA violation.

Because attacks cross borders, countries cooperate through treaties. The Council of Europe's Budapest Convention (2001) is the main one, ratified by over 80 states including the US, with a second protocol (2022) on obtaining electronic evidence across borders. The new UN Convention against Cybercrime, adopted in December 2024 and opened for signature in Hanoi in October 2025, needs 40 ratifications to enter into force and is not in force yet.

Data breaches trigger notification duties, each with its own clock: GDPR 72 hours to the supervisory authority, the SEC four business days after deciding an incident is material, every US state's breach law, and sector rules (CC 1.3). The incident response plan should list them all (objective 7.6).

Intellectual property and licensing

Objective 1.4Protecting the organization's intellectual property and respecting others', including the terms of software licenses.

TypeProtectsUS duration
CopyrightOriginal works of authorship, including software code and documentationLife of the author plus 70 years; for works made for hire, 95 years from publication or 120 from creation, whichever ends first
PatentInventions: the right to "exclude others from making, using, offering for sale, or selling" themUtility patents up to 20 years from filing; design patents 15 years from grant
TrademarkWords, symbols or designs that identify the source of goods or servicesIndefinitely while used and renewed (every 10 years)
Trade secretInformation valuable because it is secret, kept with reasonable secrecy measuresAs long as it stays secret; protection is lost once disclosed

A trade secret is only protected if the organization takes reasonable steps to keep it secret, which makes security controls (classification, access control, NDAs, DLP) part of the legal protection. The DMCA (17 U.S.C. 1201) makes it illegal to circumvent technical measures that control access to copyrighted works, or to traffic in tools for doing so.

Software licensing is a contract: using software beyond the license is infringement and a compliance risk (SP 800-53 CM-10 asks organizations to track license use). Common forms are commercial end-user license agreements, subscription licenses, and open source licenses, which must meet the Open Source Initiative's ten-point definition, including free redistribution and access to source code. Copyleft licenses such as the GPL require derived works to be released under the same license; permissive licenses such as MIT, BSD and Apache allow reuse in proprietary software with attribution. The retail group's developers run software composition analysis to catch both vulnerable and incompatibly licensed open source components (objective 8.4).

Import and export controls

Objective 1.4Knowing that encryption and some security technology are controlled exports, and which regimes control them.

  • US Export Administration Regulations (EAR, Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security) control dual-use items, including encryption, which is in Category 5 Part 2 of the Commerce Control List (for example ECCN 5A002 for hardware and 5D002 for software). Most commercial encryption can be exported under License Exception ENC; BIS notes "there is no 'unexportable' level of encryption under license exception ENC", but classification, reporting and some destinations still require care.
  • ITAR (State Department) controls defense articles, services and technical data on the US Munitions List, including encryption specially designed for military use.
  • Wassenaar Arrangement: 42 participating states agree lists of conventional arms and dual-use goods and technologies, which each country implements in its own law; much of the EAR's encryption control comes from it.

Some countries also restrict importing or using encryption. Before the retail group ships hardware security modules to its new China operation, its legal team checks both US export rules and Chinese import and use rules.

Transborder data flow

Objective 1.4Moving personal data across borders lawfully, and tracking the legal mechanisms that keep changing.

GDPR Chapter V allows personal data to leave the EU only through approved routes:

  • Adequacy decisions (Art. 45): the European Commission finds a country's protection essentially equivalent.
  • Appropriate safeguards (Art. 46), mainly the Commission's standard contractual clauses (SCCs), usually with a transfer impact assessment.
  • Binding corporate rules (Art. 47) for transfers within a multinational group.
  • Derogations for specific situations (Art. 49), such as explicit consent, for occasional transfers only.

EU–US transfers have been unstable. The EU Court of Justice struck down the Safe Harbor arrangement (Schrems I, 2015) and the Privacy Shield (Schrems II, July 2020) because of US surveillance law. The EU–US Data Privacy Framework received an adequacy decision on July 10, 2023, and the EU General Court rejected a challenge to it on September 3, 2025 (Latombe). After a June 2026 US Supreme Court ruling on the independence of agencies such as the FTC, privacy campaigners announced a new challenge, so organizations relying on the framework often keep SCCs ready as a fallback.

Other countries restrict data leaving. China's PIPL requires, for exports of personal information, one of a government security assessment, certification, or the government's standard contract, plus separate consent; critical infrastructure operators and large data handlers must keep data in China unless an assessment allows otherwise. The retail group hosts its Chinese store's customer data in China and sends only aggregated sales figures to headquarters.

Privacy laws

Objective 1.4Knowing the main privacy laws by region, what they share, and where they differ.

  • GDPR (EU): applies to processing of EU residents' personal data, including by non-EU organizations targeting them; fines up to 4% of global turnover (CC 1.3).
  • CCPA as amended by the CPRA (California): consumer rights to know, delete, correct and opt out of sale or sharing; new regulations on cybersecurity audits and risk assessments took effect January 1, 2026.
  • PIPL (China, in force November 1, 2021): applies to processing inside China and to processing abroad that offers products or services to, or analyzes the behavior of, people in China; foreign processors must appoint a representative in China. Unlike GDPR it has no "legitimate interests" basis, and sensitive data such as biometrics, health, financial and location data needs separate consent.
  • POPIA (South Africa, enforceable from July 1, 2021): eight conditions for lawful processing (accountability, processing limitation, purpose specification, further processing limitation, information quality, openness, security safeguards and data subject participation), overseen by the Information Regulator.
  • HIPAA (US healthcare): the Privacy and Security Rules protect health information held by covered entities and their business associates; a proposed update to the Security Rule (January 2025) is not yet final.

Most privacy laws trace back to the OECD privacy guidelines (1980, revised 2013), commonly summarized as eight principles: collection limitation, data quality, purpose specification, use limitation, security safeguards, openness, individual participation and accountability. Security safeguards appear in every law, which is where the security team comes in. The retail group's payroll provider in South Africa processes its staff data under POPIA, so the contract includes POPIA's security conditions.

Contractual and industry requirements

Objective 1.4Treating contracts and industry standards as security obligations alongside law and regulation.

Not every obligation is a law. Industry standards become binding through contracts: PCI DSS through the agreements with card brands and the acquiring bank. Customers write security requirements into contracts and ask for assurance such as an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate or a SOC 2 report. Contracts with suppliers should set security requirements, incident notification times, audit rights, data location and return or deletion of data at the end. Service level agreements set measurable commitments; in the 2027 outline they sit with personnel and third-party policies in objective 1.8.

A compliance register (the obligations register in CC 2.1) brings all of these together: law, regulation, contract and industry standard, each mapped to the controls that meet it.

Types of investigation

Objective 1.5Matching how evidence is gathered and handled to the type of investigation and the standard of proof it must meet.

TypePurpose and who leadsStandard of proof
AdministrativeInternal: policy violations or operational problems; led by the organization (HR, security, management)Set by internal policy; usually the lowest bar
CriminalBreaking criminal law; led by law enforcement and prosecutors; can lead to prisonBeyond a reasonable doubt, which "requires more certainty than any other burden of proof in law"
CivilDisputes between parties, such as breach of contract or IP infringement; led by the parties and their lawyers; leads to damagesPreponderance of the evidence: "more likely true than not"
RegulatoryBreaches of regulations; led by a regulator such as the FTC, SEC or a data protection authority; leads to fines or ordersDepends on the regulator and law

An administrative investigation can turn criminal, so handle evidence from the start as if it may go to court: preserve it, document a chain of custody (CC 5.3), and involve legal counsel early. In US courts, key rules for digital evidence are that it must be authenticated with "evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is" (Federal Rule of Evidence 901), that the original is generally required to prove a recording's content (the best evidence rule, FRE 1002), and that regularly kept business records such as logs can be admitted despite the hearsay rule (FRE 803(6)).

Civil cases bring eDiscovery: once litigation is reasonably anticipated, a legal hold suspends normal deletion of relevant data. If electronically stored information is lost because reasonable steps were not taken, US courts can order remedies, and with "intent to deprive" can instruct the jury to assume the data was unfavorable (Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e)). The Electronic Discovery Reference Model (EDRM 2.0, September 2026) describes the stages: identification, preservation, collection, processing, review, production, presentation and, new in 2.0, disposition.

The investigation type drives the process. At the retail group, a manager suspected of copying customer lists starts as an administrative case run by HR and security. When the copies are found at a competitor, legal counsel issues a legal hold, the evidence already preserved with hashes and a chain of custody supports a civil claim for trade secret theft, and the police are informed because the theft may also be a crime.

Further reading

  • Van Buren v. United States (US Supreme Court, 2021, via Cornell LII): the CFAA "exceeds authorized access" decision.
  • International dimension of data protection (European Commission): adequacy decisions, SCCs and BCRs.
  • Trade secret policy (USPTO): what qualifies as a trade secret and the laws that protect it.

Key takeaways

  • Cybercrime: CFAA covers unauthorized access, fraud, damage, password trafficking and extortion; Van Buren (2021) limits "exceeds authorized access" to off-limits areas. Budapest Convention for cooperation; the UN cybercrime convention is not yet in force. Breaches trigger notification clocks.
  • IP: copyright (life + 70), patent (20 years from filing), trademark (renewable while used), trade secret (while secret, needs reasonable protection). DMCA bans circumvention. Licenses: commercial, open source (OSI definition), copyleft vs permissive.
  • Export controls: EAR Category 5 Part 2 and License Exception ENC for commercial encryption; ITAR for military items; Wassenaar (42 states) for shared lists.
  • Transborder data: GDPR adequacy, SCCs, BCRs, derogations. Safe Harbor and Privacy Shield fell; the 2023 Data Privacy Framework survived a 2025 challenge but faces another. PIPL requires an assessment, certification or standard contract.
  • Privacy laws: GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, PIPL (no legitimate interests basis), POPIA (eight conditions), HIPAA; the OECD principles underlie them.
  • Contracts and industry standards (PCI DSS, SOC 2, ISO certificates, SLAs) are obligations too; track them with law in one register.
  • Investigations: administrative (internal policy), criminal (beyond a reasonable doubt), civil (preponderance), regulatory (regulator's rules). Treat evidence as court-bound; FRE 901, 1002 and 803(6); legal holds and FRCP 37(e); EDRM 2.0 stages.
