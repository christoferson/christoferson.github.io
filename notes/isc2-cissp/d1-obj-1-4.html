Overview

Objective 1.4

1.4 Understand legal, regulatory, and compliance issues that pertain to information security in a holistic context. Sub-topics: cybercrimes and data breaches; licensing and intellectual property; import/export controls; transborder data flow; privacy (GDPR, CCPA, PIPL, POPIA, HIPAA); contractual, legal, industry standards and regulatory requirements.

CISSP does not expect legal expertise. It expects security leaders to recognize which laws apply, what they require of security, and when to bring in legal counsel. The basics (GDPR fines and reach, HIPAA, SOX, the SEC rule, NIS2, DORA and US breach laws) are in CC 1.3; investigation types are on the 1.5 page. The official outline is on isc2.org. This page is general information, not legal advice.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is listed in the US, is opening an online store in China, and uses a payroll provider in South Africa.

Thinking about law holistically

Objective 1.4Seeing the whole set of legal obligations, how they overlap, and who decides how to meet them.

"Holistic" means looking at all the obligations together, because one system or incident usually triggers several. A breach of EU customers' card data at the retail group engages GDPR, PCI DSS through its bank contract, US state breach laws for American customers, SEC disclosure rules as a listed company, and possibly NIS2 if it is an important entity, each with its own deadline and regulator.

Three kinds of law, and their typical consequences:

  • Criminal law: offenses against society, prosecuted by the state; penalties include prison and fines (computer crime, fraud, theft of trade secrets).
  • Civil law (in the sense of private law): disputes between parties, such as contract, negligence and intellectual property; remedies are damages and injunctions.
  • Administrative or regulatory law: rules made and enforced by government agencies under statute, such as data protection authorities, the FTC, the SEC or health regulators; penalties are fines, orders and audits.

Legal systems differ too. Common law countries (the US, UK, Australia) rely heavily on court precedent; civil law countries (most of continental Europe, China, Latin America) rely mainly on written codes. Laws also reach beyond borders: GDPR applies to non-EU organizations that offer goods or services to, or monitor, people in the EU; China's PIPL reaches processing abroad aimed at people in China. The practical consequences for a security leader:

  • Keep an obligations register covering laws, regulations, contracts and standards by country and business line, each mapped to controls and an owner (below).
  • Involve legal counsel in decisions with legal effect: incident notification, monitoring employees, investigations, cross-border transfers, contract terms. The security team identifies the issue; counsel interprets the law.
  • Where laws conflict (one country requires data to stay in-country, another requires it to be disclosed), escalate to leadership and counsel rather than choose alone.

Cybercrime law

Objective 1.4Recognizing what computer crime laws prohibit and how countries cooperate across borders.

The main US law is the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA, 18 U.S.C. 1030). In summary, it makes it a crime to:

  • access a computer without authorization, or exceed authorized access, to obtain national defense information, financial records, government information, or information from any "protected computer";
  • access a nonpublic federal government computer without authorization;
  • access a protected computer to defraud and obtain something of value;
  • knowingly transmit code or commands that intentionally damage a protected computer, or cause damage recklessly or otherwise through unauthorized access;
  • traffic in passwords with intent to defraud, and extort by threatening damage to computers or data.

Almost any computer connected to the internet counts as a "protected computer" because it is used in interstate or foreign commerce. The CFAA also gives victims a civil right of action, so companies can sue as well as report.

Van Buren narrowed "exceeds authorized access". In Van Buren v. United States (2021) the Supreme Court held that the phrase covers only obtaining information from parts of a computer that are "off-limits" to the person: a "gates-up-or-down inquiry." Misusing data you are allowed to access, such as a police officer searching a database for a bribe, is not a CFAA crime, though it may break other laws, contracts or policy. Older study material often treats insider misuse as a CFAA violation, so strong access control and policy are what protect against authorized insiders.

Because attacks cross borders, countries cooperate through treaties. The Council of Europe's Budapest Convention (2001), ratified by over 80 states including the US, harmonizes offenses (illegal access, interception, data and system interference, misuse of devices, computer fraud and forgery, child abuse material) and procedures for preserving and sharing evidence; a second protocol (2022) covers obtaining electronic evidence directly from providers in other countries. The UN Convention against Cybercrime, adopted in December 2024 and opened for signature in Hanoi in October 2025, needs 40 ratifications to enter into force and is not in force yet.

Common categories of computer crime: the computer as the target (intrusion, denial of service, ransomware), as the tool (fraud, identity theft, theft of trade secrets), and as incidental (evidence of other crimes stored on it). The category affects who investigates and which law applies.

Data breach notification

Objective 1.4Knowing when a breach triggers legal duties to notify, to whom, and how fast.

A data breach (unauthorized access to or disclosure of protected data) triggers notification duties that differ by law, sector and country. Most clocks start when the organization becomes aware of or determines the breach, not when it happened, which is why detection and fast triage matter legally.

Notification clocks worth knowing
GDPR (EU)
Supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach; affected people without undue delay when the risk to them is high
NIS2 (EU)
Significant incidents: early warning within 24 hours, notification within 72 hours, final report within one month
SEC (US public companies)
Form 8-K Item 1.05 generally within four business days after determining an incident is material
US states
Every state, DC, Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands has a breach law; timing and triggers differ
FTC Safeguards Rule
Non-bank financial institutions notify the FTC within 30 days of discovering unencrypted data of at least 500 consumers was acquired
EU Cyber Resilience Act
Manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents in their products from September 11, 2026
CIRCIA (US critical infrastructure)
Final rule not yet issued; reports are not yet required (proposed 72 hours for incidents, 24 hours for ransom payments)

Common features: encryption of the data often removes or reduces the duty (a "safe harbor" in many US state laws, if the key was not also taken); notices must describe what happened, what data was involved and what people should do; and contracts add their own clocks, such as notifying a customer or the card brands within a set number of hours. The incident response plan should list every applicable clock with the owner who notifies (objective 7.6).

Intellectual property and licensing

Objective 1.4Protecting the organization's intellectual property and respecting others', including the terms of software licenses.

TypeProtectsUS duration

PatentInventions: the right to "exclude others from making, using, offering for sale, or selling" them; requires public disclosureUtility patents up to 20 years from filing; design patents 15 years from grant

Security is part of IP law in two ways. A trade secret is only protected if the organization takes reasonable steps to keep it secret, so classification, access control, NDAs, DLP and exit procedures are evidence in court. And the DMCA (17 U.S.C. 1201) makes it illegal to circumvent technical measures that control access to copyrighted works or to traffic in tools for doing so, with exemptions reviewed every three years. Choosing between a patent and a trade secret is a security decision too: a patent publishes the invention for 20 years of protection; a trade secret lasts as long as it is kept secret, which suits things like a pricing algorithm.

Software licensing is contract law: using software beyond the license is infringement and a compliance risk, and audits by vendors can be expensive. Common forms:

  • Commercial licenses: perpetual or subscription, per user, device or core; end-user license agreements set the terms, including restrictions on reverse engineering.
  • Open source licenses: must meet the Open Source Initiative's ten-point definition, including free redistribution and access to source code. Copyleft licenses such as the GPL require derived works distributed to others to be released under the same license; permissive licenses such as MIT, BSD and Apache allow reuse in proprietary software with attribution.
  • Cloud and AI terms: who owns inputs and outputs, whether the provider may train on your data, and indemnities for infringement claims about AI-generated content.

Controls: a software inventory and license tracking (SP 800-53 CM-10), software composition analysis that flags incompatible licenses as well as vulnerabilities (objective 8.4), and SBOMs that record licenses.

Import and export controls

Objective 1.4Knowing that encryption and some security technology are controlled exports, and which regimes control them.

  • US Export Administration Regulations (EAR, Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security) control dual-use items, including encryption, which is in Category 5 Part 2 of the Commerce Control List (for example 5A002 for hardware and 5D002 for software). Most commercial encryption can be exported under License Exception ENC; BIS notes "there is no 'unexportable' level of encryption under license exception ENC", but classification, reporting, and some destinations and end users still require licenses.
  • ITAR (State Department) controls defense articles, services and technical data on the US Munitions List, including encryption specially designed for military use.
  • Wassenaar Arrangement: 42 participating states agree lists of conventional arms and dual-use goods and technologies, which each country implements in its own law; much of the EAR's encryption control comes from it.
  • Sanctions: separately, sanctions laws (such as those administered by the US Treasury's OFAC) restrict dealing with certain countries, entities and people, which matters for selling software, paying ransoms and hiring contractors.

Exports include more than shipping hardware: uploading controlled software to a server abroad, giving a foreign national access to controlled technical data (a "deemed export" in US rules), or carrying an encrypted laptop across a border can all count. Some countries also restrict importing or using encryption. Before the retail group ships hardware security modules to its China operation, its legal team checks US export rules and Chinese import and use rules.

Transborder data flow

Objective 1.4Moving personal data across borders lawfully, and tracking mechanisms that keep changing.

GDPR Chapter V allows personal data to leave the EU only through approved routes:

  • Adequacy decisions (Art. 45): the European Commission finds a country's protection essentially equivalent.
  • Appropriate safeguards (Art. 46), mainly the Commission's standard contractual clauses (SCCs), usually with a transfer impact assessment and supplementary measures such as encryption where the destination's laws are a concern.
  • Binding corporate rules (Art. 47) approved for transfers within a multinational group.
  • Derogations for specific situations (Art. 49), such as explicit consent or performance of a contract, for occasional transfers only.

Article 48 adds that a foreign court or authority's demand for data is not, by itself, a lawful basis for transfer. EU–US transfers have been unstable. The EU Court of Justice struck down the Safe Harbor arrangement (Schrems I, 2015) and the Privacy Shield (Schrems II, July 2020) because of US surveillance law. The EU–US Data Privacy Framework received an adequacy decision on July 10, 2023, and the EU General Court rejected a challenge to it on September 3, 2025 (Latombe). After a June 2026 US Supreme Court ruling on the independence of agencies such as the FTC, privacy campaigners announced a new challenge, so organizations relying on the framework often keep SCCs ready as a fallback.

Other countries restrict data leaving:

  • China (PIPL): exports of personal information need one of a government security assessment, certification, or the government's standard contract, plus separate consent; critical information infrastructure operators and large data handlers must keep data in China unless an assessment allows otherwise; rules were eased in March 2024. Personal information may not be provided to foreign courts or police without approval.
  • South Africa (POPIA section 72) allows transfers only where the recipient is bound by comparable protection or another condition applies.

Remember that data "flows" through more than file transfers: cloud regions, backups, support staff abroad who can see the data, and AI services that process prompts elsewhere. The retail group hosts its Chinese store's customer data in China, sends only aggregated sales figures to headquarters, and requires its cloud providers to name every country where support staff can access EU data.

Privacy laws

Objective 1.4Knowing the main privacy laws by region, what they share, and where they differ.

  • GDPR (EU): applies to processing of personal data in the EU, and to non-EU organizations offering goods or services to, or monitoring, people in the EU. Lawful bases include consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task and legitimate interests; rights include access, rectification, erasure and portability; data protection by design and default (Art. 25); fines up to EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher.
  • CCPA as amended by the CPRA (California): consumer rights to know, delete, correct and opt out of sale or sharing of personal information, plus limits on sensitive data; regulations on cybersecurity audits, risk assessments and automated decision-making technology took effect January 1, 2026, with the first audits due from April 2028 depending on revenue.
  • PIPL (China, in force November 1, 2021): reaches processing abroad aimed at people in China, which must appoint a representative there. It has seven legal bases but no "legitimate interests" basis; sensitive personal information (biometrics, religion, health, financial accounts, location, and all data of children under 14) needs separate consent and an impact assessment. Serious violations can bring fines up to 50 million yuan or 5% of the previous year's turnover.
  • POPIA (South Africa, enforceable from July 1, 2021): eight conditions for lawful processing (accountability, processing limitation, purpose specification, further processing limitation, information quality, openness, security safeguards and data subject participation); organizations register an information officer; overseen by the Information Regulator.
  • HIPAA (US healthcare): the Privacy and Security Rules protect health information held by covered entities (providers, health plans, clearinghouses) and their business associates, which sign business associate agreements; a proposed update to the Security Rule (January 2025) is not yet final.

Most privacy laws trace back to the OECD privacy guidelines (1980, revised 2013), commonly summarized as eight principles: collection limitation, data quality, purpose specification, use limitation, security safeguards, openness, individual participation and accountability. Every one of these laws requires security safeguards appropriate to the risk, which is where the security team comes in; most also require breach notification, records of processing, and assessments for high-risk processing. The retail group's South African payroll provider processes staff data under POPIA, so the contract includes POPIA's security conditions and a right to audit.

Privacy law protects people, not just data. A perfectly secure system can still break privacy law if it collects more than needed, uses data for a new purpose, or keeps it too long. Security controls are necessary for compliance, not sufficient.

Contractual, industry and regulatory requirements

Objective 1.4Treating contracts and industry standards as binding obligations alongside law and regulation, and tracking them all together.

Obligations come from four sources, and a compliance program has to cover all of them:

  • Laws: statutes such as SOX (financial reporting controls for US public companies), GDPR, state privacy and breach laws.
  • Regulations: rules from agencies, such as the SEC's cybersecurity disclosure rules, the FTC Safeguards Rule for non-bank financial institutions, HIPAA's Security Rule, and in the EU, NIS2 for essential and important entities and DORA for financial entities (applying since January 17, 2025).
  • Industry standards: binding through contracts, such as PCI DSS through agreements with card brands and the acquiring bank, enforced by fines and loss of the right to accept cards rather than prosecution.
  • Contracts: customers' security requirements and audit rights, suppliers' obligations (security requirements, incident notification times, data location, return or deletion of data at the end), cyber insurance conditions, and SLAs (objective 1.8).

Customers increasingly ask for assurance: an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate, a SOC 2 Type 2 report, PCI attestations, or completed questionnaires. Answering them accurately is itself an obligation; overstating controls in a questionnaire can become a breach of contract or misrepresentation.

US laws the exam names

Objective 1.4Federal and sector laws that appear in CISSP questions.

  • Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA, 1986, amended several times): the main US law against unauthorized access to computers.
  • Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA, 1986): protects communications in transit (wiretapping) and stored communications, with rules for when providers and employers may access them.
  • Economic Espionage Act (1996): makes theft of trade secrets a federal crime, with heavier penalties when it benefits a foreign government. The Defend Trade Secrets Act (2016) adds a federal civil remedy.
  • HIPAA (1996): privacy and security rules for protected health information held by covered entities (providers, health plans, clearinghouses). HITECH (2009) added breach notification, made business associates directly liable and raised penalties.
  • Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA, 1999): financial institutions must give privacy notices and protect customer information; the FTC Safeguards Rule sets security program requirements for many of them.
  • Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX, 2002): public companies must maintain internal controls over financial reporting, which include the IT systems behind it.
  • Privacy Act (1974): limits how US federal agencies collect, use and disclose records about individuals.
  • COPPA (1998): websites and online services directed at children under 13 need verifiable parental consent before collecting their personal data.
  • FERPA (1974): protects student education records at institutions receiving federal funding.
  • FISMA (2002, updated 2014): requires federal agencies to run information security programs, the basis of the NIST RMF.

US states add their own laws, including breach notification in every state and comprehensive privacy laws such as California's CCPA as amended by the CPRA.

GDPR in depth

Objective 1.4The parts of the GDPR most often tested.

  • Scope: applies to processing of personal data in the EU, and to organizations outside the EU that offer goods or services to, or monitor, people in the EU.
  • Principles (Art. 5): lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimization; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; accountability (objective 2.4).
  • Lawful bases (Art. 6): consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interests.
  • Special categories (Art. 9): racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data for identification, health, and sex life or sexual orientation; processing is prohibited unless a specific condition applies.
  • Data protection officer (Art. 37 to 39): required for public authorities and for organizations whose core activities involve large-scale monitoring or large-scale special category data; independent, reports to top management.
  • Data protection impact assessment (Art. 35): required before processing likely to result in high risk, such as large-scale profiling or monitoring.
  • Breach notification: to the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless unlikely to result in risk (Art. 33); to the individuals without undue delay when high risk (Art. 34).
  • Right to erasure (Art. 17): with exceptions such as legal obligations, freedom of expression, public interest and legal claims.
  • Fines: up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, for the most serious infringements.

Intellectual property details and legal systems

Objective 1.4The specifics behind each type of IP protection, and the main legal systems.

  • Patents: protect inventions that are novel, useful and non-obvious; in the US generally 20 years from filing; the invention is disclosed publicly in return.
  • Copyright: protects the expression of ideas (code, documents, images) automatically on creation; generally the author's life plus 70 years. Fair use allows limited use for purposes such as criticism, teaching and research; the first sale doctrine lets the buyer of a lawful copy resell it.
  • Trademarks: protect names, logos and slogans; TM marks an unregistered claim, the ® symbol a registered mark; renewable indefinitely while in use.
  • Trade secrets: protected only while the owner takes reasonable steps to keep them secret (NDAs, access control); no registration and no fixed term.
  • Licensing: end-user license agreements accepted by click-wrap (clicking "I agree"), shrink-wrap (opening the package) or browse-wrap (using a website, the weakest form to enforce).
  • International: WIPO administers the main IP treaties; the WTO's TRIPS agreement sets minimum IP standards for member countries.

Legal systems: common law (based on precedent, as in the US and UK), civil law (based on written codes, as in most of Europe), customary law (based on tradition), religious law (based on religious texts), and mixed systems that combine them. Categories of law: criminal (offenses against society, punished by fines or prison), civil or tort (disputes between parties, remedied by damages) and administrative or regulatory (rules made by government agencies).

Scenario check

PracticeApplying legal and compliance reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the curious analyst
Situation
A customer service analyst with legitimate access to the loyalty database looks up a celebrity's purchase history and shares it with a journalist.
Best answer
Treat it as a policy violation and a likely privacy breach: preserve logs, involve HR, legal and the privacy officer, and assess notification duties. Strengthen controls (purpose-based access, monitoring of unusual lookups).
Why
After Van Buren, misuse of data someone may access is generally not a CFAA crime, but it can still breach privacy law, contracts and policy. The organization's duties to the customer remain.
Scenario 2: the new analytics vendor
Situation
Marketing wants to send EU customers' purchase data to a US analytics start-up next week. The vendor is not certified under the Data Privacy Framework.
Best answer
Use a lawful transfer mechanism (standard contractual clauses with a transfer impact assessment), sign a processor agreement, and minimize or pseudonymize the data before sending; involve the privacy officer and counsel.
Why
GDPR Chapter V requires a transfer route; without DPF certification, SCCs are the usual choice. Consent of the marketing team is not a legal basis.
Scenario 3: the leaked source code
Situation
Your pricing algorithm, kept as a trade secret, appears in a public code repository. Investigation shows it was in a folder every employee could read.
Best answer
Request takedown, preserve evidence, involve counsel, and fix access control. Expect the weak protection to be raised in any legal action.
Why
Trade secret protection depends on reasonable secrecy measures; broad access undermines the claim. Security controls are part of the legal protection.

Key takeaways

  • Holistic view: criminal, civil and administrative law; common and civil law systems; extraterritorial reach; one incident triggers many obligations; an obligations register and early legal counsel.
  • Cybercrime: CFAA covers unauthorized access, fraud, damage, password trafficking and extortion, with civil claims too; Van Buren limits "exceeds authorized access" to off-limits areas. Budapest Convention for cooperation; the UN convention is not yet in force.
  • Breach clocks: GDPR 72 hours; NIS2 24 hours, 72 hours, one month; SEC four business days after materiality; every US state; FTC Safeguards 30 days (500+ consumers); CRA reporting from September 2026; CIRCIA not yet in force. Encryption often gives a safe harbor.
  • IP: copyright (life + 70), patent (20 years, disclosed), trademark (renewable while used), trade secret (while secret, needs reasonable protection). DMCA bans circumvention. Licenses: commercial, open source (copyleft vs permissive), cloud and AI terms.
  • Export controls: EAR Category 5 Part 2 and License Exception ENC; ITAR; Wassenaar (42 states); deemed exports; sanctions.
  • Transborder data: GDPR adequacy, SCCs with transfer assessments, BCRs, derogations; Art. 48 on foreign orders; Data Privacy Framework upheld in 2025 but challenged again; PIPL assessments, certification or standard contract; localization.
  • Privacy laws: GDPR, CCPA/CPRA (2026 audit and risk assessment rules), PIPL (no legitimate interests), POPIA (eight conditions), HIPAA (business associates); OECD principles; security is necessary, not sufficient.
  • Other obligations: laws, regulations (SEC, FTC, NIS2, DORA), industry standards (PCI DSS by contract), contracts and assurance requests, tracked together.
