Overview

Objective 3.3

Baselines and tailoring are on the 2.6 page; the RMF's Select step on the 1.9 page; the hardware and operating system capabilities controls rely on are on the 3.4 page. The official outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The retail group: about 4,000 staff, 300 stores in the US and the EU, its own card payment processing, a data center plus two cloud providers, an in-house development team and AI services. It is buying hardware security modules and a new firewall for the payment platform.

Selecting controls from requirements

Objective 3.3Deriving controls from a system's security requirements and writing them into design and purchasing decisions.

Controls should trace back to requirements, so every control has a reason and every requirement is met. Two kinds of requirement matter:

  • Functional requirements: what security features must do, such as "administrators authenticate with phishing-resistant MFA" or "card numbers are encrypted at rest with keys held in an HSM".
  • Assurance requirements: how much confidence is needed that the features work and cannot be bypassed: independent evaluation, testing depth, development process evidence, documentation.

Requirements come from the system's security categorization and risk assessment, from laws, regulations and contracts (PCI DSS for the payment platform), from the organization's policies and standards, from stakeholders' needs, and from the threat model. The usual path:

  1. Define requirementsFunctional and assurance, each specific and testable, with its source recorded.
  2. Start from a baselineSelect the baseline that matches the system's impact level and tailor it (objective 2.6).
  3. AllocateDecide which component, team or provider implements each control, including inherited and common controls (a cloud provider's physical security).
  4. Choose productsPrefer products whose security has been independently evaluated, and write the requirements into the purchase.
  5. VerifyTest and assess that the controls meet the requirements before go-live (section 3 and Domain 6).

A requirements traceability matrix links each requirement to its source, the controls that meet it, and the tests that prove it. It shows auditors why each control exists, exposes requirements with no control, and makes it easy to see what changes when a requirement changes.

For purchases, SP 800-53 control SA-4 Acquisition Process requires contracts to include, explicitly or by reference, security and privacy functional requirements, strength of mechanism, assurance requirements, the controls needed, documentation requirements and its protection, a description of the development and operating environment, allocation of responsibilities, and acceptance criteria. The retail group's tender for HSMs requires FIPS 140-3 validation at the security level its PCI requirements call for, vendor patch support for the life of the contract, and documentation of every administrative interface.

Product assurance: Common Criteria and FIPS 140-3

Objective 3.3Using independent evaluations to judge how much a product's security claims can be trusted.

Common Criteria (published as ISO/IEC 15408) is the international framework for evaluating IT products. The current version is CC:2022, which replaced CC 3.1 Release 5. Its vocabulary:

  • Target of evaluation (TOE): the product or system being evaluated.
  • Protection profile (PP): a set of security requirements for a class of products, such as firewalls or mobile devices, usually written by a user community.
  • Security target (ST): the vendor's statement of the TOE's security properties, which may claim conformance to a protection profile.
  • Evaluation assurance levels (EAL1 to EAL7): how deeply the product was evaluated, commonly listed as functionally tested, structurally tested, methodically tested and checked, methodically designed, tested and reviewed, semi-formally designed and tested, semi-formally verified design and tested, and formally verified design and tested.

Under the Common Criteria Recognition Arrangement (CCRA), 36 member nations accept certificates issued by the others, so a certified product can be bought without being re-evaluated. Recognition is generally limited to evaluations against collaborative protection profiles or the lower EALs, and the US scheme (NIAP) is described as accepting only protection-profile-based evaluations.

An EAL measures evaluation depth, not security. A product at EAL4 has been examined more thoroughly than one at EAL2, but only against its own security target: check that the evaluated configuration and claims match how you will use it.

FIPS 140-3 (effective September 2019, built on ISO/IEC 19790) is the US and Canadian standard for cryptographic modules, validated under NIST's Cryptographic Module Validation Program (CMVP). It has four security levels, which rise from correct algorithms and basic production-grade parts (Level 1), through tamper evidence and role-based authentication (Level 2), to tamper detection and response that erases secrets (Level 3) and protection against environmental attacks (Level 4).

FIPS 140-2 is now historical. After September 21, 2026, NIST moved the remaining FIPS 140-2 certificates to the Historical list, where they may be used "for existing systems only". New purchases for US federal use need FIPS 140-3 validation, and FIPS 140-3 certificates stay active for five years.

Other forms of product assurance: cloud services' FedRAMP authorizations and SOC 2 reports, ISO/IEC 27001 certification of the supplier, penetration test reports, the supplier's secure development evidence (SSDF-style attestations), and SBOMs (objective 1.11).

Verifying controls before acceptance

Objective 3.3Checking that selected controls are implemented and effective before a system is accepted and authorized.

Selection is not the end: controls must be implemented correctly and shown to work in the real configuration. Before acceptance:

  • Test against requirements: each requirement in the traceability matrix has a test case, including negative tests (an unauthenticated request is refused).
  • Configuration review: the product is set up as evaluated; many certified products are certified only in a specific configuration, and defaults may differ.
  • Security testing: vulnerability scans, penetration tests and code review proportionate to risk (objective 6.2).
  • Assessment and authorization: in the RMF, assessors confirm controls are "in place, operating as intended, and producing the desired results", and a senior official authorizes operation with the residual risk.
  • Acceptance criteria: written into the contract (SA-4), so the organization can refuse delivery that does not meet them.

After go-live, continuous monitoring keeps checking that controls stay effective (objective 1.9).

Choosing between control options

Objective 3.3Weighing alternative controls by effectiveness, cost, operability and assurance.

Most requirements can be met in more than one way. Choosing well means weighing:

  • Effectiveness: how much the control reduces the risk it targets, including against the threats in the threat model, and whether it can be bypassed.
  • Cost: purchase, implementation, operation, training and the business cost of friction; the control should cost less than the risk it reduces (objective 1.9).
  • Operability: whether staff can run it, whether it fits existing tools, and whether it works at the needed scale and performance.
  • Assurance: independent evaluation, maturity of the product and vendor, and how easily its effectiveness can be tested.
  • Coverage and layering: preventive, detective and corrective controls together, and diversity so one failure does not expose everything (objective 3.1).
  • Inheritance: controls provided by a platform or provider may be stronger and cheaper than building your own, if their scope and evidence are verified.

Where the ideal control is not possible (a legacy system cannot support MFA), a compensating control gives "equivalent or comparable protection" by other means, such as a jump host with MFA in front of it, documented with the risk owner's approval. Reference architectures and pre-approved patterns (a standard secure web application stack, a standard cloud landing zone) save teams from choosing from scratch and make reviews faster.

For the payment platform's firewall, the retail group compared three options: a hardware appliance evaluated against the network device protection profile, the cloud provider's native firewall service, and a virtual appliance. It chose the evaluated appliance for the card environment because PCI scope and assurance mattered most, and the native service for non-card workloads because it was cheaper to run and automate.

Assurance for cloud and managed services

Objective 3.3Selecting and relying on controls operated by a provider.

  • Know who operates each control: map required controls to provider, customer or shared responsibility for the service model in use.
  • Use provider evidence: attestation reports, certifications and authorizations show the provider's controls operate; check that the service and region you use are in scope.
  • Complementary controls: provider reports list controls the customer must operate (such as access management) for the provider's controls to be effective.
  • Configure the provider's options: encryption with customer keys, logging, network restrictions and identity integration are often available but not enabled by default.

Earlier evaluation criteria

Objective 3.3The predecessors of the Common Criteria that older material still mentions.

  • TCSEC (the US "Orange Book", 1985): rated systems mainly for confidentiality in divisions D (minimal protection), C (discretionary: C1, C2), B (mandatory: B1, B2, B3) and A (verified design: A1). It was part of the "Rainbow Series", with companion books such as the Red Book for networks.
  • ITSEC (Europe, 1991): rated functionality (F) and assurance (E0 to E6) separately and covered integrity and availability as well as confidentiality.
  • Common Criteria (ISO/IEC 15408) replaced both, with protection profiles, security targets and EALs (above).

Certification is the technical evaluation of a system's security; accreditation (now called authorization) is management's formal decision to accept the residual risk and operate it.

Scenario check

PracticeApplying control selection reasoning to situations like those in exam questions.

Scenario 1: the EAL comparison
Situation
Two firewalls are offered: one certified at EAL4 against its vendor's own security target, the other evaluated against the network device collaborative protection profile.
Best answer
Compare what each evaluation actually covered against your requirements; the protection-profile evaluation may be more relevant and more widely recognized, even if its EAL looks lower.
Why
EALs measure depth against the stated target, not overall security; protection profiles define requirements for a product class.
Scenario 2: the old crypto module
Situation
A vendor offers a VPN appliance whose crypto module has only a FIPS 140-2 certificate, now on the Historical list.
Best answer
For new deployments that must meet federal or contractual FIPS requirements, require a FIPS 140-3 validated module; the historical certificate is for existing systems only.
Why
FIPS 140-2 certificates moved to Historical after September 21, 2026.
Scenario 3: the missing requirement
Situation
After go-live, auditors find the new order system has no audit logging of administrator actions. The project says it "was not asked for".
Best response
Add the requirement and control now, and fix the process: derive requirements from the baseline and obligations, trace them to controls and tests, and include them in acceptance criteria.
Why
Controls should trace to requirements systematically; traceability and acceptance testing catch gaps before go-live.
Scenario 4: the provider's control
Situation
A cloud provider's SOC 2 Type 2 report covers physical security and hypervisor isolation. The project wants to mark those controls as met for the new system.
Best response
Mark them as inherited (common) controls after confirming the report covers the services and regions used, the period is current, and any complementary customer controls in the report are implemented.
Why
Inherited controls still need evidence and scope checks; SOC reports often list controls the customer must operate for the provider's controls to work.

Key takeaways

  • Requirements: functional (what features do) and assurance (confidence they work), from categorization, risk, law, contract, policy, stakeholders and threat models; each specific and testable.
  • Path: define requirements, start from a baseline and tailor, allocate, choose evaluated products, verify. A traceability matrix links requirement, source, control and test.
  • SA-4 puts functional, strength-of-mechanism and assurance requirements, documentation, environment, responsibilities and acceptance criteria into contracts.
  • Common Criteria (ISO/IEC 15408, CC:2022): TOE, protection profile, security target, EAL1-7 (evaluation depth, not security); CCRA recognition among 36 nations.
  • FIPS 140-3: four levels via the CMVP; FIPS 140-2 historical after September 21, 2026.
  • Verify before acceptance: tests per requirement, evaluated configuration, security testing, assessment and authorization, contractual acceptance criteria; then continuous monitoring.
