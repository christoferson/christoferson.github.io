5.2: LLM-as-Judge Engineering

A model grader is a measuring instrument, and an uncalibrated instrument can gate releases with confident nonsense. This page measures a judge against people, criterion by criterion, sets realistic targets from how well people agree with each other, and keeps the judge honest when its own model changes.

Overview

Skill 5.2

LLM-as-Judge Engineering: "Analyzing LLM-as-judge evaluator quality against human-labeled data, including calibration, rubric design, bias and position effects, inter-rater reliability, and judge drift across model versions."

Exam items give agreement figures between a judge and human raters and ask whether the judge can be trusted, what is wrong with it, or what to do first. The four classic biases and their corrections are in CCAR-P 4.3; this page is about measuring a judge’s quality in the first place.

Calibrate the judge per criterion against human labels before it gates anything

Skill 5.2Per criterion, on a labelled set, before the judge gates anything.

Take a service that drafts replies to customer emails. A judge model scores every draft from 1 to 5 on accuracy (billing facts), tone and policy (what may be promised), and the team wants it to replace human spot checks in the release gate.

judge_quality.py (judge_quality.py) 
"""Measure an LLM judge against human labels before it gates anything.
Scores are 1-5 per criterion on drafted customer emails (accuracy, tone, policy)."""
from collections import Counter


def weighted_kappa(a: list[int], b: list[int], k=5) -> float:
    """Quadratic-weighted Cohen's kappa: agreement beyond chance, near misses penalised less."""
    n = len(a)
    obs = sum((x - y) ** 2 for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n
    ca, cb = Counter(a), Counter(b)
    exp = sum(ca[i] * cb[j] * (i - j) ** 2 for i in range(1, k + 1) for j in range(1, k + 1)) / n ** 2
    return 1 - obs / exp if exp else 1.0


def calibration(judge: dict, human_a: dict, human_b: dict) -> dict:
    """Per criterion: judge vs the human consensus, the human-human ceiling, and leniency."""
    out = {}
    for crit in judge:
        consensus = [round((x + y) / 2) for x, y in zip(human_a[crit], human_b[crit])]
        out[crit] = {
            "judge_kappa": round(weighted_kappa(judge[crit], consensus), 2),
            "human_kappa": round(weighted_kappa(human_a[crit], human_b[crit]), 2),   # the realistic ceiling
            "leniency": round(sum(judge[crit]) / len(judge[crit]) - sum(consensus) / len(consensus), 2),
        }
    return out


def verdict(c: dict, min_ratio=0.85, max_leniency=0.3) -> dict:
    """Trust a criterion when the judge isn't systematically kind or harsh and gets close to human-human agreement."""
    v = {}
    for crit, m in c.items():
        if m["human_kappa"] < 0.6:
            v[crit] = "fix the rubric first: people don't agree either"
        elif abs(m["leniency"]) > max_leniency:
            v[crit] = "judge is systematically offset: anchor the score bands, then re-measure"
        elif m["judge_kappa"] < min_ratio * m["human_kappa"]:
            v[crit] = "judge below human agreement: add anchors, split into checks, or keep humans"
        else:
            v[crit] = "trusted for gating"
    return v


def drift(old: dict, new: dict, tolerance=0.05) -> list[str]:
    """Rerun the same calibration set on a new judge model version before switching."""
    return [f"{c}: kappa {old[c]['judge_kappa']} -> {new[c]['judge_kappa']}"
            for c in old if old[c]["judge_kappa"] - new[c]["judge_kappa"] > tolerance]

  • Anthropic’s rule: test a model grader’s reliability first, then scale; model graders need calibration against human graders.
  • Label a set: a few hundred outputs, each scored independently by two people, covering every segment and the hard cases.
  • Measure agreement beyond chance: weighted kappa (quadratic) treats a 4-versus-5 disagreement as smaller than 1-versus-5. Raw percent agreement looks good when most scores are the same.
  • Measure leniency separately: the mean difference between judge and humans. A judge can rank drafts like people do and still score everything a point higher.
  • Per criterion: here accuracy is trusted, tone is a point too generous, and the policy scores are unrelated to human judgement. One overall agreement figure would have hidden the policy problem.

Compare the judge with how well people agree; that is the ceiling

Skill 5.2Compare the judge with how well people agree.

  • Human-human agreement is the realistic target. If two trained raters agree at a kappa of 0.91, a judge at 0.88 is close to human; if they agree at 0.55, no judge can be validated, because the criterion itself is unclear.
  • The rules in verdict(): fix the rubric when people don’t agree (below 0.6); recalibrate when the judge is offset by more than 0.3 points; keep humans when the judge reaches less than 85% of human agreement.
  • Resolve human disagreements into a consensus label, and record cases people disagreed on: they are often where the rubric needs a rule.
  • Enough labels per segment: a judge can be good on billing emails and poor on complaints; measure where it will be used.

Anchor every score and split vague criteria into yes/no checks

Skill 5.2Make the judge’s job specific.

  • Anthropic’s tips: detailed, clear rubrics (possibly several per criterion), specific outputs (correct or incorrect, or a 1–5 scale), and reasoning before the score.
  • Anchors for every score: what a 2 and a 4 look like for tone, with a short example of each. Unanchored scales drift toward the generous middle, which is where the tone offset comes from.
  • Split vague criteria into checks: "follows policy" becomes yes/no questions: promises a refund amount? commits to a repair date? mentions a payment plan only when eligible? Binary checks agree better, and some can be code-graded.
  • Give the judge the facts it needs: the account data and the policy text, so accuracy is checked against a source, not the judge’s own knowledge.
  • Use a different model from the generator, as Anthropic’s grader example does.

Test for leniency, verbosity, self-preference and position effects

Skill 5.2Systematic errors to test for.

  • Leniency or severity: a constant offset from people, as with tone here. Anchors fix it; a score transformation hides it.
  • Verbosity: longer drafts scored higher when people preferred concise ones. Check scores against length on the labelled set.
  • Self-preference: a judge favouring outputs from its own model; use a different judge model.
  • Position effects in pairwise comparison: verdicts that change when the two answers swap places; judge both orders and count only consistent wins. Absolute scoring against a rubric, as here, avoids order effects but still needs calibration.
  • Diagnose before correcting: each bias has its own signature, and a fix for one doesn’t help another (CCAR-P 4.3).

Pin the judge's model and prompt, and recalibrate on any change

Skill 5.2The judge is a dependency with versions.

  • Pin the judge’s model ID and prompt, and record them with every score, so results from different months are comparable.
  • Rerun calibration on any judge change: a new judge model, a rubric edit or a retiring snapshot. drift() flags criteria whose kappa fell by more than 0.05.
  • Re-check on a schedule even without changes, because the outputs being judged change: a new generator model may write differently enough to move a judge that was calibrated on older drafts.
  • Don’t switch judges mid-comparison: a release compared with its baseline by two different judges measures the judges.

"The judge agrees with humans 90% of the time overall, so let it gate releases" can hide a criterion where it agrees no better than chance, or a consistent one-point generosity. Calibrate per criterion with chance-corrected agreement and leniency, compare with human-human agreement, and only then give it gating authority.

  1. A. Yes
  2. B. Not yet; check chance-corrected agreement and leniency per criterion first
  3. C. Yes, if it uses a larger model
  4. D. Only for small releases
Answer
B
Why
An overall figure can hide a criterion at chance level or a consistent offset.
Question 2
Scenario
Two trained human raters agree at kappa 0.55 on a criterion. What should be done before validating a judge on it?
  1. A. Validate the judge anyway
  2. B. Fix the rubric, since the criterion itself is unclear
  3. C. Use the judge’s scores as truth
  4. D. Add more raters only
Answer
B
Why
Human agreement is the ceiling; no judge can be validated against an unclear criterion.
Question 3
Scenario
A judge ranks drafts like humans but scores every draft about a point higher on tone. What is the fix?
  1. A. Subtract one point
  2. B. Add anchors with examples for each score level and recalibrate
  3. C. Ignore tone
  4. D. Use pairwise comparison
Answer
B
Why
Anchors fix leniency at its cause; a transformation hides it.
Question 4
Scenario
The team switches to a newer judge model mid-quarter. What must happen?
  1. A. Nothing
  2. B. Rerun calibration and don’t compare scores across the two judges
  3. C. Average the two judges
  4. D. Keep both forever
Answer
B
Why
A judge is a versioned dependency; comparing across judges measures the judges.

Key takeaways

  • Calibrate before scaling: a labelled set scored by two people, measured per criterion with weighted kappa and leniency.
  • Human-human agreement is the ceiling; if people don’t agree, fix the rubric before blaming the judge.
  • Anchor every score, split vague criteria into yes/no checks, give the judge source facts, and use a different model from the generator.
  • Test for leniency, verbosity, self-preference and, in pairwise setups, position effects; diagnose before correcting.
  • Pin the judge, rerun calibration on any change and on a schedule, and never compare releases scored by different judges.
