Overview

Task statement 3.1

Implement highly available solutions to meet resilience and business requirements.

High availability means the workload keeps serving users when a component, an Availability Zone or even a Region fails. This task tests whether you can read a business requirement ("99.99% uptime", "no data loss", "users on three continents") and pick the services, replication modes and failover mechanisms that meet it at a sensible cost. Typical scenarios: "the app went down when one AZ failed", "the database failover took too long", or "users in Europe need the app even if us-east-1 is unavailable".

Multi-AZ and multi-Region deployments

Knowledge

A Region is a geographic area containing at least three Availability Zones (AZs). Each AZ is one or more data centers with independent power, cooling and networking, close enough to the others for low-latency synchronous replication. AZ failures are rare but expected; Region-wide failures are much rarer.

  • Multi-AZ: protects against data center and AZ failures. It's the baseline for any production workload.
  • Multi-Region: protects against Region-wide failures and brings data closer to global users. It costs more and is more complex, so the requirement must justify it.

Compute layer

ComputeHow to spread it across AZs
EC2An Auto Scaling group with subnets in at least two (ideally three) AZs; it balances instances across them
ECSService subnets in several AZs; the default spread placement strategy balances tasks across AZs
EKSNode groups spanning AZs; topology spread constraints or pod anti-affinity to keep replicas in different AZs
LambdaMulti-AZ automatically. For functions attached to a VPC, give them subnets in several AZs
FargateSubnets in several AZs

Data layer

Data storeBuilt-in AZ resilience
Amazon S3 (Standard)Stored across at least three AZs; S3 One Zone classes use one
Amazon DynamoDBReplicated across three AZs automatically
Amazon EFS (Regional)Stored across AZs; mount targets in each AZ
RDS Multi-AZ DB instanceA synchronous standby in another AZ (not readable); automatic failover in about 1–2 minutes
RDS Multi-AZ DB clusterA writer and two readable standbys in three AZs; faster failover (typically under 35 seconds)
Amazon AuroraStorage keeps six copies across three AZs; up to 15 read replicas, any of which can be promoted on failover (typically about 30 seconds)
ElastiCache (Redis OSS or Valkey)A replication group with Multi-AZ and automatic failover to a replica
Amazon EBSOne AZ only. Protect with snapshots, or keep state elsewhere

An RDS read replica is not a high availability feature by itself: replication is asynchronous and failover isn't automatic. For "automatic failover with no data loss" within a Region, the answer is RDS Multi-AZ (or Aurora with replicas).

SLAs and availability math

Knowledge
  • SLA (service level agreement): AWS's commitment for a service, with service credits if it's missed. It isn't a guarantee of your workload's availability.
  • SLO (objective): the target you set for your own workload, such as 99.95% of requests succeeding each month.
  • SLI (indicator): the metric you measure it with, such as the success rate or p99 latency.

What the nines mean

AvailabilityDowntime per yearDowntime per month
99%3.65 days7.3 hours
99.9%8.77 hours43.8 minutes
99.95%4.38 hours21.9 minutes
99.99%52.6 minutes4.38 minutes
99.999%5.26 minutes26.3 seconds

Combining components

  • In series (every component is needed): multiply. A web tier at 99.9% calling a database at 99.9% gives 0.999 × 0.999 = 99.8%. Each dependency lowers the total.
  • In parallel (any one copy is enough): 1 − (1 − A)n. Two independent copies at 99% give 1 − 0.01² = 99.99%. Redundancy raises the total.

To reach a high target, add redundant copies of each component (parallel) and remove hard dependencies (series), for example with caching or queues so one failing component doesn't stop the whole request.

Replication and failover methods for stateful services

Knowledge

Synchronous vs asynchronous

SynchronousAsynchronous
HowA write is confirmed only after every copy has itA write is confirmed first, then copied
Data loss on failover (RPO)ZeroWhatever hadn't been copied yet (usually seconds)
DistanceShort, so it's used across AZsAny, so it's used across Regions
ExamplesRDS Multi-AZ, Aurora storage, DynamoDB within a RegionRead replicas, Aurora Global Database, DynamoDB global tables, S3 replication

Failover by service

ServiceReplicationFailover
RDS Multi-AZSynchronous to a standby in another AZAutomatic; the same DNS endpoint points at the new primary
RDS cross-Region read replicaAsynchronousManual: promote the replica to a standalone database, then repoint the application
Aurora (in a Region)Shared storage across three AZsAutomatic promotion of a replica (set priority with failover tiers)
Aurora Global DatabaseAsynchronous storage-level replication to secondary Regions, typically under a second behindSwitchover for planned moves (no data loss) or failover for an outage (RPO of about a second, RTO typically under a minute)
DynamoDB global tablesMulti-active: every replica Region accepts writes; asynchronous by default, last writer wins on conflictsNothing to fail over: send traffic to another Region
S3 replicationAsynchronous, cross-Region (CRR) or same-Region (SRR); Replication Time Control replicates 99.99% of objects within 15 minutesPoint readers at the replica bucket, or use a Multi-Region Access Point with failover controls
ElastiCache Global DatastoreAsynchronous to secondary RegionsPromote a secondary Region
EFS replicationAsynchronous to another Region or AZFail over to the replica file system

DynamoDB global tables also offer a multi-Region strong consistency mode, which gives an RPO of zero across Regions at the cost of higher write latency. The default, and the usual exam answer, is eventual consistency.

Techniques to achieve high availability

Knowledge
TechniqueWhat it means
RedundancyAt least two of everything, in different AZs
Health checks and self-healingLoad balancers stop sending traffic to unhealthy targets; Auto Scaling, ECS and Kubernetes replace them
Static stabilityKeep enough capacity running in the remaining AZs to absorb an AZ failure without scaling up during the event (for example, 3 AZs each sized for 50% of peak)
Stateless computeSessions and files outside the instances, so any instance can serve any request
Loose couplingQueues (SQS) and events between components, so a slow or failed component doesn't block the others
Timeouts, retries and backoffFail fast, retry with exponential backoff and jitter, and stop retrying when a dependency is clearly down (circuit breaker)
Graceful degradationServe a reduced experience (cached data, a feature turned off) rather than an error
Cell-based architectureSplit the workload into independent copies (cells), each serving a share of customers, so one failure affects few
Data plane over control planeDuring an outage, rely on actions that don't need to create or change resources (for example, Route 53 health checks shifting traffic) rather than launching new ones

Multi-Region approaches

  • Active-passive: one Region serves traffic; the other is a standby (pilot light or warm standby) that takes over on failover. It's simpler and avoids write conflicts.
  • Active-active: every Region serves traffic all the time (Route 53 latency or geolocation routing, DynamoDB global tables). It gives the lowest RTO and best global latency, with the most complexity.

Amazon Application Recovery Controller (ARC) helps control failover:

  • Zonal shift: moves load balancer traffic away from an impaired AZ, and zonal autoshift lets AWS do it automatically.
  • Routing controls: on/off switches for Route 53 failover between Regions, operated through a highly available data plane.

Translating business requirements into technical resiliency needs

Skill

Start with the business's tolerance for downtime and data loss, expressed as two numbers:

  • RTO (recovery time objective): how long the service may be unavailable.
  • RPO (recovery point objective): how much recent data may be lost, measured in time.
RequirementDesign that meets it
Survive an AZ failure with no data lossMulti-AZ everything: Auto Scaling across AZs, RDS Multi-AZ or Aurora, DynamoDB, S3
99.99% availability for a Regional appMulti-AZ, statically stable capacity, health-checked load balancing, no single points of failure
Survive a Region outage, RTO hours, RPO 24 hoursBackup and restore: cross-Region backup copies and IaC to rebuild
Survive a Region outage, RTO minutes, RPO secondsWarm standby or active-passive: Aurora Global Database or DynamoDB global tables, a scaled-down stack in the second Region, Route 53 failover
Near-zero RTO, global usersActive-active: DynamoDB global tables, stacks in each Region, Route 53 latency routing or Global Accelerator
Strict cost limitsThe cheapest pattern that still meets RTO and RPO; don't build active-active for a 4-hour RTO

AWS Resilience Hub

Resilience Hub turns these targets into something you can check. You define a resiliency policy (RTO and RPO for four kinds of disruption), add your application (from CloudFormation stacks, Terraform state, EKS clusters or tags), and run an assessment. It reports whether the current architecture can meet the policy, recommends changes (such as Multi-AZ or backups), and suggests alarms, standard operating procedures and Fault Injection Service experiments to prove it.

resiliency-policy.yml 
CriticalAppPolicy:
  Type: AWS::ResilienceHub::ResiliencyPolicy
  Properties:
    PolicyName: mission-critical
    Tier: MissionCritical
    Policy:
      Software:   # bad deployment, application bug
        RtoInSecs: 300
        RpoInSecs: 60
      Hardware:   # instance or volume failure
        RtoInSecs: 300
        RpoInSecs: 60
      AZ:   # Availability Zone outage
        RtoInSecs: 300
        RpoInSecs: 60
      Region:   # Region outage
        RtoInSecs: 3600
        RpoInSecs: 300

"Continuously verify that the application can still meet its RTO and RPO as the architecture changes" points to AWS Resilience Hub assessments, run from the pipeline after each deployment.

Identifying and remediating single points of failure

Skill

A single point of failure (SPOF) is any component whose failure stops the workload. Look for anything there's only one of, or anything that exists in only one AZ.

Single point of failureFix
One EC2 instance running the appAn Auto Scaling group across AZs behind a load balancer. Even a group of 1 adds self-healing
Single-AZ RDS instanceEnable Multi-AZ (or use Aurora with a replica in another AZ)
One NAT gateway for all private subnetsOne NAT gateway per AZ, with each AZ's route table pointing to its own
Sessions stored on the instanceElastiCache or DynamoDB for sessions
Uploaded files on EBSS3 or EFS
Single-node ElastiCache clusterA replication group with Multi-AZ and automatic failover
Scheduled jobs on one "cron server"EventBridge Scheduler invoking Lambda, ECS tasks or Step Functions
One VPN connection or one Direct Connect linkBoth VPN tunnels in use, a second connection on different equipment, or VPN as backup for Direct Connect
Interface VPC endpoints or VPC Lambda in one subnetSubnets in several AZs
Application connects to an instance's IP addressConnect through DNS names: load balancer, RDS endpoint, service discovery

Example: a NAT gateway per AZ

With one shared NAT gateway, losing its AZ cuts off internet access for private subnets in every AZ. Give each AZ its own:

nat-per-az.yml (AZ a shown; repeat for b and c) 
NatGatewayA:
  Type: AWS::EC2::NatGateway
  Properties:
    SubnetId: !Ref PublicSubnetA                      # lives in AZ a
    AllocationId: !GetAtt NatEipA.AllocationId

PrivateRouteTableA:
  Type: AWS::EC2::RouteTable
  Properties:
    VpcId: !Ref Vpc

PrivateDefaultRouteA:
  Type: AWS::EC2::Route
  Properties:
    RouteTableId: !Ref PrivateRouteTableA
    DestinationCidrBlock: 0.0.0.0/0
    NatGatewayId: !Ref NatGatewayA                   # AZ a traffic stays in AZ a

PrivateSubnetARouteTable:
  Type: AWS::EC2::SubnetRouteTableAssociation
  Properties:
    SubnetId: !Ref PrivateSubnetA
    RouteTableId: !Ref PrivateRouteTableA

Tools that find single points of failure

  • AWS Trusted Advisor: fault tolerance checks, such as single-AZ RDS instances, load balancers in one AZ, or EBS volumes without recent snapshots.
  • AWS Resilience Hub: assesses the application against its resiliency policy.
  • AWS Well-Architected Tool: the reliability pillar review.
  • AWS Fault Injection Service: proves the fix works by actually failing the component.

Enabling cross-Region solutions

Skill

Amazon Route 53

Route 53 decides which Region (or endpoint) a user reaches. Its routing policies:

PolicyRoutes byUse for
SimpleOne answer (or several, returned in random order)Single endpoints
FailoverPrimary while healthy, otherwise secondaryActive-passive across Regions
WeightedPercentagesGradual migrations, blue/green at DNS level
LatencyThe Region with the lowest latency for the userActive-active global applications
GeolocationThe user's continent, country or stateData residency, localized content
GeoproximityDistance, adjustable with a biasShifting more or less traffic to a Region
Multivalue answerUp to 8 healthy recordsSimple client-side load spreading
IP-basedThe client's IP range (CIDR)Routing known networks to specific endpoints

Health checks

  • Endpoint health checks: Route 53 checkers on the internet probe an IP or domain over HTTP, HTTPS or TCP, optionally matching a string in the response.
  • Calculated health checks: combine other health checks (for example, healthy if at least 2 of 3 are healthy).
  • CloudWatch alarm health checks: healthy while an alarm is OK. Use them for private resources that internet checkers can't reach, or to fail over on application metrics.
  • Evaluate target health: on alias records pointing at a load balancer, Route 53 uses the load balancer's own health instead of a separate check.

Example: active-passive failover between two Regions

route53-failover.yml 
PrimaryHealthCheck:
  Type: AWS::Route53::HealthCheck
  Properties:
    HealthCheckConfig:
      Type: HTTPS
      FullyQualifiedDomainName: eu-west-1.app.example.com
      ResourcePath: /health
      RequestInterval: 10              # fast checks (10 or 30 seconds)
      FailureThreshold: 3

PrimaryRecord:
  Type: AWS::Route53::RecordSet
  Properties:
    HostedZoneName: example.com.
    Name: app.example.com.
    Type: A
    SetIdentifier: primary-eu-west-1
    Failover: PRIMARY
    HealthCheckId: !Ref PrimaryHealthCheck
    AliasTarget:
      DNSName: !GetAtt PrimaryAlb.DNSName
      HostedZoneId: !GetAtt PrimaryAlb.CanonicalHostedZoneID
      EvaluateTargetHealth: true

SecondaryRecord:
  Type: AWS::Route53::RecordSet
  Properties:
    HostedZoneName: example.com.
    Name: app.example.com.
    Type: A
    SetIdentifier: secondary-eu-central-1
    Failover: SECONDARY                 # answered only when primary is unhealthy
    AliasTarget:
      DNSName: eu-central-1-alb-123456.eu-central-1.elb.amazonaws.com
      HostedZoneId: Z215JYRZR1TBD5          # the ALB hosted zone ID for eu-central-1
      EvaluateTargetHealth: true

"Route 53 can't health check an internal application in a private subnet": its checkers run on the internet. Use a CloudWatch alarm-based health check on a metric from inside the VPC.

Skill

DynamoDB global tables

A global table is one table with replicas in several Regions. Applications read and write their local replica, and changes replicate to the others, typically within a second.

global-table.yml 
OrdersTable:
  Type: AWS::DynamoDB::GlobalTable
  Properties:
    TableName: orders
    BillingMode: PAY_PER_REQUEST
    AttributeDefinitions:
      - { AttributeName: orderId, AttributeType: S }
    KeySchema:
      - { AttributeName: orderId, KeyType: HASH }
    StreamSpecification:
      StreamViewType: NEW_AND_OLD_IMAGES          # required for replication
    Replicas:
      - Region: eu-west-1
        PointInTimeRecoverySpecification:
          PointInTimeRecoveryEnabled: true
      - Region: eu-central-1
        PointInTimeRecoverySpecification:
          PointInTimeRecoveryEnabled: true
  • Conflicts: concurrent writes to the same item in two Regions are resolved by last writer wins. Design so each item is usually written in one Region (for example, a user's "home" Region).
  • Capacity: each replica needs enough write capacity for all writes, since every write is replicated to it.
Skill

RDS and Aurora across Regions

OptionRPO and RTONotes
Cross-Region automated backup replicationRPO: minutes (point-in-time restore). RTO: the time to restoreCheapest; backups only
RDS cross-Region read replicaRPO: replication lag. RTO: minutes (promote, then repoint)Also serves reads in the other Region
Aurora Global DatabaseRPO: about 1 second. RTO: typically under a minuteManaged switchover or failover; secondary Regions can serve reads, and write forwarding sends their writes to the primary

An unplanned failover of an Aurora global database promotes a secondary Region's cluster to primary:

aws rds failover-global-cluster \
  --global-cluster-identifier orders-global \
  --target-db-cluster-identifier arn:aws:rds:eu-central-1:111111111111:cluster:orders-eu-central-1 \
  --allow-data-loss                   # unplanned failover; use switchover-global-cluster when the primary is healthy

"Cross-Region disaster recovery for a relational database with an RPO of seconds and an RTO of about a minute" points to Aurora Global Database. A read replica needs manual promotion and repointing, which is slower.

Skill

S3 cross-Region replication

  • Requirements: versioning on both buckets, and an IAM role that S3 assumes to replicate.
  • New objects only: objects that existed before the rule aren't copied. Use S3 Batch Replication for them.
  • Options: replicate delete markers (off by default), change replica ownership to the destination account, replicate KMS-encrypted objects with a destination key, and Replication Time Control for a 15-minute replication SLA with metrics.
  • Multi-Region Access Points: one global endpoint routed to the nearest bucket, with failover controls to move traffic between buckets.
s3-replication.yml 
SourceBucket:
  Type: AWS::S3::Bucket
  Properties:
    VersioningConfiguration: { Status: Enabled }
    ReplicationConfiguration:
      Role: !GetAtt ReplicationRole.Arn
      Rules:
        - Id: to-eu-central-1
          Status: Enabled
          Priority: 1
          Filter: { Prefix: "" }
          DeleteMarkerReplication:
            Status: Enabled
          Destination:
            Bucket: arn:aws:s3:::orders-documents-eu-central-1
            ReplicationTime:   # Replication Time Control
              Status: Enabled
              Time: { Minutes: 15 }
            Metrics:
              Status: Enabled
              EventThreshold: { Minutes: 15 }
Skill

CloudFront

  • Edge locations worldwide: cached content keeps being served from the edge even if the origin is briefly unavailable.
  • Origin groups: a primary and a secondary origin (for example, S3 buckets in two Regions, or ALBs in two Regions). CloudFront retries the secondary when the primary returns chosen status codes (such as 500, 502, 503, 504) or times out. Origin failover works for GET, HEAD and OPTIONS requests.
  • Origin Shield: an extra caching layer that reduces load on the origin.

"A static website must stay available if the S3 bucket's Region fails" points to S3 cross-Region replication plus a CloudFront origin group with the replica as the secondary origin.

An ALB needs subnets in at least two AZs. Without cross-zone load balancing, each load balancer node only sends traffic to targets in its own AZ, so unequal target counts per AZ give unequal load.

Health checks, Auto Scaling and draining together

alb-asg.yml 
WebTargetGroup:
  Type: AWS::ElasticLoadBalancingV2::TargetGroup
  Properties:
    VpcId: !Ref Vpc
    Protocol: HTTP
    Port: 8080
    HealthCheckPath: /health
    HealthCheckIntervalSeconds: 15
    HealthyThresholdCount: 3
    UnhealthyThresholdCount: 2
    Matcher: { HttpCode: "200" }
    TargetGroupAttributes:
      - Key: deregistration_delay.timeout_seconds   # let in-flight requests finish (default 300)
        Value: "30"
      - Key: slow_start.duration_seconds   # ramp traffic to new targets
        Value: "60"

WebAsg:
  Type: AWS::AutoScaling::AutoScalingGroup
  Properties:
    MinSize: "3"
    MaxSize: "9"
    VPCZoneIdentifier: [!Ref PrivateSubnetA, !Ref PrivateSubnetB, !Ref PrivateSubnetC]   # three AZs
    TargetGroupARNs: [!Ref WebTargetGroup]
    HealthCheckType: ELB                # replace instances the load balancer marks unhealthy
    HealthCheckGracePeriod: 120         # time to boot before checks count
    LaunchTemplate:
      LaunchTemplateId: !Ref WebLaunchTemplate
      Version: !GetAtt WebLaunchTemplate.LatestVersionNumber

By default an Auto Scaling group uses EC2 status checks only. An instance whose application has crashed still passes them, so it's never replaced, even though the load balancer has stopped sending it traffic. Set HealthCheckType: ELB.

"When one AZ is degraded, shift traffic away from it quickly" points to ARC zonal shift on the load balancer (or zonal autoshift to let AWS do it).

Configuring applications to support multiple AZs and Regions while minimizing downtime

Skill

Infrastructure failover only helps if the application copes with it.

ConcernWhat to do
Database failoverConnect through the cluster or instance endpoint (DNS), never an IP; respect a short DNS TTL; retry failed connections. RDS Proxy keeps client connections open and reduces failover time
SessionsStore them in ElastiCache or DynamoDB, not in memory on one instance
RetriesRetry with backoff and jitter; make writes idempotent so retries are safe
DNS-based failoverLower the TTL (for example, 60 seconds) well before you might need to fail over

Example: Region failover for an active-passive app

Assume eu-west-1 is primary and eu-central-1 runs a warm standby, with Aurora Global Database, ECR replication and Route 53 failover records.

  1. Detect the problemRoute 53 health checks or CloudWatch alarms show eu-west-1 unhealthy.
  2. Promote the databaseFail over the Aurora global database to eu-central-1, which becomes the writer.
  3. Scale out the standbyRaise the Auto Scaling group or ECS service in eu-central-1 to full production capacity.
  4. Move trafficRoute 53 failover records switch automatically, or ARC routing controls flip them deliberately.
  5. Verify and communicateCheck synthetic canaries and error rates in eu-central-1, then plan the return (failback) once eu-west-1 recovers.

Automate these steps as a tested runbook (Systems Manager Automation or Step Functions) and rehearse them regularly. Untested failover procedures often fail when they're needed.

Key takeaways

  • Multi-AZ is the production baseline; multi-Region only when the requirement (Region outage, global users) justifies it.
  • Compute: Auto Scaling, ECS, EKS and VPC Lambda with subnets in several AZs. Data: RDS Multi-AZ, Aurora, DynamoDB, S3 and EFS are multi-AZ; EBS is single-AZ.
  • Series components multiply availability down; parallel copies raise it: 1 − (1 − A)n.
  • Synchronous replication gives RPO 0 across AZs; asynchronous replication works across Regions with an RPO of seconds.
  • Cross-Region: Aurora Global Database (RPO about 1 second, RTO about 1 minute), DynamoDB global tables (multi-active, last writer wins), S3 CRR (versioning, existing objects need Batch Replication, RTC for 15 minutes), CloudFront origin groups.
  • Route 53 failover with health checks; CloudWatch alarm health checks for private resources; latency routing for active-active.
  • Remove single points of failure: one NAT gateway per AZ, Multi-AZ databases, external sessions, no cron servers.
  • ALB plus Auto Scaling with HealthCheckType: ELB, a grace period and a short deregistration delay. ARC zonal shift moves traffic away from a bad AZ.
  • Applications: use endpoints not IPs, low DNS TTLs, retries with backoff, RDS Proxy, Global Accelerator, KMS multi-Region keys and replica secrets.
