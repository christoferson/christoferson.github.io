5.3: Persistent-Instruction Design

Writing the standing instructions behind repeated work so every chat behaves the same way: what belongs in them, what stays in each request, and how to cover the awkward cases before they happen.

Overview

Skill 5.3

Persistent-Instruction Design: "Applying persistent-instruction design to repeated interactions that require consistent behavior, including Project instructions and other persistent instructions, the division of labor between persistent instructions and per-request prompts, and anticipating the use cases and constraints that keep outputs uniform."

Exam items show a set of instructions and inconsistent results, and ask what to add, move or remove. Building a single prompt is skill 1.1; deciding whether a requirement is an instruction, knowledge or a Skill is skill 5.1. This skill is writing the instructions that persist.

Put team rules in Project instructions, personal preferences in account instructions

Skill 5.3Where standing guidance can live in claude.ai.

  • Instructions for Claude (Settings > Your Claude > Instructions): applied to all of your conversations. For your own general preferences: methods you prefer, terms you use, typical situations, how you like answers.
  • Project instructions: apply only to chats in that Project, and to everyone using it. For a task's context, workflow rules, requirements, and the role Claude should take.
  • Skills: add behaviours or capabilities, such as a format or a procedure, that Claude uses when relevant (skill 4.3).

The Help Center says these can be used on their own or together; it doesn't set out which wins in a conflict, so avoid contradictions between them rather than relying on one to override another. A team's rules belong in the Project instructions, not in each person's account instructions, where they'd differ from person to person.

Make persistent what is true for every request; keep the rest per request

Skill 5.3The division of labour.

  • Persistent: what's true for every request: the role, the audience, tone and length, the format, the rules (never promise money), the sources to use, how to handle each recurring kind of case, and what to do when something is missing.
  • Per request: what changes each time: the specific input (this customer's email, this order), and any one-off variation ("they'd prefer a phone call").
  • Signs the split is wrong: people paste the same paragraph into every request (it should be persistent); or the instructions name one customer or one week's issue (that's per request, and it will confuse later chats).

With the standing rules in the Project, a per-request prompt can be one line, and four people asking in four ways still get the same kind of reply.

Write measurable rules, named cases and a marker for gaps

Skill 5.3Two versions of the Project instructions, and the requests that go with them.

Take four customer service staff who draft complaint replies in a shared Project. Replies vary in length, tone and what they promise, depending on who asks and how.

reply_instructions.txt (Complaint replies instructions) 
Project "Complaint replies" (customer service, 4 staff)

VERSION 1 (outputs varied by person and by day)
  Be helpful and professional. Write good replies to customer complaints.

VERSION 2 (anticipates the cases and constraints)
  Role: you draft email replies to customers who've complained about faulty
  outdoor gear. A staff member reviews and sends every reply; you never
  promise anything on the shop's behalf without their approval.

  Every reply:
  - Opens by naming the product and the problem in the customer's words.
  - Is under 150 words, plain language, no exclamation marks, signed
    "[staff name], Customer Service".
  - Uses the returns policy in Project knowledge for every rule you state;
    if the policy doesn't cover the case, write [CHECK POLICY] and stop.

  By case:
  - Within warranty, standard fault: offer repair or replacement per policy.
  - Outside warranty: explain kindly, offer the paid repair service.
  - Customer mentions injury, fire, or a safety risk: don't draft a reply;
    write SAFETY - ESCALATE TO SERVICE LEAD and summarise the email.
  - Customer is angry or threatens to go public: draft a calm, short
    acknowledgement only, and mark it [LEAD TO REVIEW].
  - Request for compensation beyond policy: say a manager will reply within
    two working days; never offer money.

  If anything needed for the reply is missing (order number, product),
  ask for it in the reply rather than guessing.

PER-REQUEST PROMPTS (what changes each time)
  "Draft a reply to this email. Customer's order: 48213. [email pasted]"
  "Same as above, but the customer prefers a phone call; offer one."
  "This customer has written three times; acknowledge the delay."
  • Role and oversight up front: Claude drafts, a person sends; that one line shapes every reply.
  • Measurable rules: "under 150 words, no exclamation marks" can be checked; "professional" can't.
  • Grounded in knowledge: policy rules come from the policy in Project knowledge, with a marker when it doesn't cover the case.
  • Cases named: each kind of complaint has its own instruction, including the ones Claude must not handle.
  • Still short: the policy itself stays in knowledge; the instructions point to it.

Cover the variations and edge cases before outputs go wrong

Skill 5.3Finding what the instructions need to cover before the outputs go wrong.

  • List the variations: go through recent real examples and group them; each group that needs different handling gets a line.
  • Cover the edges: missing information, requests outside the rules, sensitive or risky content, and requests Claude should hand back to a person.
  • State constraints positively, with the reason: "keep replies under 150 words so they read well on a phone" works better than a list of don'ts, in line with Anthropic's prompting advice.
  • Say what to do when unsure: a marker such as [CHECK POLICY] keeps Claude from inventing an answer to fill the gap.
  • Ask Claude: "What kinds of complaints would these instructions handle badly?" is a quick way to find gaps.

"Add 'be consistent' to the instructions" doesn't make outputs consistent. Consistency comes from specific rules, named cases, shared sources and short per-request prompts.

Test the instructions on real cases with different people asking

Skill 5.3Before the team relies on them.

  • A small test set: a handful of real emails covering each case, including the awkward ones.
  • Different askers: have two people send the same emails with their own wording; the replies should match in structure and rules.
  • Change one thing at a time: after an edit, rerun the set, so you know which change helped.
  • Keep a dated copy: note what changed and why, so a later problem can be traced (skill 5.4).

Practice questions

Exam-style questions written for these notes. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
Four people get different kinds of reply to similar complaints. Each has their own account instructions. What should change?
  1. A. Nothing
  2. B. Move the team’s rules into the shared Project instructions
  3. C. Ask everyone to be consistent
  4. D. Use separate Projects
Answer
B
Why
Team rules in personal instructions differ from person to person.
Question 2
Scenario
Staff paste the same paragraph of rules into every request. What does that suggest?
  1. A. Good practice
  2. B. Those rules should be persistent Project instructions
  3. C. The rules are wrong
  4. D. A Skill is needed
Answer
B
Why
Repeated content belongs in persistent instructions.
Question 3
Scenario
Which instruction makes output easier to keep uniform?
  1. A. “Be professional”
  2. B. “Under 150 words, no exclamation marks, so it reads well on a phone”
  3. C. “Be consistent”
  4. D. “Do your best”
Answer
B
Why
Measurable rules with a reason can be followed and checked.
Question 4
Scenario
What should Claude do when the policy doesn’t cover a case?
  1. A. Guess
  2. B. Insert a marker such as [CHECK POLICY] for a person to resolve
  3. C. Refuse the reply
  4. D. Use general knowledge
Answer
B
Why
A marker keeps Claude from inventing an answer.

Key takeaways

  • Instructions for Claude apply to all your chats (personal preferences); Project instructions apply to one Project and everyone in it (team rules); Skills add procedures and formats. Avoid contradictions between them.
  • Persistent instructions hold what's true every time (role, audience, format, rules, sources, cases, what to do when unsure); each request holds the specific input and one-off changes.
  • Uniform outputs come from measurable rules, named cases, rules grounded in knowledge, and markers for gaps, not from words like "be consistent".
  • Anticipate variations from real examples, cover the edges, give reasons, and ask Claude where the instructions are weak.
  • Test with a set of real cases and different askers, change one thing at a time, and keep a dated record of changes.
