2.2: Hallucination Detection and Verification

Catching what Claude stated without support: sources that don't exist, precise figures nobody gave it, contradictions and skewed assumptions. Then checking before anyone relies on them.

Overview

Skill 2.2

Hallucination Detection and Verification: "Analyzing outputs for unsupported content, including fabricated citations, confident invented specifics, inconsistencies, and bias, and applying verification techniques such as source checking, citation review, and independent confirmation before the output is used."

Exam items give a draft with a few too-good details and ask what to check, how, and what to do with what fails. The general evaluation of a draft is skill 2.1; how far checking must go for a given use is skill 2.3.

Sources: Anthropic's guide to reducing hallucinations and the Claude Help Center article on web search, checked on 20 November 2026 and paraphrased. The verification log is original.

Running example. The warehouse supervisor asks Claude, with web search on, for a one-page safety handout for new staff based on the shop's handbook.

Why it happens

Skill 2.2Plausible isn't the same as supported.

Anthropic describes hallucination as text that is factually incorrect or inconsistent with the context Claude was given. Claude produces the most plausible continuation of what it's working on, so when it lacks a fact it can produce something that looks like the right kind of fact: a realistic statistic, a credible-sounding study, a reasonable date. Fluency and confidence are therefore no evidence of accuracy. The risk is highest where the request needed specifics the sources didn't contain.

The techniques that reduce it lower the rate but don't eliminate it, and Anthropic's own guidance is to always validate critical information, especially for high-stakes decisions.

Four kinds of unsupported content

Skill 2.2What the guide names, and how each shows itself.

  • Fabricated citations: a study, report, organisation, author, quote or link that doesn't exist, or doesn't say what's claimed. They look most convincing when they're specific: a year, a percentage, a named institute.
  • Confident invented specifics: exact numbers, dates, names or guarantees that aren't in any source given, stated as plainly as the real facts beside them.
  • Inconsistencies: the draft disagrees with itself (two different break intervals) or with the source. One of them, at least, is wrong.
  • Bias: skewed assumptions or framing, such as assumed gender or background, one-sided descriptions of groups, or a slant the sources don't support. It's harder to spot because nothing is factually checkable; read for assumptions about people.

  • Pull out the checkable claims: numbers, dates, names, quotes, citations and promises are what can be wrong and what readers will rely on.
  • Label each claim's type, because the type tells you the check: a citation is looked up, a specific is traced to a source, an inconsistency is settled by the authority, and bias is read for.
  • Act on every failure: correct it from the source, remove it, or mark it for a person. Don't leave an unverified claim in because it sounds right.

Verification techniques

Skill 2.2The guide's three, and how to use each.

  • Source checking: compare each claim with the document it should come from. If the handbook doesn't say it, Claude didn't get it from the handbook.
  • Citation review: open every cited source and confirm it exists and says what's attributed to it. With web search, Claude's answers include citations and links precisely so you can do this. The Help Center advises cross-referencing them, and relying on authoritative sources for important decisions. Links can also break, which is one more reason to open them.
  • Independent confirmation: check against something Claude didn't produce: the original records, an authoritative publication, or a colleague who knows. Asking the same chat "are you sure?" isn't independent; it may simply repeat or rationalise the claim.
  • Spotting drift: asking for the same thing more than once and comparing the answers can expose shaky claims. Anthropic's guidance notes that inconsistencies across attempts can indicate hallucination.

"The citation has an author, a year and a percentage, so it's real" is exactly how fabricated citations get through. Specificity is not verification; only opening the source is.

Reducing it up front

Skill 2.2Requests that leave less room for invention.

  • Give the sources, and tell Claude to use only them, not general knowledge.
  • Allow "I don't know": Anthropic says explicitly permitting uncertainty can drastically reduce false information.
  • Quotes first: for long documents, ask Claude to pull the relevant quotes before answering, and to base the answer on them.
  • Make it retract: ask Claude to find a supporting quote for each claim after drafting, and to remove any it can't support.

These narrow the gap; checking the output still closes it.

Key takeaways

  • Claude can produce plausible facts it doesn't have; confidence and specificity are no evidence of accuracy.
  • Look for fabricated citations, confident invented specifics, inconsistencies (within the draft or with the source), and bias in assumptions about people.
  • Log each checkable claim with its type and the check it needs; correct, remove or escalate every failure.
  • Verify by source checking, citation review (open every link; web search answers include citations for this) and independent confirmation; asking the same chat "are you sure?" isn't independent.
  • Reduce it up front with sources only, permission to say "I don't know", quotes first and retraction of unsupported claims; still validate critical information.
