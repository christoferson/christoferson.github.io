4.2: Delegation and Workflow Redesign

Going through an existing workflow step by step and deciding, for each one, whether Claude does it, a person checks what Claude does, or a person keeps it, and noticing when too much has been handed over.

Overview

Skill 4.2

Delegation and Workflow Redesign: "Evaluating an existing business workflow using Delegation criteria, including task reversibility, stakes, need for human judgment or empathy, and accountability, to assign each step as AI-executed, human-retained, or human-reviewed, and recognizing over-delegation."

Exam items describe a workflow, or a plan to hand part of one to Claude, and ask which steps a person should keep or review, or what's wrong with the plan. The guide's preparation advice lists exactly this: deciding which steps Claude may execute and which a person must retain.

Delegation is one of the four competencies in Anthropic's AI Fluency framework. The course page names its parts as Problem Awareness, Platform Awareness and Task Delegation, and names three ways of working with AI: Automation, Augmentation and Agency. It doesn't define them there, so the readings on this page are mine; the four criteria come from the exam guide itself.

Sources: the CCAO-F exam guide, the AI Fluency course page and Anthropic's Usage Policy, checked on 20 November 2026 and paraphrased. The workflow and assignments are my own.

Running example. The outdoor-gear shop gets about 60 complaints a week about faulty gear. The customer service lead wants Claude to take over as much of the handling as it safely can.

The four criteria

Skill 4.2The questions to ask of every step (my reading of each).

  • Reversibility: if the step goes wrong, can it be undone cheaply? A draft can be rewritten; a payment, a sent message or a public statement can't be taken back.
  • Stakes: what does a mistake cost: money, safety, legal exposure, a customer relationship, someone's job or rights? Higher stakes call for more human involvement.
  • Need for human judgment or empathy: does the step weigh competing interests, make an exception, or deal with someone upset, grieving or vulnerable? Claude can help prepare, but the person on the other side may need a person.
  • Accountability: who answers for the outcome? If a named person or role is responsible, by policy, law or job, that person must make or approve the decision. Accountability can't be handed to a tool.

Anthropic's Usage Policy is a hard example of the last two. In its high-risk areas (such as legal, medical, financial, employment and insurance decisions), a qualified person must meaningfully review an AI recommendation before it's delivered or acted on (skill 2.3, and Domain 6).

Three assignments

Skill 4.2Where each step ends up.

  • AI-executed: Claude does it and the result is used without checking each item. Suits steps that are reversible, low-stakes, need no judgment and where errors show up later anyway. Spot-check samples over time.
  • Human-reviewed: Claude does the work and a person checks, edits and approves it before it's used. Suits medium stakes, or steps where the person stays accountable but Claude can do most of the effort.
  • Human-retained: a person does the step. Claude may still help around it, by preparing a summary or suggesting points, but the decision or the contact stays with the person. Suits irreversible, high-stakes, sensitive or accountable steps.

  • Split mixed steps: "handle the complaint" was one job; redesign separates the routine parts (sorting, lookup, drafting) from the parts that need a person (exceptions, angry customers, safety).
  • Route by case, not only by step: the same step can be human-reviewed for routine cases and human-retained for unusual ones. Write down what sends a case to a person.
  • Keep humans where accountability sits: the refund rules belong to the service lead; safety reporting belongs to whoever owns product safety.
  • Revisit the assignments: if spot checks show AI-executed steps going wrong, move them to human-reviewed; if a reviewed step is clean for months, its review can become lighter sampling.

Recognising over-delegation

Skill 4.2Signs that too much has been handed to Claude.

  • Irreversible actions without a person: Claude's output is sent, paid or published with no one approving it.
  • Review in name only: a person "approves" so many items, so fast, that they can't really check them. That's AI-executed with a signature.
  • Sensitive contact automated: upset, vulnerable or high-value people get replies no one has read.
  • No one accountable: when something goes wrong, nobody owns the decision because "Claude decided".
  • Policy or regulated decisions automated: exceptions, eligibility, or anything in a high-risk area without qualified review.
  • Lost skill: nobody on the team can do or check the step any more without Claude.

"Claude is accurate on 95 percent of cases, so automate the whole workflow" ignores what the other 5 percent cost and who answers for them. Accuracy decides how much review is needed; stakes, reversibility, empathy and accountability decide whether a person must be involved at all.

Under-delegation

Skill 4.2The opposite mistake.

Keeping every step with people, or reviewing every low-stakes item line by line, wastes the time Claude could save and leaves less attention for the steps that really need it. Sorting emails and pulling order details are good candidates for full delegation. Communicating capability honestly to the people affected by the redesign is skill 4.4.

Key takeaways

  • Judge each step on reversibility, stakes, need for human judgment or empathy, and accountability.
  • Assign it as AI-executed (reversible, low-stakes, spot-checked), human-reviewed (Claude drafts, a person approves) or human-retained (a person decides or makes the contact, with Claude helping around it).
  • Redesign by splitting mixed steps, routing unusual cases to people, keeping decisions where accountability sits, and revisiting assignments as evidence comes in.
  • Over-delegation: irreversible actions with no approver, rubber-stamp review, unread replies to sensitive contacts, no owner, automated policy or high-risk decisions.
  • Accuracy sets how much review is needed; it doesn't remove the need for a person on high-stakes, irreversible or accountable steps. Don't under-delegate the routine work either.
