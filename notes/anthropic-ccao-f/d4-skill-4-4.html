4.4: Capability Communication

Explaining a Claude use case to people who don't use AI much: what it will do, what it can't be trusted with, who stays in charge, and what results to expect, so they neither oversell it nor dismiss it.

Overview

Skill 4.4

Capability Communication: "Applying capability communication for stakeholders with limited AI familiarity, including what Claude can and cannot reliably do for a proposed use case, the human oversight retained, and realistic expected outcomes, without overstating or understating capability."

Exam items give a stakeholder statement or a draft explanation and ask which version is accurate, or what's wrong with it. Deciding which steps Claude should do is skill 4.2; this skill is explaining that decision honestly to the people who approve it or are affected by it.

Sources: the Claude Help Center articles on incorrect or misleading responses and on training data, and Anthropic's guidance on reducing hallucinations, checked on 20 November 2026 and paraphrased. The briefing and the examples are my own.

Running example. The customer service lead needs the shop's two owners, who have hardly used AI, to approve the complaint-handling redesign from skill 4.2.

A one-page briefing

Skill 4.4The four things the guide names, in plain language.

owner_briefing.txt (Briefing for the owners) 
Briefing for the owners: using Claude for complaint handling (one page)

What we propose
  Claude sorts complaint emails, pulls each customer's order and warranty
  details into a summary, and drafts replies for routine cases. Staff read,
  edit and approve every reply and every refund. Angry customers, payments
  outside policy and possible safety defects stay entirely with staff.

What Claude does well here
  - Sorting emails by product and problem, and summarising long threads.
  - First drafts of polite, clear replies in our house style.
  - Weekly complaint figures, calculated from the records with code.

What it can't be relied on for
  - Being right every time: it can write things that look correct but are
    wrong, such as a warranty date or a policy detail. That's why staff
    check every reply before it goes out.
  - Knowing anything we haven't given it: our latest policy changes must be
    in its reference material.
  - Judging when a customer needs a personal call, or when to make an
    exception. Those stay with staff.

What stays with people
  Every reply is approved by staff. Refund and goodwill decisions, distressed
  customers and safety reports are handled by the service lead.

What to expect
  Pilot for 6 weeks on one product range. We'll measure: time per routine
  complaint (now about 12 minutes), replies needing major edits, and
  customer satisfaction scores. We expect routine cases to get faster; we
  don't expect fewer staff hours on difficult cases. If more than one reply
  in five needs a major rewrite after week 3, we stop and review.

What we need from you
  Approval for the pilot, and agreement that the service lead owns the rules.
  • Specific to the use case: it says what Claude will do in this workflow, not what AI can do in general.
  • Limits in their terms: "a wrong warranty date in a reply", not "hallucination".
  • Oversight named: who approves what, and what never leaves people's hands.
  • Measurable expectations: a pilot, a baseline, what will be measured, and the point at which it stops.

What Claude can and can't reliably do

Skill 4.4Anthropic's own statements are a good basis.

  • It can be confidently wrong: the Help Center says Claude "can write things that might look correct but are very mistaken", including quotes that sound authoritative but aren't grounded in fact. It calls this a limitation of current generative AI models in general.
  • Not a single source of truth: Anthropic says users shouldn't rely on Claude as "a singular source of truth" and should carefully scrutinise high-stakes advice.
  • Knowledge has a date: each model has a training cutoff (June 2026 for the current models) and may not know about later events unless it searches the web or is given the information.
  • It only knows what it's given: your policies, records and decisions reach Claude only through files, Projects or connectors, and connectors only see what the person using them can see.
  • Where it's strong: drafting, summarising, sorting, explaining, and analysing data with code, at speed and at volume, with a person checking what matters.

Put the limits next to the safeguard that handles each one ("it can get a date wrong, so staff check every reply"), so the audience sees a plan rather than a risk list.

The oversight retained

Skill 4.4Saying clearly who stays responsible.

  • Name the roles: who reviews drafts, who decides exceptions, who owns the rules, who handles escalations.
  • Say what is never automated: for example payments outside policy, distressed customers and safety issues.
  • Say how problems get caught: review before sending, spot checks, a weekly look at the figures, and a way for staff to flag a bad output.
  • Don't promise oversight you won't do: if 60 replies a week need reading, plan the staff time for it (skill 4.2 on rubber-stamp review).

Realistic outcomes

Skill 4.4Expectations you can check later.

  • Start small: a pilot on part of the work, for a set period.
  • Measure against today: record the current time, error rate or satisfaction first, so improvement is a number, not an impression.
  • Say what won't change: the hard cases still take people's time; set-up and review take effort too.
  • Agree a stop rule: the result that would make you stop or rethink, decided before the pilot starts.

Overstating and understating

Skill 4.4Both are inaccurate; the exam tests both.

  • Overstating:
    • "Claude will handle complaints automatically." (Staff approve every reply.)
    • "It knows our policies." (Only what's in its reference material.)
    • "It doesn't make mistakes if we use the best model." (Any model can be confidently wrong.)
    • "It will cut the team's workload in half." (Not measured yet.)
  • Understating:
    • "It's just autocomplete; it can't really help." (It drafts, summarises and analyses well.)
    • "We can't use it because it sometimes gets things wrong." (Review is designed for exactly that.)
    • "It's only for technical people." (The workflow uses plain-language requests.)

Hiding the limits to win approval backfires the first time a reply goes wrong, and so does listing only risks to look cautious. The accurate version states both, with the safeguard for each limit.

Key takeaways

  • Explain the specific use case in plain language: what Claude will do, what it can't be relied on for, who stays responsible, and what to expect.
  • Ground the limits in Anthropic's own statements: Claude can write things that look correct but are wrong, isn't a single source of truth, has a knowledge cutoff, and knows only what it's given.
  • Pair each limit with its safeguard, and name the roles and the steps that are never automated; don't promise review you won't staff.
  • Set realistic outcomes: a pilot, a baseline, measures, what won't change, and a stop rule agreed in advance.
  • Avoid both overstating ("fully automatic", "knows everything", "never wrong") and understating ("just autocomplete", "unusable because it errs").
