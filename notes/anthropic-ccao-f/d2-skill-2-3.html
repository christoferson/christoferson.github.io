2.3: Review Depth and Escalation

How much checking a piece of AI-assisted work needs before it's used: more when mistakes are costly, public or permanent, and a qualified person's decision when the work affects people in high-risk areas.

Overview

Skill 2.3

Review Depth and Escalation: "Evaluating the risk and accountability profile of an AI-assisted work product and its intended use, including consequence of error, internal-draft versus external-delivery standards, and what must be independently verified, corrected, or escalated for human review."

Exam items describe a piece of work and where it's going, and ask how much review it needs, what to check, and whether someone else must decide. The techniques for checking are in skill 2.1 and skill 2.2; deciding which steps of a workflow Claude may do at all is skill 4.2; the wider policy and ethics picture is skill 6.3.

Raise review depth with the severity, reversibility, reach and reliance of an error

Skill 2.3How bad a mistake would be decides how hard to look.

  • Severity: what would a mistake cost? Wasted time, money, reputation, someone's safety, a legal position, a person's job or home.
  • Reversibility: can it be undone? A wrong internal note is fixed in a reply; a wrong price sent to 40,000 customers, or a commitment in a legal letter, may not be.
  • Reach: who sees or relies on it? You, your team, customers, the public, a regulator. The further it goes, the less chance anyone catches the error before it matters.
  • Reliance: will someone act on it without checking? Numbers, dates, commitments and quotes are what people act on.

Review depth should rise with each of these, and Anthropic's own guidance is to always validate critical information, especially for high-stakes decisions.

Hold external work to a higher standard than internal drafts

Skill 2.3The same text needs a different standard depending on where it goes.

  • Internal drafts: working documents, brainstorms and notes for your team. Readers know it's a draft, mistakes are cheap and caught in discussion, and a light review is enough. Anthropic's Usage Policy also excludes internal drafting from its high-risk requirements.
  • External delivery: anything a customer, partner, the public or a regulator receives. Readers take it as the organisation's word. Every factual claim needs verifying, it must meet your organisation's standards (legal wording, brand, accessibility), and often someone with authority signs it off.
  • Drafts change status: an internal draft that gets forwarded becomes external. Review it before it leaves, not just when it was first written.

Five pieces of work need five depths of review

Skill 2.3Five pieces of work, five depths of review.

Take one week of Claude use at a company: naming a new product range, summarising a meeting, announcing price changes to 40,000 customers, replying to a customer’s injury complaint, and shortlisting applicants for a job.

The questions behind each row:
  1. What happens if this is wrong, and can it be undone?
  2. Who will see or rely on it: me, my team, customers, the public, a regulator?
  3. Does it make or shape a decision about a person in a high-risk area?
  4. Which parts are claims someone will act on (numbers, dates, commitments, quotes)?

The questions at the bottom do the work. The product names and meeting notes are internal and easily corrected. The price email is external, wide-reaching and hard to retract, so every figure is checked and a manager signs off. The injury complaint carries legal and safety stakes, so it goes to the people who own that decision. The applicant shortlist falls in a high-risk area under the Usage Policy, so a qualified person must decide, as the next section explains.

In the Usage Policy's high-risk areas, a qualified person reviews and AI use is disclosed

Skill 2.3Where human review and disclosure are required.

Anthropic's Usage Policy (effective 12 November 2026) names eleven high-risk areas:

  • legal;
  • medical, including mental health;
  • finance, such as investment advice and taxes;
  • credit;
  • insurance;
  • housing;
  • employment;
  • education and credentials;
  • healthcare access;
  • public benefits and services;
  • legal status and adjudication, such as civil matters and immigration.

Anyone using Claude to make a recommendation in these areas must meet two requirements:

  • Meaningful review by a qualified person before the recommendation is delivered or acted on. "Qualified" means relevant training or experience, plus a licence where the law requires one. That person can change it, and remains responsible for its accuracy and appropriateness.
  • Disclosure: the person affected must be clearly told that AI was used to produce it. Naming Claude isn't required.

There are narrow exceptions. Some outcomes that are entirely favourable to the person, such as paying an insurance claim, may skip review where the law allows. General educational content and internal drafting are outside these rules.

Verify what people will act on, correct what you can, escalate what isn't yours

Skill 2.3What to do with each part of the work.

  • Independently verify the claims someone will act on: figures, dates, prices, names, quotes, legal or safety statements. Check them against the source or an authority, not against Claude (verification techniques).
  • Correct what you can establish yourself: a wrong figure from the source, a tone that doesn't fit, a missing caveat.
  • Escalate what isn't yours to decide:
    • decisions that need authority or expertise you don't have, such as legal positions, commitments to customers, or medical or financial advice;
    • anything in a high-risk area;
    • anything where the facts can't be confirmed.
    Escalating means a person with the right standing reviews and decides; it's not a sign the draft was bad.

"Claude's draft was accurate last time, so a quick read is enough" applies a low-stakes standard to high-stakes work. Review depth follows the consequence and the audience, not past performance.

You stay accountable for AI-assisted work

Skill 2.3Diligence in AI Fluency terms.

The exam guide's candidate stays accountable for work produced with Claude's help, and AI Fluency's Diligence competency is about exactly that: taking responsibility for AI-assisted work. The course page names three parts without defining them here; read by their names, they are:

  • Creation: using AI responsibly while making the work.
  • Transparency: being open about where AI was involved.
  • Deployment: checking the work before it goes out.

Matching review depth to the stakes is the deployment part; the Usage Policy's disclosure rule is the transparency part made mandatory for high-risk areas.

Practice questions

Exam-style questions written for these notes. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
A price-change email will go to 40,000 customers. How much review does it need?
  1. A. A quick read
  2. B. Every figure verified against the source and sign-off by someone with authority
  3. C. None if Claude was accurate last time
  4. D. Only a spell check
Answer
B
Why
External, wide-reaching and hard to retract: review depth follows the consequence.
Question 2
Scenario
Claude helps shortlist job applicants. What does the Usage Policy require?
  1. A. Nothing special
  2. B. Meaningful review by a qualified person, and disclosure that AI was used
  3. C. Only disclosure
  4. D. Only manager approval
Answer
B
Why
Employment is a high-risk area.
Question 3
Scenario
A reply to a customer’s injury complaint is drafted. What is the right handling?
  1. A. Send it after a tone check
  2. B. Escalate to the people who own legal and safety decisions
  3. C. Ask Claude to double-check
  4. D. Send as an internal draft
Answer
B
Why
Decisions needing authority you don’t have are escalated.
Question 4
Scenario
An internal draft is about to be forwarded to a partner. What should happen?
  1. A. Nothing; it was reviewed as internal
  2. B. Review it to the external standard before it leaves
  3. C. Mark it confidential
  4. D. Shorten it
Answer
B
Why
Drafts change status when they leave the team.

Key takeaways

  • Set review depth by the consequence of error: severity, reversibility, reach, and whether people will act on it.
  • Internal drafts get a light review; anything delivered outside needs every factual claim verified, organisational standards met, and often a sign-off. Drafts that get forwarded become external.
  • Verify the claims people will act on, correct what you can establish, and escalate decisions that need authority, expertise or confirmation you don't have.
  • In the Usage Policy's eleven high-risk areas (including legal, medical, finance, employment, housing and education), a qualified person must meaningfully review AI recommendations and the person affected must be told AI was used; internal drafting is excluded.
  • You stay accountable for AI-assisted work: AI Fluency's Diligence (creation, transparency, deployment).
