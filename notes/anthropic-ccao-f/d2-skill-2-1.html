2.1: Output Evaluation and Fitness for Purpose

Deciding whether a Claude draft can be used: checking it against what was asked, what the sources say and the standard the work must meet, then sorting it into ready, needs revision, or needs a person to correct it.

Overview

Skill 2.1

Output Evaluation and Fitness for Purpose: "Evaluating a Claude output against its requirements, source material, or professional standards, including accuracy, completeness, relevance, fitness for purpose, and classification as ready to use, requiring revision, or requiring human correction."

Exam items give a request, sometimes its sources, and Claude's draft, and ask what's wrong with it or what should happen next. Other Domain 2 skills go further on parts of this: spotting invented content and verifying it is skill 2.2; how much checking the work's stakes demand is skill 2.3; reshaping a good draft for its audience is skill 2.4.

Sources: the exam guide, the AI Fluency course page and Anthropic's guidance on reducing hallucinations, checked on 20 November 2026 and paraphrased. The decision rules for the three outcomes are my own reading of the guide's categories, and the worked evaluation is original.

Running example. The customer team asks Claude for an eight-question FAQ about the new 60-day returns policy for the shop's website, using only the policy document.

Three yardsticks

Skill 2.1What the output is measured against.

  • The requirements: what was asked for. The task, audience, length, format and scope in the request (skill 1.1) become the checklist.
  • The source material: what the documents actually say. Every factual statement should be traceable to them, especially numbers, dates, names and promises.
  • Professional standards: what the work must meet regardless of the request, such as house style, legal or regulatory wording, accessibility, or the norms of the profession (a financial summary, a clinical note, a press release).

A draft can pass one and fail another: faithful to the source but too long, or exactly to brief but wrong about the policy.

Four qualities to check

Skill 2.1The guide's criteria, and the question each asks.

  • Accuracy: is each statement true according to the source or reality? Errors here are the costliest, and the most convincing when the writing is fluent.
  • Completeness: is everything required there? Check against the request item by item; omissions are easy to miss because nothing looks wrong.
  • Relevance: is everything there meant to be? Extra content that is off-topic, beyond the brief, or unsupported adds risk and length.
  • Fitness for purpose: would it work where it's going, for that reader, in that channel, at that length and tone? A correct, complete, relevant draft can still be unusable.

In AI Fluency terms, this is Discernment: evaluating Claude's output critically, and feeding what you find back into the request.

A worked evaluation

Skill 2.1The four checks applied to one draft.

evaluation.txt (Evaluation notes) 
Request (abridged): a customer FAQ on the new 60-day returns policy, eight questions, plain English,
for the website, using only returns-policy-2027.pdf.

Draft returned: nine questions and answers.

ACCURACY - does every statement match the source?
  Q3 says refunds arrive "within 5 working days"; the policy says 10.           -> error
  Q7 says used items can be returned within 14 days; the policy doesn't allow it. -> error
COMPLETENESS - is everything required there?
  Nothing on faulty items and the two-year warranty, which the request listed.    -> gap
RELEVANCE - is anything there that shouldn't be?
  Q9 is about delivery times, outside the brief.                                  -> remove
FITNESS FOR PURPOSE - would it work where it's going?
  Plain English, right length for the web page, friendly tone.                    -> fine

VERDICT: requires revision. The structure and tone are usable; two factual errors, one gap and one
off-topic item can each be named precisely, so a revised request (or a quick edit) fixes them.
It is not ready to use: Q7 would promise customers something the policy doesn't allow.

The notes record what is wrong and where, not just a feeling that the draft "isn't quite right". That's what makes the next step precise: each finding is either a correction or a line to add to the request.

Ready, revise or correct

Skill 2.1The guide's three outcomes, and a way to choose.

  • Ready to use: it meets the requirements, matches the sources and fits the purpose. Minor polish is optional. "Ready" still means you have checked it; you're accountable for it either way.
  • Requires revision: the problems are specific and fixable by Claude, given better instructions or the right material: a missing section, a wrong length, an off-topic item, a statement to align with the source. Revise the request, or ask for the named changes.
  • Requires human correction: a person has to fix or decide it. Examples:
    • the answer depends on judgement or authority Claude doesn't have, such as a legal position, a commitment to a customer, or a policy question the documents don't settle;
    • the information isn't in any source Claude has, so another round would only produce more guesses;
    • repeated revisions keep failing on the same point.

The FAQ is "requires revision": its problems are concrete, and the policy document settles them. If the policy didn't say how long refunds take, that answer would need a person who knows.

"It reads well, so it's ready" is the mistake this skill tests. Fluent writing says nothing about accuracy or completeness; check against the requirements and the sources, not against how confident the draft sounds.

Making evaluation easier

Skill 2.1Set the request up so the output can be checked.

  • Ask for the evidence: have Claude quote or cite the passage behind each factual statement, so checking is a comparison rather than a search.
  • Ask for the gaps: "list anything the source doesn't cover" turns silent omissions and guesses into visible notes.
  • Use the request as the checklist: a specific request (eight questions, these topics, under 200 words each) gives you criteria you can tick off.
  • Keep sources at hand: evaluating accuracy without opening the source is just reading.

Key takeaways

  • Measure the output against three yardsticks: the requirements, the source material, and the professional standards the work must meet.
  • Check four qualities: accuracy (true to the source), completeness (nothing required missing), relevance (nothing extra), fitness for purpose (works for its reader and channel).
  • Record specific findings, not impressions; each becomes a correction or a change to the request.
  • Ready to use (meets all, after checking); requires revision (specific problems Claude can fix with better instructions or material); requires human correction (needs judgement, authority or information Claude doesn't have, or keeps failing).
  • Fluency isn't accuracy; ask Claude for citations and a list of gaps to make checking faster.
