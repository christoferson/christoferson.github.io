3.2: Model and Effort Selection

Picking the model and effort level a task actually needs: more capability and effort for complex, high-stakes work, and the faster, cheaper option when it does the job just as well, with reasons you can defend.

Overview

Skill 3.2

Model and Effort Selection: "Evaluating Claude model tiers and effort levels against a task's quality, speed, and cost requirements, including matching tier to task complexity and stakes, recognizing when the faster, lower-cost option is the right choice, and defending the selection against the alternatives."

Exam items describe a task and ask which model or effort level fits, or why another choice is worse. Choosing the capabilities (Projects, Research, Skills and so on) is skill 3.1; this skill is about how much model and how much thinking to apply.

Pick the tier by complexity and stakes; Opus 5.5 is where most work starts

Skill 3.2What each tier is for, in Anthropic's words, summarised.

  • Fable 5.2: the most capable model open to all customers, for the most demanding reasoning and work that runs for hours, such as multi-step deep research carried through to a finished document.
  • Opus 5.5: complex reasoning and long-running knowledge and coding work. Anthropic's guide says most workloads start here.
  • Sonnet 5.5: "a faster, lower-cost complement" to Opus 5.5, balancing speed and capability for everyday work such as writing, data analysis and coding.
  • Haiku 5.5: the fastest and cheapest, "designed for high-volume, cost-sensitive tasks".

In claude.ai, you switch by clicking the model name next to the send button; "More models" shows the rest. Cost to you appears as usage: more capable models and more effort use your plan's limits faster.

Effort sets how hard the model works; try it before switching models

Skill 3.2How hard the chosen model works on each reply.

  • What effort does: it sets how much thinking Claude applies to a response. More effort gives more thorough answers, but they're slower and use more tokens, so you reach usage limits sooner.
  • The levels:
    • Default: each model's recommended level, marked in the menu;
    • Low and Medium: routine work, and they stretch your usage further;
    • High: the best overall balance of quality and speed;
    • Extra high: long-running coding and agentic work, deeper than High without the full cost of Max (Opus 4.7 and newer);
    • Max: the deepest reasoning, with slower replies, for work where correctness is critical.
  • Thinking: Claude plans and explores before answering, and you can expand a summary of its reasoning. On the current models (Fable 5.2, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5 and others) thinking is always on, so for less thinking you choose lower effort. Older models have an "Extended" toggle.
  • Changing mid-chat: model, effort and thinking can all change during a conversation; the change applies from Claude's next response.

Anthropic's model guide notes that changing effort is often a better lever than switching models. Try raising or lowering effort on the same model before you move to a different one.

Choose the faster, cheaper option for simple, high-volume or easy-to-check work

Skill 3.2The guide explicitly tests recognising this.

  • Simple and repetitive: tagging, sorting, short rewrites and extraction from a clear format don't improve much with more capability, and run faster on a smaller model or lower effort.
  • High volume: doing the same small task many times multiplies the usage cost of a larger model.
  • Speed matters: a quick answer you need now is worth more than a deeper one later.
  • Easy to check: when a mistake is cheap and quick to catch, a faster option plus a spot check beats a slower one.
  • Efficiency first: Anthropic's guide describes starting with a faster model, testing, and upgrading only where you find a real gap.

"Always use the most capable model at Max" sounds safe, but it's slower and uses limits faster, with no gain on simple work. The reverse trap is a low-effort setting for a contract review or a plan with many constraints, where a missed detail is expensive.

Defend the choice against one level down and one level up

Skill 3.2Saying why your choice beats the alternatives.

  • Name the requirements: how complex the task is, what a mistake would cost, how fast you need it, and how often it runs.
  • Compare against both neighbours: why not one level down (what would it miss?) and why not one level up (what extra time or usage would it cost, for what gain?).
  • Test, don't assume: run a few real examples on two settings and compare accuracy and quality before settling on one for repeated work.
  • Effort doesn't replace review: higher effort makes careful work more likely, not certain. High-stakes output still gets the review depth from skill 2.3.
  • Know what a model change can't fix: if the problem is stale or wrong context, a bigger model won't help; fix the context instead (skill 3.3).

Organisation settings can hide models or cap effort

Skill 3.2Why a model or level might be missing.

  • Model access (Enterprise): owners turn models on or off for the whole organisation, and custom roles can get a subset. The organisation setting is the ceiling. Haiku models are always available and can't be turned off.
  • Effort caps: owners can set a maximum effort per model, for the organisation and for each custom role; levels above your cap are hidden.
  • Defaults: admins can choose the model and effort that new chats start with; you can still change both within a chat.
  • Where it applies: chat on web, desktop and mobile, Cowork, Claude Code and other Claude apps. If a model is turned off mid-conversation, that chat falls back to your default model.

Practice questions

Exam-style questions written for these notes. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
A user tags 2,000 short customer comments into five categories. Which choice fits best?
  1. A. Fable 5.2 at Max
  2. B. A fast model such as Haiku 5.5, or lower effort, with a spot check
  3. C. Opus 5.5 at Extra high
  4. D. Research
Answer
B
Why
Simple, high-volume, easy-to-check work suits the faster, cheaper option.
Question 2
Scenario
A contract review with many constraints is run at Low effort and misses a clause. What should change?
  1. A. Switch to Haiku
  2. B. Raise effort for this kind of work, since a missed detail is expensive
  3. C. Shorten the contract
  4. D. Use memory
Answer
B
Why
Effort should rise with complexity and stakes.
Question 3
Scenario
Before switching to a bigger model because answers are shallow, what should be tried first?
  1. A. Nothing
  2. B. Raising effort on the same model
  3. C. A new chat
  4. D. Turning off thinking
Answer
B
Why
Changing effort is often a better lever than switching models.
Question 4
Scenario
A model the user wants isn’t in the menu at work. Why?
  1. A. It was retired
  2. B. An Enterprise owner may have turned it off or capped access for the user’s role
  3. C. It needs a Project
  4. D. The chat is too long
Answer
B
Why
Organisation settings are the ceiling.

Key takeaways

  • Tiers: Fable 5.2 for the hardest, longest work; Opus 5.5 for complex work (where most start); Sonnet 5.5 faster and cheaper for everyday work; Haiku 5.5 fastest and cheapest for high-volume tasks.
  • Effort sets how much Claude thinks: Low and Medium for routine work, High for balance, Extra high for long agentic work, Max for correctness-critical work. More effort is slower and uses limits faster.
  • Thinking is always on in the current models; lower the effort for less. Adjusting effort is often better than switching models.
  • Faster and cheaper is right for simple, repetitive, high-volume, time-sensitive or easy-to-check work. Complex, high-stakes work justifies more.
  • Defend a choice by requirements and by both neighbours (one level down, one level up); test on real examples. Enterprise owners can restrict models and effort; Haiku can't be turned off.
