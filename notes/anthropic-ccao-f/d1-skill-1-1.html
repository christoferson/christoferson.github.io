1.1: Prompt Construction

Overview

Skill 1.1

Prompt Construction: "Applying prompt structure to a business objective, its audience, constraints, and source material, including role, context, task, output format, and the context Claude does not have by default, to produce a first response that is fit for purpose."

Exam items describe a work request and ask which prompt would produce a usable first draft, or what a weak prompt is missing. The answer is rarely "add more words"; it's adding the specific things Claude can't infer. Breaking large work into steps is skill 1.2, and working out why a prompt underperformed is skill 1.3.

Sources: Anthropic's prompting best practices, its blog post on prompt-engineering best practices, and the AI Fluency course page, checked on 20 November 2026 and paraphrased. The AI Fluency framework is by Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller with Anthropic, released under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. The prompts below are my own.

Running example. A customer-team lead at an outdoor-gear shop needs to announce a new, longer returns window to existing customers, and uses Claude in claude.ai to draft the email.

What Claude starts with

Skill 1.1The context it doesn't have by default.

Anthropic's prompting guide compares Claude to a brilliant but new employee who doesn't know your norms and workflows. In claude.ai, Claude starts with Anthropic's own system prompt and whatever you've set up yourself: your preferences, a Project's instructions and files, or memory if it's turned on. Unless you supply them, it does not know:

  • your organisation's specifics: policies, products, names, numbers;
  • who will read the output, and what they already know;
  • what happened before: past decisions, what went wrong last time, why this matters now;
  • your constraints: length, tone, what must not be said, deadlines;
  • what "good" looks like to you.

Without these, Claude fills the gaps with plausible generic choices. That's why a short request gets a fluent but generic draft.

The parts of a fit-for-purpose prompt

Skill 1.1The guide's elements, and what each contributes.

  • Role or perspective: who Claude is helping and in what capacity. Anthropic's blog cautions against overly narrow roles; stating the perspective you want ("you're helping the customer team write to existing customers") usually works better.
  • Context: the facts it can't know, and the purpose. Saying why something matters ("a confusing email last year caused 200 support tickets") lets Claude apply that reason to choices you didn't spell out.
  • Task: one clear, explicit request. Ask for exactly what you want rather than hoping it will be inferred.
  • Constraints: length, tone, exclusions and deadlines, each with its reason. Say what to do rather than only what to avoid.
  • Output format: the shape you need (a subject line and a body, a table, three options), so the draft drops into your work without reformatting.
  • Permission to be uncertain: asking Claude to say when it isn't sure, or when the material doesn't cover something, instead of guessing. Anthropic's blog notes this reduces made-up content.

A request, before and after

Skill 1.1The same task, specified.

first_try.txt (Prompt, first try) 
Write an email to our customers about the new returns policy.

This will produce an email, but Claude has to guess the new terms, the audience, the length and the tone. It will probably invent a cheerful marketing message about a policy it hasn't seen.

fit_for_purpose.txt (Prompt, fit for purpose) 
You're helping the customer team at an outdoor-gear shop write to existing customers.

Context you wouldn't otherwise know:
- From 1 January, unused items can be returned within 60 days instead of 30. Worn or used
  items still can't be returned, except for faults, which our two-year warranty covers.
- The full policy is attached as returns-policy-2027.pdf. Use only what it says.
- Our customers are hikers and campers, mostly regulars. Last year a confusing policy email
  led to over 200 support tickets, so clarity matters more than marketing polish.

Task: write the announcement email.

Constraints:
- Under 180 words, so it reads on a phone.
- Plain, friendly tone; no exclamation marks or sales language, because this is a service
  message, not a promotion.
- Don't mention competitors or promise anything the policy doesn't say.

Output: a subject line, then the email body, then a one-line note listing anything in the
policy you weren't sure how to explain. If something I asked for isn't covered by the policy,
say so instead of filling it in.
  • Perspective and purpose: who it's for, and why clarity beats polish here.
  • The facts Claude couldn't know: the change, the exceptions, the warranty.
  • The source: the attached policy, with an instruction to use only what it says.
  • Constraints with reasons: the length because readers are on phones, the tone because it's a service message.
  • An exact output shape, and a request to flag uncertainty rather than fill gaps.

It's longer, but each line removes a guess. Anthropic's suggested check: would a colleague with no background produce the right email from it? If not, Claude won't either.

Source material

Skill 1.1Giving Claude the facts instead of asking it to recall them.

  • Attach or paste the policy, report or data the answer depends on, and say whether Claude should use only that material. Otherwise it may mix in general knowledge that doesn't match your organisation.
  • Put long material first: Anthropic's guidance is to place long documents before the question.
  • Label each source if there are several ("the attached policy", "last year's email for tone only"), so Claude knows how to use each one.
  • Reuse it: for material you'll use repeatedly, a Project's knowledge saves attaching it every time (Domain 5).

Description in AI Fluency

Skill 1.1The competency this skill puts into practice.

The exam guide builds on Anthropic's AI Fluency framework and its four competencies: Delegation, Description, Discernment and Diligence. Prompt construction is Description, which the course splits into three kinds. The course page names them; the explanations below are my reading of the names:

  • Product description: what you want, and its qualities: the deliverable, format, audience and style.
  • Process description: how to approach the work, such as the steps to follow or the sources to rely on.
  • Performance description: how Claude should behave while helping, for example concise, or flagging what it isn't sure of.

The "fit for purpose" prompt above contains all three. Description is paired with Discernment, judging the output, in what the course calls the Description–Discernment loop: describe, evaluate the result, refine the description (Domain 2).

Checking the first response

Skill 1.1What "fit for purpose" means here.

  • Does it meet the purpose and audience? Would the reader understand it and know what to do?
  • Does it stay within the constraints? Length, tone and exclusions.
  • Is every fact from the source? Look especially at numbers, dates and promises.
  • Did Claude flag gaps? If it noted something the policy doesn't cover, that's the prompt working as intended.

If the draft misses, the fix is usually in the prompt: a missing fact, an unstated constraint, an unclear audience. That diagnosis is skill 1.3.

Key takeaways

  • Claude doesn't know your organisation's facts, audience, history, constraints or standards unless you supply them; generic output usually means missing context.
  • A fit-for-purpose prompt states the perspective, the context and purpose (with the why), one explicit task, constraints with their reasons, the output format, and permission to flag uncertainty.
  • Give the source material and say whether to use only it; long material goes before the request.
  • In AI Fluency terms this is Description: product, process and performance.
  • Judge the first response against purpose, audience, constraints and sources; fix misses in the prompt.
