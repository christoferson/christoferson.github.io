Reference architecture

Encrypted hybrid connectivity: MACsec and private IP VPN

PCI DSS requires card data between the US data center and the payment VPC to be encrypted on every hop. The travel company layers three controls: MACsec on every dedicated Direct Connect link, a private IP VPN over the transit VIFs that ends on the Transit Gateway, and TLS from the payment service to the card vault. Separate route tables make the card path fail closed, and Encryption Controls and CloudWatch provide the evidence.

Scenario and requirements

The travel company (example.com) connects its US data center to us-east-1 with the four 10 Gbps dedicated connections built on the resilient Direct Connect page, ordered with MACsec requested. Payment services run in the payments VPC vpc-0payment01 (10.40.48.0/21), attached to tgw-use1. The card vault stays in the data center at 10.100.20.5. Direct Connect is private but not encrypted, and the PCI DSS assessor wants encryption on every hop that carries card data, with evidence (Task 4.3).

RequirementDesign choice

Card data never falls back to a clear pathA pci Transit Gateway route table that only the VPN attachments and the payments VPC use

The numbers used on this page, consistent with the hub and Direct Connect pages:

  • Connections dxcon-us1a, dxcon-us1b (US location 1) and dxcon-us2a, dxcon-us2b (US location 2), with transit VIFs dxvif-us1a to dxvif-us2b to dxgw-travel-global (AS 64512). Routers us-dc1-r1 and us-dc1-r2, AS 65010.
  • Transit Gateway tgw-use1 (ASN 64521); its Direct Connect gateway attachment is tgw-attach-dxgw, associated with onprem and propagated into prod (hub page).
  • New on this page: Transit Gateway CIDR block 10.255.10.0/24, router loopbacks 10.100.255.1 (r1) and 10.100.255.2 (r2), and the vault subnet 10.100.20.0/24. The European data center follows the same pattern and is not repeated.

How it works

The payment service at 10.40.50.20 sends a card number to the vault, then two other cases.

  1. A card payment
  2. TLS sessionPayments teamThe service opens TLS 1.2 or later to vault.dc.example.com (10.100.20.5). The payload is encrypted from here to the vault, whatever the network does.
  3. Into the pci tableAWSThe VPC route table sends 10.0.0.0/8 to tgw-use1. The payments attachment is associated with the pci table, where 10.100.20.0/24 points to the two private IP VPN attachments (ECMP). There is no route to the Direct Connect gateway attachment in this table.
  4. IPsec on the Transit GatewayAWSThe Transit Gateway encrypts the packet into ESP. The outer header runs from a tunnel address in 10.255.10.0/24 to the router loopback 10.100.255.1.
  5. Over the transit VIFsAWSThe outer packet leaves through the transport attachment tgw-attach-dxgw and the Direct Connect gateway, on one of the four transit VIFs chosen by ECMP.
  6. MACsec on the cross connectAWSThe AWS Direct Connect device encrypts the whole Ethernet frame, VLAN tag included, with GCM-AES-XPN-256. The router decrypts it at line rate.
  7. Into the data centerNetwork teamThe router ends the IPsec tunnel on its loopback and forwards the inner packet to the vault, where TLS ends.
  8. A booking flow that is not card data
  9. Direct Connect onlyAWSA booking pod at 10.40.12.30 uses the prod table, where 10.100.0.0/16 points to tgw-attach-dxgw. Its traffic gets MACsec on the cross connect and no IPsec, at full 10 Gbps per link and 8500-byte MTU.
  10. A MACsec key mismatch on one link
  11. The link stopsAWSWith must_encrypt, dxcon-us1b carries no traffic until the keys match. BGP on dxvif-us1b drops, ECMP continues over the other three links, and the IPsec tunnels move with it.
  12. The alarm firesAWSConnectionEncryptionState for dxcon-us1b drops to 0 and the network on-call is paged.

Build it

Step 1

MACsec keys on the four dedicated connections

MACsec (IEEE 802.1AE) encrypts at layer 2 between the router and the AWS device on the cross connect. It needs dedicated 10, 100 or 400 Gbps connections at a location that supports it, so 1 Gbps and hosted connections are out. The connections were ordered with --request-mac-sec, so check macSecCapable first (Task 1.5).

  • Ciphers: 256-bit keys only. At 10 Gbps, GCM-AES-256 or GCM-AES-XPN-256. At 100 and 400 Gbps, XPN (64-bit packet numbers) is required. The company uses XPN everywhere so a later 100 Gbps upgrade keeps the same router template.
  • Keys: static CAK mode only. You supply a CKN/CAK pair, each exactly 64 hexadecimal characters. AWS stores the pair in Secrets Manager under an AWS managed key and derives and rotates the SAK automatically.
  • Keychain: up to three CKN/CAK pairs per connection or LAG, for manual rotation. The device tries the newest key and falls back to the previous one.
Generate one key pair per connection and associate it with dxcon-us1a (bash on a secured admin host, AWS CLI, network account, us-east-1) 
# 32 random bytes = 64 hex characters; keep them out of shell history and tickets
CKN=$(openssl rand -hex 32)
CAK=$(openssl rand -hex 32)

# AWS stores the pair in Secrets Manager; the response returns its secretARN
aws directconnect associate-mac-sec-key \
  --connection-id dxcon-us1a \
  --ckn "$CKN" --cak "$CAK"

# hand the same pair to the router team over the approved secure channel,
# then repeat for dxcon-us1b, dxcon-us2a and dxcon-us2b with their own pairs
Check all four connections before changing the mode (AWS CLI) 
# expect macSecCapable true, key state associated, and "Encryption Up" once the router matches
for c in dxcon-us1a dxcon-us1b dxcon-us2a dxcon-us2b; do
  aws directconnect describe-connections --connection-id "$c" \
    --query "connections[0].[connectionId,macSecCapable,encryptionMode,portEncryptionStatus,macSecKeys[].state]" \
    --output text
done

The --secret-arn option associates an existing MACsec secret instead of a new pair. LAG: if a path later grows into a LAG, the key is associated with the LAG ID (dxlag-...). Every member must be MACsec-capable and the same speed, one key is active across all members, and a connection added to the LAG loses its own keys and takes the LAG key.

Step 2

Encryption mode must_encrypt, and the router side

The encryption mode decides what happens when MACsec cannot come up:

  • should_encrypt (the default) falls back to unencrypted traffic. The link stays up and nobody notices, unless the metrics are watched.
  • must_encrypt passes no traffic without MACsec. The link fails closed and BGP fails over to the other paths.
  • no_encrypt is the third API value; it is not used here.

Order: associate the key, configure the router, confirm Encryption Up, then set must_encrypt. One connection at a time.

Switch one connection to must_encrypt after MACsec is up on it (AWS CLI) 
# only after portEncryptionStatus shows "Encryption Up" for this connection
aws directconnect update-connection \
  --connection-id dxcon-us1a \
  --encryption-mode must_encrypt

# confirm, then move on to dxcon-us1b, us2a and us2b one by one
aws directconnect describe-connections --connection-id dxcon-us1a \
  --query "connections[0].encryptionMode"

Disassociating the last CKN from a connection in must_encrypt silently changes the mode to should_encrypt. A careless key rotation can turn the control off, so always add the new key before removing the old one, and alarm on the mode as well as the state.

The router. For MACsec-enabled VIFs the downloaded router configuration includes MACsec for Cisco Nexus 9K and later on NX-OS 9.3 or later, and Juniper M/MX on JunOS 9.5 or later. Other platforms are configured by hand with the parameters below. SCI must be on, and the VLAN tag cannot be left outside the encrypted payload.

MACsec parameters for us-dc1-r1 on the dxcon-us1a port (parameter sheet for the router team, not vendor syntax) 
# pre-shared key, static CAK mode (no dynamic CAK)
CKN                     = <64 hex characters, same value as associated in Step 1>
CAK                     = <64 hex characters, same value as associated in Step 1>
key algorithm           = AES_256_CMAC
SAK cipher              = GCM_AES_XPN_256   # GCM_AES_256 also allowed at 10 Gbps
key server cipher suite = 16
confidentiality offset  = 0
ICV indicator           = No
SCI                     = on (required)
SAK rekey               = on packet number rollover
VLAN tag in clear       = no (dot1q-in-clear not supported)

Key rotation uses the keychain: associate the new pair, add it to the router, confirm the newest key is in use, then remove the old pair from both sides.

Step 3

A Transit Gateway CIDR block in the allowed prefixes

MACsec ends at the AWS device at the Direct Connect location, so the next layer must end inside AWS. A private IP VPN runs IPsec over the transit VIFs to the Transit Gateway, with private addresses on both ends of each tunnel (RFC 1918 or RFC 6598). The AWS tunnel endpoints come from a Transit Gateway CIDR block: /24 or larger for IPv4, up to five blocks per Transit Gateway (not adjustable). The pool is shared with Connect peers and Client VPN endpoints. The block must not overlap any other attachment's addresses.

Add a CIDR block for the private IP VPN endpoints to the Virginia Transit Gateway (AWS CLI, network account) 
# 10.255.10.0/24 is outside every VPC and data center range in the company plan
aws ec2 modify-transit-gateway --region us-east-1 \
  --transit-gateway-id tgw-use1 \
  --options AddTransitGatewayCidrBlocks=10.255.10.0/24

The data center routers must learn that block over the transit VIFs. For a Transit Gateway association, the Direct Connect gateway advertises the allowed prefixes, not the attachments behind them. So the block is added next to the Region summary 10.40.0.0/14. Without it, the routers have no route to the tunnel endpoints and the tunnels never come up.

Advertise the Transit Gateway CIDR block to the data centers (AWS CLI; the association ID is a placeholder) 
# the association between dxgw-travel-global and tgw-use1
aws directconnect update-direct-connect-gateway-association \
  --association-id a1b2c3d4-0000-0000-0000-dxgwtgwuse1 \
  --add-allowed-prefixes-to-direct-connect-gateway cidr=10.255.10.0/24

# afterwards each US transit VIF should advertise 10.255.10.0/24 too
aws directconnect list-virtual-interface-routes \
  --virtual-interface-id dxvif-us1a \
  --filters routeDirection=advertised,addressFamily=ipv4
Step 4

Two private IP VPN connections, one per router

Each router gets its own customer gateway whose IP address is a private loopback, not the VIF's point-to-point address. AWS recommends a loopback or LAN interface as the tunnel source. A loopback is reachable over all four VIFs (10.100.0.0/16 is advertised on each), so when a VIF fails the outer packets take another path; a point-to-point address would tie the tunnel to one link.

Customer gateways on the router loopbacks and the first private IP VPN (AWS CLI, network account, us-east-1) 
# private loopback addresses, BGP AS of the US data center
aws ec2 create-customer-gateway --type ipsec.1 \
  --ip-address 10.100.255.1 --bgp-asn 65010
aws ec2 create-customer-gateway --type ipsec.1 \
  --ip-address 10.100.255.2 --bgp-asn 65010

# VPN over the Direct Connect gateway attachment, private outside IPs, BGP, IKEv2, AES-GCM
aws ec2 create-vpn-connection \
  --type ipsec.1 \
  --customer-gateway-id cgw-us-dc1-r1-pip \
  --transit-gateway-id tgw-use1 \
  --pre-shared-key-storage SecretsManager \
  --options '{
    "OutsideIpAddressType": "PrivateIpv4",
    "TransportTransitGatewayAttachmentId": "tgw-attach-dxgw",
    "TunnelBandwidth": "standard",
    "StaticRoutesOnly": false,
    "TunnelOptions": [
      { "TunnelInsideCidr": "169.254.40.0/30",
        "IKEVersions": [{"Value": "ikev2"}],
        "Phase1EncryptionAlgorithms": [{"Value": "AES256-GCM-16"}],
        "Phase2EncryptionAlgorithms": [{"Value": "AES256-GCM-16"}],
        "Phase1DHGroupNumbers": [{"Value": 20}],
        "Phase2DHGroupNumbers": [{"Value": 20}],
        "StartupAction": "start",
        "DPDTimeoutAction": "restart" },
      { "TunnelInsideCidr": "169.254.40.4/30",
        "IKEVersions": [{"Value": "ikev2"}],
        "Phase1EncryptionAlgorithms": [{"Value": "AES256-GCM-16"}],
        "Phase2EncryptionAlgorithms": [{"Value": "AES256-GCM-16"}],
        "Phase1DHGroupNumbers": [{"Value": 20}],
        "Phase2DHGroupNumbers": [{"Value": 20}],
        "StartupAction": "start",
        "DPDTimeoutAction": "restart" }
    ]
  }'
# the second VPN uses cgw-us-dc1-r2-pip and inside CIDRs 169.254.40.8/30 and .12/30

TransportTransitGatewayAttachmentId is required with PrivateIpv4: it names the Direct Connect gateway attachment the tunnels ride on. Acceleration does not apply, and VPN Concentrators are not supported.

Capacity. A standard tunnel carries up to 1.25 Gbps. With BGP and VpnEcmpSupport on the Transit Gateway, both connections' four tunnels share the load, about 5 Gbps in total. ECMP balances flows, not packets, so one flow stays within one tunnel. Card traffic peaks well below 1 Gbps, so standard is enough; "large" gives up to 5 Gbps per tunnel, works with private IP VPN, and since May 2026 can be set on an existing VPN.

us-dc1-r1: tunnel sourced from the loopback, MTU and MSS for IPsec (Cisco IOS-style; the IKE and IPsec profiles come from the downloaded VPN configuration) 
ip prefix-list VAULT-ONLY permit 10.100.20.0/24
!
interface Loopback10
 description private IP VPN source, inside 10.100.0.0/16
 ip address 10.100.255.1 255.255.255.255
!
interface Tunnel1
 description to tgw-use1 tunnel 1 (outside address from 10.255.10.0/24)
 ip address 169.254.40.2 255.255.255.252
 ! IPsec allows at most 1446 bytes inside, even over an 8500-byte VIF
 ip mtu 1446
 ip tcp adjust-mss 1406
 tunnel source Loopback10
 ! AWS outside address for tunnel 1, from the VPN configuration (inside 10.255.10.0/24)
 tunnel destination 10.255.10.10
 tunnel mode ipsec ipv4
!
router bgp 65010
 neighbor 169.254.40.1 remote-as 64521
 address-family ipv4
  ! advertise only the vault subnet into the tunnel
  network 10.100.20.0 mask 255.255.255.0
  neighbor 169.254.40.1 prefix-list VAULT-ONLY out

The 1446/1406 values are the maximum, with AES-GCM and no NAT traversal. CBC ciphers lower them, and there is no Path MTU Discovery inside the tunnel, so clamp MSS rather than rely on ICMP.

Step 5

A card-data route table that fails closed

The Transit Gateway prefers a Direct Connect gateway route over a VPN route for the same prefix (static, then VPC, Direct Connect gateway, Connect, private IP VPN, VPN). A longer prefix still wins first. If the payments VPC stayed in prod, the VPN's 10.100.20.0/24 would beat the 10.100.0.0/16 from Direct Connect, so the card path would look right. But when both VPNs dropped, it would fall back to the unencrypted Direct Connect route without any error. So the design keeps the two paths in different tables. The private IP VPN documentation calls this out: the VPN attachment's table can differ from the Direct Connect attachment's table, so encrypted and unencrypted traffic flow side by side.

The pci table: payments VPC and the two private IP VPN attachments only (AWS CLI, network account) 
aws ec2 create-transit-gateway-route-table --transit-gateway-id tgw-use1 \
  --tag-specifications 'ResourceType=transit-gateway-route-table,Tags=[{Key=Name,Value=pci}]'

# the payments VPC and both VPN attachments look up their destinations here
for a in tgw-attach-payment tgw-attach-pci-vpn1 tgw-attach-pci-vpn2; do
  aws ec2 associate-transit-gateway-route-table \
    --transit-gateway-route-table-id tgw-rtb-pci \
    --transit-gateway-attachment-id "$a"
  aws ec2 enable-transit-gateway-route-table-propagation \
    --transit-gateway-route-table-id tgw-rtb-pci \
    --transit-gateway-attachment-id "$a"
done
# the payments attachment must first be disassociated from prod (one table per attachment)

The return direction needs the same care. If the payments VPC still propagated into onprem, a data center host could reach it over plain Direct Connect. Removing that propagation means a packet arriving on tgw-attach-dxgw for 10.40.48.0/21 finds no route and is dropped. The routers learn 10.40.48.0/21 over the tunnels and only the summary 10.40.0.0/14 over Direct Connect, so longest prefix match sends vault traffic into the tunnels as well.

Stop the payments VPC being reachable over the unencrypted path (AWS CLI) 
# onprem is the table the Direct Connect gateway attachment uses
aws ec2 disable-transit-gateway-route-table-propagation \
  --transit-gateway-route-table-id tgw-rtb-onprem \
  --transit-gateway-attachment-id tgw-attach-payment

# then check tgw-rtb-pci in the console (or with search-transit-gateway-routes):
# 10.100.20.0/24 via the two VPN attachments, nothing via tgw-attach-dxgw
Step 6

Proof: MACsec and tunnel alarms, Encryption Controls and flow logs

Three signals cover the three layers. AWS/DX ConnectionEncryptionState is 1 when MACsec is up and 0 when it is down (for a LAG, 0 means at least one member is down). AWS/VPN TunnelState is 1 when up; for a connection, a value between 0 and 1 means one tunnel is down (Task 1.4). Inside the VPC, VPC Encryption Controls in monitor mode adds the encryption-status flow log field.

Page when MACsec drops on a US connection (AWS CLI; one alarm per connection) 
aws cloudwatch put-metric-alarm \
  --alarm-name dx-us1a-macsec-down \
  --namespace AWS/DX --metric-name ConnectionEncryptionState \
  --dimensions Name=ConnectionId,Value=dxcon-us1a \
  --statistic Minimum --period 300 --evaluation-periods 1 \
  --threshold 1 --comparison-operator LessThanThreshold \
  --alarm-actions arn:aws:sns:us-east-1:111111111111:network-oncall

A daily job also flags any connection whose encryptionMode is no longer must_encrypt.

Encryption Controls on the payments VPC and a flow log with the encryption-status field (AWS CLI, payments account) 
# an existing VPC goes to monitor first; the command takes no mode
aws ec2 create-vpc-encryption-control --vpc-id vpc-0payment01

# version 10 field: 0 not encrypted, 1 Nitro, 2 application, 3 both, "-" controls off
aws ec2 create-flow-logs --resource-type VPC --resource-ids vpc-0payment01 \
  --traffic-type ALL --max-aggregation-interval 60 \
  --log-destination-type s3 \
  --log-destination arn:aws:s3:::travel-log-archive/flow-logs/ \
  --log-format '${version} ${account-id} ${interface-id} ${srcaddr} ${dstaddr} ${srcport} ${dstport} ${protocol} ${packets} ${bytes} ${start} ${end} ${action} ${flow-direction} ${traffic-path} ${reject-reason} ${encryption-status}'

Read the field carefully. It reports what AWS can see inside the VPC: Nitro encryption between supported instances, and application encryption inferred for TCP 443 to interface and gateway endpoints. Flows from the payment service toward the Transit Gateway are expected to show 0, because IPsec starts at the Transit Gateway and AWS does not inspect TLS. The audit lists every 0 flow: TLS flows to 10.100.20.0/24 are explained by the design, anything else is a finding. Encryption Controls costs $0.15 per non-empty VPC-hour in us-east-1, about $110 a month for this VPC.

Design decisions

The four options compared, for card data from the US data center:

OptionWhere encryption endsFits when
1. MACsecThe AWS Direct Connect device, one hopDedicated 10, 100 or 400 Gbps; line rate; not 1 Gbps or hosted
2. Private IP VPNThe Transit GatewayTransit VIF to a Transit Gateway; any connection type or speed; 1.25 or 5 Gbps per tunnel
3. VPN over a public VIFThe VPN endpoint, on public IPsOlder designs or a virtual private gateway; needs public addresses
4. Application TLSThe application, end to endEvery card-data protocol supports TLS; always needed for the hops inside AWS

Why MACsec alone is not enough here

MACsec is hop by hop. It protects the cross connect between the router and the AWS device, then the frame is decrypted. The path from the Direct Connect device to the Transit Gateway, and from the Transit Gateway to the VPC, is outside its reach. AWS encrypts traffic between its facilities and Regions at the physical layer, but this page could not confirm that coverage for the Direct Connect location to Region leg. The assessor also wanted encryption the company controls, ending inside AWS. So MACsec is the baseline for all traffic, and the card path adds IPsec and TLS.

Why not put all traffic into the private IP VPN

Four standard tunnels give about 5 Gbps, against 40 Gbps of Direct Connect, with an MTU of 1446 instead of 8500. Only card data needs the overlay, and the separate pci table lets the two run side by side.

Fail closed, even in a total Direct Connect outage

The private IP VPN rides on the transit VIFs, so it goes down with them. The internet backup VPN from the Direct Connect page is associated with onprem, which no longer holds the payments VPC. So card traffic stops instead of moving to another path. With four links at two locations this is a rare case, and the company accepts it.

Transit Gateway encryption support: not on the shared hub

After IPsec ends at the Transit Gateway, the hop into the payments VPC is protected by TLS. Transit Gateway encryption support, used with VPC Encryption Controls, is the AWS-side option, but it would cost a lot on tgw-use1. Every VPC attached to an encryption-enabled Transit Gateway is billed for Encryption Controls, whatever its mode, even when empty. If 100 VPCs were attached, that would be 100 × $0.15 × 730 ≈ $10,950 a month. Enabling it can also take up to 14 days, and it is not supported with Connect attachments (see Task 4.3). The payments VPC stays in monitor mode; enforce would also be blocked by Network Firewall or Gateway Load Balancer endpoints.

Cost

MACsec has no extra charge; it only needs ports that support it, and these 10 Gbps ports already exist. Two standard VPN connections cost about 2 × $0.05 × 730 ≈ $73 a month (the pricing page's US East (Ohio) example rate). With Large tunnels it would be 2 × $0.60 × 730 ≈ $876. The VPN attachments add Transit Gateway attachment-hour and data processing charges; those rates were not confirmed here. Direct Connect data transfer out is unchanged apart from the ESP overhead. Encryption Controls on the payments VPC adds about $110 a month.

Alternatives

VPN over a public VIF

The older pattern: a public VIF carries a standard Site-to-Site VPN to the public endpoints of a virtual private gateway or Transit Gateway. It works with a virtual private gateway, but it needs public IP addresses (more exposure to denial of service), and the public VIF opens all AWS public endpoints to the data center. Acceleration cannot be used over a public VIF. A private IP VPN avoids all three problems and offers more route scale (5,000 routes out and 1,000 in, against 200 and 100 on Direct Connect alone).

TLS only, no network encryption

If every card-data protocol used TLS 1.2 or later with strong policies and mutual TLS, TLS alone could meet the requirement, and TLS is already mandatory on this page. The overlay exists for the protocols that cannot use TLS easily: a legacy batch transfer to the vault and database replication monitoring. If those move to TLS, the private IP VPN can be retired.

Hosted connections or a 1 Gbps port

Neither supports MACsec, so private IP VPN is the network-layer option: the classic exam case.

Testing it

  1. Baseline the encryption stateFor all four connections, describe-connections must show must_encrypt, Encryption Up and keys associated, and ConnectionEncryptionState must be 1. Both VPNs must show TunnelState 1, and tgw-rtb-pci must hold 10.100.20.0/24 from both VPN attachments and nothing from tgw-attach-dxgw.
  2. Prove must_encrypt drops traffic, in a labOn a lab connection, set should_encrypt and put a wrong CAK on the router: the link passes traffic in clear and portEncryptionStatus shows Encryption Down. That is why the default is unsafe. Then set must_encrypt with the same mismatch: no traffic, BGP down, and the alarm fires. Restore the right key and traffic returns. Never run this first on a production link.
  3. Rotate a keyIn a maintenance window on dxcon-us1b, associate a second pair, update the router, and confirm the newest key is in use with no BGP flap. Then remove the old pair, and check that the mode is still must_encrypt.
  4. Fail over between encrypted pathsRun start-bgp-failover-test on dxvif-us1a. The tunnels sourced from the loopbacks should stay up while their outer packets move to the other VIFs (watch TunnelState and IKE logs), and card transactions should continue. Then shut Tunnel1 and Tunnel2 on us-dc1-r1: ECMP must move card flows to r2's VPN within the BGP hold time.
  5. Prove the card path fails closedIn a window, take both VPNs down. Card traffic must stop, not move to Direct Connect. search-transit-gateway-routes on tgw-rtb-pci returns no route to 10.100.20.0/24. A traceroute from the vault subnet toward 10.40.50.20 dies at the Transit Gateway, and Reachability Analyzer from the payment service ENI toward tgw-attach-dxgw, destination 10.100.20.5, reports no path.
  6. Check MTU and the flow logsFrom the vault subnet, a DF-bit ping of 1418 bytes of payload (1446 total) to 10.40.50.20 succeeds, and 1419 fails. A packet capture shows SYNs with MSS 1406. Run the encryption-status audit query from Step 6 and confirm that every 0 flow matches a documented TLS flow.

"Encrypt all traffic on 10 or 100 Gbps dedicated Direct Connect connections at line rate" is MACsec with must_encrypt. "Encrypt Direct Connect traffic to a Transit Gateway without public IP addresses", or over a 1 Gbps or hosted connection, is private IP VPN over a transit VIF, with the Transit Gateway CIDR block in the allowed prefixes. "The private IP VPN tunnels never come up" points to a Transit Gateway CIDR block missing from the allowed prefixes. "MACsec traffic is flowing but the key does not match" points to should_encrypt. "Encryption on every hop, end to end" adds TLS, because MACsec is hop by hop and IPsec ends at the Transit Gateway.
