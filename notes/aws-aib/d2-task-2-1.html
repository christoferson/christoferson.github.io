Task 2.1: Develop AI strategies that align with business objectives

Turning "we should use AI" into a portfolio of the right initiatives: finding use cases that move business outcomes, deciding whether to build, buy or partner, ranking initiatives and deciding which to scale, pause or stop, recognizing problems AI shouldn't solve, and moving processes onto AI, or from one AI platform to another, without disrupting the business.

Overview

Task 2.1

Develop AI strategies that align with business objectives.

An AI strategy is not a list of AI projects. It is a set of decisions about which business outcomes AI should improve, which initiatives to fund and in what order, how to source each one, and when to stop. Without those decisions, organizations end up with many disconnected pilots, each started because a tool was available or a team was enthusiastic. Few of them reach production, and none are measured against what the business actually needs.

The common thread in this task is starting from the business objective, not the technology. Every skill below applies that idea to a different decision: what to do, how to source it, what to do first, what not to do with AI at all, and how to change over safely.

Identifying high-impact use cases

Skill 2.1.1Identify high-impact AI use cases across business functions and map AI capabilities to specific business outcomes

Use case

A specific business task that AI will perform or improve, with a measurable outcome. "Draft the after-call notes for contact-center agents, saving about 90 seconds per call" is a use case: you can build it, price it and measure it. "Use AI in the contact center" is not; it is a wish, and nobody can tell when it has succeeded.

AWS's Machine Learning Lens calls business goal identification the most important phase of the ML life cycle: an organization should know the problem it is solving and the value to be gained, measurable against specific success criteria (AWS Well-Architected Machine Learning Lens, Business goal identification).

Business outcome

The measurable change the business wants, expressed in its own terms: days saved, cost per transaction, revenue retained, losses avoided, customer satisfaction. An outcome is what leadership tracks; a use case is one way to move it. Mapping a capability to an outcome means being able to say "this AI does X, which changes measure Y by about Z".

Working from objective to use case

Finding good use cases means starting with what the business is trying to achieve and tracing where it loses time, money or quality. A mortgage lender that wants to approve home loans faster shows the method. (A mortgage is a loan to buy a home; before approving it, the lender checks the applicant's income, savings and debts from documents such as pay slips and bank statements.)

  1. Start with a business objective"Cut the time from application to loan decision from 30 days to 15." Applicants who wait too long take their business to a faster lender.
  2. Map the process and measure each stepFollow applications through each step and time how long they wait. About 6 of the 30 days are spent waiting for processors to retype figures from the applicant's pay slips and bank statements into the loan system; about 4 more are spent waiting for an underwriter (the person who decides whether to lend) to read the whole file.
  3. Ask what kind of capability would remove each delayRetyping figures from documents is a job for extraction: AI that reads the document and fills in the fields. Reading long files is a job for summarization: AI that condenses the file into the points the underwriter needs.
  4. Define the use case and how success is measured"Extract income and balance figures from applicants' documents automatically, so applications no longer wait 6 days for retyping, with at least 98% of fields correct."
  5. Check data and constraintsAre the documents digital? (Mostly uploaded PDFs and phone photos.) Do privacy and lending regulations limit how they are processed? Are there enough past applications, with correctly typed figures, to test against?

Working backwards from the customer or business outcome, rather than forwards from a technology, is how Amazon itself approaches new products, and it is the most reliable way to find use cases that matter.

Mapping AI capabilities to business outcomes

Most business use cases are built from a small set of AI capabilities. Knowing them helps translate a business problem ("staff retype documents") into the kind of AI that solves it ("extraction"), and then into the outcome it moves.

Predict and score

Estimates how likely an outcome is, as a probability. Used where a decision depends on a risk.

Business outcomeLower losses and better targeting of effort.

ExampleA mobile phone company scores every customer each month for the likelihood of leaving when their contract ends, so retention offers go to the 5% most at risk instead of to everyone.

Forecast

Predicts future volumes or values from past patterns and current signals.

Business outcomeThe right number of staff, stock or capacity at the right time.

ExampleA grocery chain forecasts demand for each fresh product in each store for the next three days, using sales history, weather and local events, so it orders enough strawberries for a sunny weekend without throwing away unsold ones on Monday.

Extract

Reads documents, images or audio and turns them into structured data: named fields a system can store, check and use.

Business outcomeFaster processing and fewer typing errors.

ExampleThe mortgage lender above reads employer, monthly pay and account balances from applicants' documents straight into the loan system.

Classify and route

Sorts items into categories so each goes to the right place.

Business outcomeFaster handling, and the right team gets the item first time.

ExampleA software company sorts incoming support tickets into billing, bug, account and complaint, and sends each to the matching team instead of a person reading and forwarding each one.

Summarize

Condenses long content into the key points for a particular reader.

Business outcomeStaff time saved and faster decisions.

ExampleA hospital summarizes a patient's history, recent test results and current medicines on one screen before each appointment, so the doctor doesn't spend the first ten minutes reading notes.

Generate

Drafts new content such as letters, emails, descriptions or code.

Business outcomeHigher productivity and more consistent communication.

ExampleAn online retailer drafts product descriptions for 10,000 new items a season from the supplier's specification sheets; a copywriter reviews and approves each one.

Search and answer

Finds information in documents and explains it in plain language, with sources.

Business outcomeFaster, more accurate answers and fewer escalations to specialists.

ExampleA company's IT helpdesk assistant answers "How do I connect to the office network from home?" from the internal guides, linking to the page it used.

Act (agents)

Completes a multi-step task by deciding the steps and using other systems.

Business outcomeWhole tasks done end to end, including outside office hours.

ExampleWhen a flight is cancelled, an airline's agent finds each passenger's options, rebooks them on the best alternative, issues hotel vouchers where needed and messages them, before they reach the service desk.

Use cases across business functions

High-impact use cases exist in every part of a business, not just where AI first appears. The exam guide names customer operations, sales and marketing, research and development, and software development; back-office functions add more.

Customer operations

Agent assistant and after-call notes. In a typical contact center, agents spend minutes per call searching documents and several more afterwards writing notes. An assistant that answers questions from the documents (search and answer) and drafts the notes from the call transcript (summarize) targets both.

How success is measuredAverage handling time (talk time plus after-call work), first-contact resolution (issues solved in one call) and customer satisfaction, which must not fall.

Self-service. Many contacts are simple: "Where is my order?", "Change my address." An assistant or agent on the website can handle these around the clock (act).

How success is measuredThe share of contacts resolved without an agent, and satisfaction with the self-service experience.

Sales and marketing

Retention. A subscription business, such as a streaming service or a gym chain, predicts which customers are likely to cancel (predict), so offers go to them rather than to everyone. This matters because keeping a customer is usually far cheaper than winning a new one.

How success is measuredRetention rate, and the cost of offers per customer retained.

Personalized communication. Campaign emails tailored to each customer's purchases, generated at scale (generate).

How success is measuredOpen and conversion rates, and the time to produce a campaign.

Research and development

Faster discovery. A pharmaceutical company uses models to screen millions of candidate molecules and pick the few hundred most promising ones to test in the laboratory (predict). A manufacturer uses generative design to propose lighter part shapes that meet strength requirements (generate).

How success is measuredTime and cost to reach a viable candidate or design, and the share of candidates that pass real-world tests.

Software development

Coding assistants. A bank modernizing decades-old systems uses AI coding assistants to explain old code, write new code and generate tests (generate, explain).

How success is measuredDelivery speed, and the number of defects found after release.

Back office

Invoice matching. A finance team checks each supplier invoice against the purchase order and the delivery record before paying (a "three-way match"). AI can read all three documents and flag differences, such as being billed for 120 units when 100 were delivered (extract, classify).

How success is measuredProcessing cost per invoice, and overpayments caught.

What makes a use case high-impact

Volume

The task happens often. Saving 90 seconds on a task done 24,000 times a day is worth far more than saving an hour on a monthly report.

Value per instance

Each case is expensive or risky. A bank that loses an average of $40,000 on each fraudulent loan gains a lot from even a modest improvement in detection.

Link to an objective

It moves a measure leadership already cares about, so it gets sponsorship and budget.

Data available

The inputs exist digitally and in usable quality. Call transcripts already exist; a use case that first needs years of data collection is slower to pay off.

Tolerance for error

Mistakes can be caught by review or are cheap. An agent reviewing every draft note catches errors; an AI sending payments without review would not.

A quick estimate shows the scale. In a contact center taking 24,000 calls a day, saving 90 seconds per call frees 600 agent-hours a day. At a loaded cost of $35 an hour (pay plus benefits and overheads), that time is worth about $21,000 a day, or about $4.6 million a year over 220 working days. This is before subtracting the AI's running cost, and freed time only becomes cash savings if it reduces spending, for example by hiring fewer new agents as call volume grows.

The strongest answer links a use case to a stated business objective and a measurable outcome. Answers that start from a technology ("deploy a chatbot because competitors have one") are usually wrong.

A use case that impresses in a demo isn't necessarily high-impact. Ask how often it happens, what each instance is worth, and which objective it moves.

Build, buy or partner

Skill 2.1.2Evaluate build-buy-partner decisions using budget, timelines, capabilities, vendor proposals and regulatory requirements

Every AI initiative needs a sourcing decision: who creates the solution. Each option trades control against speed and effort.

The three options

Buy

Use a ready-made product, or the AI features built into software you already use. It is the fastest route: the vendor builds and maintains the product, and the price is predictable.

The downsides: competitors can buy the same tool, so it brings no advantage; customization is limited; and you depend on the vendor's roadmap and its terms for using your data.

On AWSAmazon Quick for business users, and third-party AI software bought through AWS Marketplace.

Build

Create your own solution using AI services or models. It fits your needs exactly, can use your proprietary data, can set you apart from competitors and keeps full control of the data.

The downsides: it needs skilled people, time and budget, and you own everything after launch: maintenance, monitoring and moving to new model versions.

On AWSAmazon Bedrock for applications and agents built on foundation models; Amazon SageMaker AI for building and training your own ML models.

Partner

Work with a consulting or technology partner who designs and builds the solution with you, ideally transferring skills to your team. It brings expertise and speed you don't have, and shares the risk.

The downsides: cost, and dependency on the partner unless knowledge is deliberately handed over.

On AWSAWS Partners (many offer professional services through AWS Marketplace) and the AWS Generative AI Innovation Center.

The AWS options in more detail

Amazon Quick

An AI-powered service that business users work with through natural language chat. It includes Quick Sight (dashboards and business intelligence), Quick Flows and Quick Automate (task and process automation), Quick Index (grounding answers in company documents) and Quick Research (cited research reports). It is fully managed, needs no ML expertise, and is paid for through per-user subscriptions (Amazon Quick User Guide).

AWS Marketplace

A curated catalog of third-party software, data and services, including machine learning products. Charges appear on the AWS bill, which simplifies procurement, and buyers can accept a seller's private offer with custom pricing and terms (AWS Marketplace Buyer Guide).

AWS Generative AI Innovation Center

A team of AWS scientists and strategists who work with customers through advisory engagements, hands-on development or embedded teams.

Decision factors

Differentiation

Is this capability part of how you compete? Meeting notes and coding help are needs every company has, so buying is sensible. A bank's credit risk models or a retailer's pricing decide whether the business wins customers and makes money; those point toward building, or partnering if the skills are missing today.

Timeline

Needed within weeks points to buying. Months are acceptable for building. A partner can deliver a custom result faster than an inexperienced internal team.

Budget

Buying usually fits an operating budget with a predictable subscription. Building needs an investment budget plus the ongoing cost of a team. Partnering needs a project budget, then your own running costs afterwards.

In-house capability

With little AI expertise, buy. With strong data, engineering and ML teams, build. With some capability you want to grow, partner with knowledge transfer.

Data sensitivity and regulation

If the vendor's terms, certifications and data handling meet your requirements, buying is possible. If requirements are strict and you need full control over how data is processed, build. A partner with experience in regulated industries can help with either.

Fit with existing systems

A product that works with tools you already use is easy to buy. Deep integration with your own systems, such as a bank's decades-old core banking system, often requires building, or a partner if the integration is too complex to staff.

The choice is often mixed and changes over time: buy to learn fast, then build once the use case proves its value and becomes strategic. Many solutions are hybrid, such as a partner building a custom application on Amazon Bedrock that the company's own team then takes over.

Worked example: a regional bank's sourcing decisions

A regional bank with 2,000 staff and little AI experience has four initiatives to source:

Meeting notes and email drafting for office staff: buy

A common need with no competitive advantage in building it, needed quickly. The decision rests on choosing a product whose data-use terms meet the bank's policy.

Dashboards and plain-language questions for branch managers: buy (Amazon Quick)

Managers want to ask "How many new accounts did each branch open last month?" without waiting for a report. Managed business intelligence with AI chat does this and needs no ML skills.

Small-business credit risk model: build (on SageMaker AI)

Lending decisions decide the bank's losses and competitiveness, and the model's strength comes from the bank's own 20 years of loan repayment history, which no vendor has. Regulators also expect the bank to explain its credit decisions, which is easier with a model it controls.

Contact-center assistant answering product questions: partner, building on Amazon Bedrock, with handover

Strategic and close to customers, and it must answer from the bank's own product terms and fees. But the internal team has no generative AI experience yet. A partner builds it with two of the bank's engineers, who then take it over, building the skills needed for later use cases.

Total cost of ownership

Compare options over several years, not just the first invoice: setup, licences or usage, the people needed to run it, and later changes. An illustrative three-year comparison for a 400-agent contact center's assistant:

Cost itemBuy (vendor product)Build with a partner
Upfront$20,000 setup and integration$300,000 partner project
Licences or usage400 seats × $45 a month = $216,000 a yearModel usage and hosting about $90,000 a year
Internal team0.5 person for administration, about $60,000 a year2 people to run and improve it, about $280,000 a year
Three-year total$20,000 + 3 × $276,000 = $848,000$300,000 + 3 × $370,000 = $1,410,000

On these numbers, buying is cheaper. Building is justified only if it delivers more value: higher accuracy on the company's own products, integration the product can't offer, or control over data that compliance requires. Notice also how the pricing models scale differently. The vendor charges per seat, so cost grows with headcount; the built solution is charged by usage, so cost grows with call volume. A plan to double the number of agents would change the comparison.

AI pricing structures

Vendor proposals and AWS services use three main pricing structures:

Consumption-based

You pay per use: per token, request, page or minute. Costs rise and fall with usage, which suits uncertain or variable demand. Example: Amazon Bedrock on-demand, charged per input and output token.

Instance-based

You pay per hour for reserved computing capacity, whether you use it or not. It suits steady, high, predictable workloads. Examples: a model hosted on dedicated capacity in Amazon SageMaker AI; Amazon Bedrock Provisioned Throughput for custom models; the Bedrock Reserved tier, a 1-month or 3-month commitment. Savings Plans lower the hourly rate of steady SageMaker AI or compute use in exchange for a 1-year or 3-year commitment.

Seat-based

You pay per user per month, regardless of how much each person uses it. It suits tools used daily by a known group. Examples: Amazon Quick, whose organization plans are priced per user per month (Essentials 3 USD, Professional 20 USD, Enterprise 40 USD) plus a 250 USD infrastructure fee per account, so seat pricing is not purely per head; most office AI assistants.

Evaluating vendor proposals

Questions to ask before buying or partnering:

  • Evidence: has the product been tested on our data, with results we can verify? Demos on the vendor's data prove little.
  • Data use: is our data used to train or improve the vendor's models, and can we opt out? Where is it stored and processed?
  • Underlying model: which models does the product use, and what happens when they change or are retired?
  • Compliance: which certifications and independent audit reports exist (for example, ISO/IEC 42001 for AI management, ISO/IEC 27001 for security)? Will they support our regulatory obligations?
  • Intellectual property: who owns the outputs and any custom models? Is there protection against claims that outputs infringe others' rights?
  • Cost at scale: what does it cost at three times today's volume?
  • Exit: can we export our data, prompts and configurations if we leave? What notice and support does the contract give?

"Common capability, needed quickly, little in-house expertise" points to buy. "Differentiating, uses proprietary data, needs full control" points to build. "Strategic but the team lacks skills, needs speed" points to partner, ideally with knowledge transfer.

Building something a vendor already sells well, just to "own" it, wastes budget and skilled people better spent on differentiating work. Buying a generic tool for a capability that is core to how you compete gives away your advantage.

AWS Marketplace in more detail

  • One bill: Marketplace purchases appear on the AWS bill, which simplifies procurement and can count toward existing AWS spend commitments where eligible.
  • Pricing options: pay-as-you-go, annual contracts, or private offers with negotiated prices and terms for one customer.
  • Bedrock models: some third-party models in Bedrock are offered through Marketplace, so using them requires Marketplace permissions; Bedrock added private-offer distribution through Marketplace in October 2026.
  • Faster buying: standard contract terms and existing vendor approval shorten procurement compared with a new supplier relationship.

Prioritizing initiatives and deciding to scale, pause or terminate

Skill 2.1.3Prioritize initiatives by value, feasibility, sustainability and strategic alignment, and decide to scale, pause or terminate

Budgets, data teams and leadership attention are limited, and most organizations have more AI ideas than they can fund. Prioritization chooses which initiatives go first. The exam guide names four criteria.

The four criteria

Business value

How much will it improve outcomes? Look at savings, revenue, risk reduction and customer experience, expressed in money where possible.

Feasibility

Can we actually deliver it? Look at whether the data exists and is good enough, how complex the technology is, whether we have the skills, how hard the integration with existing systems is, and any regulatory hurdles.

Sustainability

Can we keep it running and valuable? Look at the ongoing cost, the effort of maintaining and monitoring it, how often models will need upgrading, whether the value will last, and its computing and energy footprint.

Strategic alignment

Does it serve our stated objectives? Look at the link to leadership's objectives, and the fit with the organization's risk appetite and brand.

Worked example: scoring the bank's candidates

The regional bank's steering group scores six candidates from 1 (low) to 5 (high). It agreed the weights before scoring: value 35%, feasibility 25%, sustainability 15% and alignment 25%. The weighted score is each score multiplied by its weight, added up.

CandidateValueFeasibilitySustainabilityAlignmentWeighted score
After-call summaries for agents45444.25
Payment fraud model upgrade53354.20
Loan document extraction44444.00
Customer-facing chatbot answering fee and eligibility questions42343.35
Fully automated small-business loan approval51233.05
AI-generated marketing images25423.05

For the first row: 4 × 0.35 + 5 × 0.25 + 4 × 0.15 + 4 × 0.25 = 1.40 + 1.25 + 0.60 + 1.00 = 4.25. The reasons behind the scores:

  • After-call summaries score high on feasibility because call transcripts already exist and an agent reviews every draft.
  • The fraud upgrade has the highest value, but feasibility suffers because card and online-banking records don't yet match the same customer across systems.
  • Automated loan approval, deciding loans without a credit officer, would be valuable but is hard to deliver and risky to explain to regulators and declined applicants.

Sorting the portfolio

The scores feed a value-versus-feasibility view that sorts candidates into four groups:

Quick wins: high value, high feasibility

Start now. They build credibility and help fund later work. Here: after-call summaries and loan document extraction.

Strategic bets: high value, harder to deliver

Invest deliberately, with milestones, and fix the blockers (data, skills) first. Here: the fraud model upgrade; automated loan approval much later, if ever.

Nice to have: lower value, easy

Only if cheap and low risk, and often better bought than built. Here: marketing images.

Avoid for now: lower value, hard

Don't fund. None in this round.

Scores support judgment rather than replace it. The customer chatbot scores in the middle, but its low feasibility comes from one specific, fixable risk: wrong answers about fees given directly to customers. A sensible plan delivers the agent-facing assistant first, where an agent checks each answer, then reconsiders the customer-facing version once accuracy is proven.

Scale, pause or terminate

Prioritization isn't a one-time ranking. Every initiative should pass through stage gates (idea, pilot, limited production, full scale) with criteria agreed before it starts, so each decision is based on evidence, not enthusiasm or sunk cost.

Scale

When the pilot met its success criteria, costs are understood, risks are controlled and users adopted it.

ExampleAfter-call summaries saved 80 seconds per call in a 40-agent pilot measured against a comparison group, with 90% of summaries accepted unedited, above the agreed 85%. Decision: roll out to all agents.

Pause

When there is value but a fixable blocker: data quality, accuracy, a regulatory question or missing skills.

ExampleThe customer chatbot gave wrong fee answers in 4% of test cases against a 1% limit. Decision: pause, answer only from retrieved product terms with grounding checks (RAG), and retest.

Terminate

When the value isn't there, the problem has changed, the risk can't be brought within appetite, or a better option exists.

ExampleMarketing images: few designers used them, legal raised unresolved questions about image rights, and the design tool the team already licenses added a similar feature. Decision: stop.

Terminating is a valid and valuable decision. Money already spent is gone whichever way the decision goes (the sunk cost), so it shouldn't keep a failing initiative alive. Stopping early frees budget and people for initiatives that work. What the organization learned, such as data issues found, skills built and components that can be reused, should be recorded.

Prioritize by value and feasibility together, tied to strategic objectives, and decide scale, pause or terminate against success criteria agreed in advance.

Continuing an initiative because "we've already invested so much" is the sunk cost fallacy. And starting with the most ambitious, highest-value idea regardless of feasibility usually means a long, expensive pilot that never reaches production.

When AI isn't the right solution

Skill 2.1.4Determine when AI is not the appropriate solution

Recognizing when not to use AI is as valuable as finding good use cases. AWS's Machine Learning Lens advises starting every review by determining whether ML is the appropriate approach, evaluating the alternatives, and considering cost and accuracy. For an ML approach to succeed, enough relevant, high-quality data must be available (AWS Well-Architected Machine Learning Lens, Business goal identification).

Warning signs

The logic is fixed and known

When the answer follows published rules, ordinary software applies them exactly, explainably and cheaply. AI would add cost and occasional errors.

Better approachRule-based automation. Example: calculating a late-payment fee from the terms in a customer's contract.

The real problem is a process or policy

AI would make a broken process run faster, not fix it.

Better approachRedesign the process first. See the approval backlog example below.

There isn't enough good data

A model can't learn reliable patterns from a handful of examples or from badly recorded data.

Better approachCollect data first, and use rules or expert judgment meanwhile. Example: a manufacturer predicting a rare machine failure that has happened only 30 times.

No error is acceptable and no review is possible

AI is probabilistic and will sometimes be wrong. If nobody can check its output and any mistake is unacceptable, it is the wrong tool.

Better approachDeterministic systems, or keep the decision with people. Example: final payment amounts sent to suppliers without any check.

Every decision must be fully explained

Some models can't give a reason a regulator or customer would accept.

Better approachRules, or simple and explainable models. Example: lenders in many countries must give declined credit applicants the main reasons for the decision.

The cost exceeds the value

A task done 20 times a month can't repay the cost of building and running an AI solution.

Better approachKeep it manual, or use a simple tool.

The question is about reporting, not prediction

"How many orders did each region ship last month?" already has an exact answer in the data; nothing needs predicting.

Better approachA business intelligence dashboard.

The law restricts it

Some jurisdictions restrict decisions made solely by automated processing that significantly affect people; in the EU, for example, the GDPR gives people the right not to be subject to such decisions, with limited exceptions.

Better approachA person decides, with AI only in support. Example: rejecting job applicants.

Worked example: the approval backlog

A manufacturer's procurement director asks for "an AI solution to clear the purchase request backlog". Purchase requests take 9 days on average from an engineer asking for a part to the order being placed. Before choosing any technology, the team maps where requests wait:

  1. Measure where requests waitOf the 9 days, about 4 are spent waiting for a director to approve any purchase above $5,000.
  2. Ask why the step existsThe limit was set 15 years ago, when $5,000 was a large purchase. Prices have risen since, and today 60% of requests exceed it, so most requests queue for a director's signature.
  3. Fix the process firstRaise the director-approval limit to $25,000, with random spot checks on purchases below it. Lead time falls by about 3 days at almost no cost.
  4. Then apply AI where it fitsExtracting prices and terms from supplier quotes, and comparing them, addresses the remaining delays.

Had the team started with AI, it might have built a model to prioritize the approval queue: real effort spent speeding up a step that shouldn't exist.

If a scenario describes a fixed rule, a broken process, too little data, a pure reporting need or zero tolerance for error with no review, the right answer is often not AI: rules, process redesign, dashboards, or a human decision.

"Everyone is using AI" is not a business case. An answer that applies AI without a measurable problem, suitable data and an acceptable error rate is usually wrong.

Transitioning to AI or between AI platforms

Skill 2.1.5Evaluate business continuity, cost, data readiness and performance when transitioning to AI or between AI platforms

Moving a business process onto AI, or moving an AI solution from one platform or model to another, changes how the business operates; it isn't just a technical upgrade. A failed cutover can stop invoices being paid, loans being approved or calls being answered. The exam guide names four considerations, shown here for the mortgage lender's document extraction going live.

The four considerations

Business continuity

What happens if the AI fails or performs badly? Can the business fall back to the old way, and for how long can both run side by side?

ExampleProcessors keep the ability to type figures manually for three months, so applications keep moving if extraction has problems.

Cost implications

Migration effort, a period of paying for both old and new systems, retraining and retesting, a different pricing model, and the exit terms of existing contracts.

ExampleThe lender's current document-capture vendor contract runs for four more months, so the first months mean paying for both.

Data readiness

Is the data the new solution needs available, clean and allowed to move? Does it need converting or reprocessing?

ExampleTesting extraction accuracy needs past applications where the typed figures are known to be correct; five years of scanned files must be checked first.

Performance impact

Is the new solution at least as accurate and fast as the old process? Can it handle peak load? How do users' workloads change?

ExampleWhen interest rates fall, homeowners rush to refinance and applications can triple within weeks; extraction must keep up with the peak, not just the average.

Also plan for people and process: updated procedures, staff training and new review responsibilities. Plan for compliance too: re-approval by risk teams, updated records of how data is processed, and notifying regulators where required.

A safe transition sequence

  1. Baseline the current processMeasure today's accuracy, speed, cost and volumes, so the new solution has something to beat.
  2. Run in shadow modeThe AI processes real work in parallel, but its output isn't used. Compare its results with the existing process.
  3. Pilot with a small groupOne team or one product type uses it for real, with close review.
  4. Roll out in phasesExpand by team, region or case type. Keep the fallback ready, and set clear rollback triggers, for example extraction errors above 2%.
  5. Support intensively after go-liveExtra support and daily monitoring for the first weeks.
  6. Decommission the old processOnly after the new one has proven stable, and after records and data are archived as required.

Moving between AI platforms or models

Changing a model or platform is a regular event. Providers release better or cheaper models, and older models are retired. On Amazon Bedrock, models move from Active to Legacy to End-of-Life. Most have a six-month Legacy period. After end-of-life, requests to the model fail, and migration doesn't happen automatically (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Model lifecycle). What does and doesn't carry over:

Application code

Carries over if it was built on a common interface. The Amazon Bedrock Converse API works the same way across Bedrock models that support messages, so code can be written once and used with different models.

Planning implicationDesign for model portability from the start.

Prompts

Carry over, but a new model may respond differently to the same wording.

Planning implicationRe-run the prompt test set and adjust the prompts.

Fine-tuned models

Don't carry over to a different base model; the customization must be done again.

Planning implicationKeep the training data and process so tuning can be repeated.

Embeddings and search indexes

Embeddings are the numeric representations of documents used for search by meaning. Embeddings from different embedding models aren't compatible, so changing the embedding model means re-processing every document.

Planning implicationBudget time and cost to rebuild the index.

Evaluation test sets

Carry over and become the basis for comparing old and new.

Planning implicationMaintain them as a core asset.

Data held in a vendor's product

Worked example: replacing a model before end-of-life

A company's internal IT helpdesk assistant runs on a model that has entered its Legacy period, with end-of-life in six months. The plan:

  1. Month 1: choose candidatesShortlist two newer models, considering quality, price per token and speed.
  2. Month 2: evaluateRun the 300-question test set with approved answers against both. The leading candidate scores 94% correct against the current model's 91%, and costs 20% less per request.
  3. Month 3: adjustTune the prompts for the new model and retest. The risk team re-approves the solution.
  4. Month 4: shadow and pilotThe new model runs in parallel, then serves one department.
  5. Month 5: phased cutoverSwitch all users, keeping the old model available as a fallback until its end-of-life.
  6. Month 6: bufferTime reserved for problems before end-of-life.

The organization that starts this work when the end-of-life notice arrives, not in the last month, avoids an emergency migration.

Good transition answers include a baseline, parallel or shadow running, a phased rollout, a fallback and rollback plan, and a check that data, costs and performance have been assessed.

A "big bang" cutover with the old process switched off on day one is a classic wrong answer. So is assuming prompts, fine-tuned models and search indexes move unchanged to a new model.

A

Question 3
Scenario
A pilot has run for nine months, missed its success criteria twice, and the business owner has left. What is the best decision?
  1. A. Continue because of the money already spent
  2. B. Terminate or pause, and record the lessons
  3. C. Scale it to more users
  4. D. Change the success criteria to match the results

Question 4

A company wants to move from one foundation model to a newer one. What should it do before switching production traffic?
  1. A. Switch immediately, since newer is better
  2. B. Re-run its evaluation set, test in shadow mode or with a small share of traffic, and plan a rollback
  3. C. Retrain all employees
  4. D. Rewrite all prompts from scratch without testing
Answer
B
Why
Model transitions need evidence: evaluation on the same test set, a phased rollout and a rollback plan.

Key takeaways

  • Start from business objectives, find where value is lost, and map AI capabilities (predict, forecast, extract, classify, summarize, generate, answer, act) to measurable outcomes. High-impact use cases combine volume, value per case, a link to an objective, available data and tolerance for error.
  • Buy for common needs, speed and limited skills; build for differentiating capabilities on proprietary data that need control; partner for strategic needs where skills are missing, with knowledge transfer. On AWS: Amazon Quick and AWS Marketplace (buy), Amazon Bedrock and SageMaker AI (build), AWS Partners and the Generative AI Innovation Center (partner).
  • Compare multi-year total cost of ownership, and note how consumption-, instance- and seat-based pricing scale differently. Test vendor claims on your own data, and check data use, compliance, IP and exit terms.
  • Prioritize by value, feasibility, sustainability and strategic alignment. Start with quick wins, invest deliberately in strategic bets, and decide to scale, pause or terminate against criteria agreed in advance, ignoring sunk cost.
  • AI is the wrong answer for fixed rules, broken processes, too little data, zero error tolerance without review, full explainability needs, pure reporting, low value or legal restrictions. Fix the process before automating it.
  • Transitions need a baseline, shadow running, phased rollout, fallback and rollback triggers, and a review of continuity, cost, data readiness and performance. Model switches are routine: use common interfaces, keep test sets, and plan to redo prompts, fine-tuning and embeddings.
