Task 2.1: Develop AI strategies that align with business objectives

Turning "we should use AI" into a portfolio of the right initiatives: finding use cases that move business outcomes, deciding whether to build, buy or partner, ranking initiatives and deciding which to scale, pause or stop, recognizing problems AI shouldn't solve, and moving processes onto AI, or from one AI platform to another, without disrupting the business.

Overview

Task 2.1

Develop AI strategies that align with business objectives.

An AI strategy is not a list of AI projects. It is a set of decisions about which business outcomes AI should improve, which initiatives to fund in which order, how to source each one, and when to stop. Without it, organizations end up with many disconnected pilots, each started because a tool was available or a team was enthusiastic. Few of them reach production, and none are measured against what the business actually needs.

The common thread in this task is starting from the business objective, not the technology. Examples use Harbor Mutual, a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees, 1.2 million policyholders and a 400-agent contact center, plus examples from other industries. Harbor Mutual's leadership has set three objectives for the next two years:

  • Cut the average claim cycle time from 12 days to 7.
  • Reduce contact-center cost per call by 15% without lowering satisfaction.
  • Reduce fraud losses by $3 million a year.

Identifying high-impact use cases

Skill 2.1.1

A use case is a specific business task that AI will perform or improve, with a measurable outcome: "draft the after-call summary for contact-center agents, saving 90 seconds per call". Compare "use AI in the contact center", which can't be built, priced or measured.

AWS's Machine Learning Lens calls business goal identification the most important phase of the ML life cycle: an organization should know the problem it is solving and the value to be gained, measurable against specific success criteria (AWS Well-Architected Machine Learning Lens, Business goal identification).

Working from objective to use case

  1. Start with a business objective"Cut claim cycle time from 12 days to 7."
  2. Find where time, money or quality is lostMap the process and measure each step. At Harbor Mutual, 3 of the 12 days are spent waiting for staff to retype repair estimates and 2 days waiting for adjusters to read long claim files.
  3. Ask what kind of capability would remove the lossRetyping documents calls for extraction. Reading long files calls for summarization.
  4. Define the use case and its measure"Extract repair estimate fields automatically, cutting 3 days of waiting to same-day processing."
  5. Check data and constraintsAre the documents available digitally? Are there privacy or regulatory limits?

Working backwards from the customer or business outcome, rather than forwards from a technology, is how Amazon itself approaches new products, and it is the most reliable way to find use cases that matter.

The same pattern in other industries: a pharmaceutical company uses AI in R&D to screen candidate molecules; a retailer forecasts demand per store; a bank's software teams use coding assistants to modernize old systems; a hospital summarizes patient histories before appointments.

What makes a use case high-impact

  • Volume: the task happens often. Saving 90 seconds on a task done 24,000 times a day is worth far more than saving an hour on a monthly report.
  • Value per instance: each case is expensive or risky, like an $8,000 fraudulent claim.
  • Link to an objective: it moves a measure leadership already cares about.
  • Data available: the inputs exist digitally and in usable quality.
  • Tolerance for error: mistakes can be caught by review or are cheap.

A quick estimate shows the scale. The after-call summary saves 90 seconds on 24,000 calls a day, which is 600 agent-hours a day. At a loaded cost of $35 an hour, that is $21,000 a day, or about $4.6 million a year over 220 working days, before subtracting the AI's running cost.

The strongest answer links a use case to a stated business objective and a measurable outcome. Answers that start from a technology ("deploy a chatbot because competitors have one") are usually wrong.

A use case that impresses in a demo isn't necessarily high-impact. Ask how often it happens, what each instance is worth, and which objective it moves.

Build, buy or partner

Skill 2.1.2

Every AI initiative needs a sourcing decision. Each option trades control against speed and effort:

OptionWhat it meansStrengthsWeaknessesOn AWS
BuyUse a ready-made product or a product's built-in AI featuresFastest; vendor maintains it; predictable priceSame as competitors' tools; limited customization; depends on the vendor's roadmap and data termsAmazon Quick for business users; third-party AI software through AWS Marketplace
BuildCreate your own solution on AI services or modelsFits exactly; uses proprietary data; can differentiate; full control of dataNeeds skills, time and budget; you own maintenance, monitoring and model upgradesAmazon Bedrock for foundation models and agents; Amazon SageMaker AI for building and training ML models
PartnerWork with a consulting or technology partner to design and build it, often with skills transferExpertise and speed you don't have in-house; shared riskCost; dependency on the partner unless knowledge is transferredAWS Partners (many listed in AWS Marketplace for professional services); the AWS Generative AI Innovation Center

Some AWS details that matter for this decision:

  • Amazon Quick is an AI-powered service that business users work with through natural language chat. It includes Quick Sight (dashboards and business intelligence), Quick Flows and Quick Automate (task and process automation), Quick Index (grounding answers in company documents) and Quick Research (cited research reports). It is fully managed and needs no ML expertise, and organizations pay through per-user subscriptions (Amazon Quick User Guide).
  • AWS Marketplace is a curated catalog of third-party software, data and services, including machine learning products. Charges appear on the AWS bill, and buyers can accept a seller's private offer with custom pricing and terms (AWS Marketplace Buyer Guide).
  • The AWS Generative AI Innovation Center pairs customers with AWS scientists and strategists, through advisory, hands-on development or embedded teams.

The choice is often mixed and changes over time: buy to learn fast, then build once the use case proves its value and becomes strategic. Many solutions are hybrid, such as a partner building a custom application on Amazon Bedrock that the company's own team then takes over.

Total cost of ownership

Compare options over several years, not just the first invoice. An illustrative three-year comparison for the contact-center assistant:

Cost itemBuy (vendor product)Build with a partner
Upfront$20,000 setup and integration$300,000 partner project
Licences or usage400 seats × $45 a month = $216,000 a yearModel usage and hosting about $90,000 a year

On these numbers, buying is cheaper. Building is justified only if it delivers more value, for example higher accuracy on Harbor Mutual's own policies, integration the product can't offer, or control over data that compliance requires. Notice also how pricing models scale differently: seat-based pricing grows with headcount, while usage-based pricing grows with volume. A plan to double the number of agents changes the comparison.

Evaluating vendor proposals

Questions to ask before buying or partnering:

  • Evidence: has the product been tested on our data, with results we can verify? Demos on the vendor's data prove little.
  • Data use: is our data used to train or improve the vendor's models, and can we opt out? Where is it stored and processed?
  • Underlying model: which models does the product use, and what happens when they change or are retired?
  • Compliance: which certifications and independent audit reports exist (for example, ISO/IEC 42001 for AI management, ISO/IEC 27001 for security)? Will they support our regulatory obligations?
  • Intellectual property: who owns the outputs and any custom models? Is there protection against claims that outputs infringe others' rights?
  • Cost at scale: what does it cost at three times today's volume?
  • Exit: can we export our data, prompts and configurations if we leave? What notice and support does the contract give?

"Common capability, needed quickly, little in-house expertise" points to buy. "Differentiating, uses proprietary data, needs full control" points to build. "Strategic but the team lacks skills, needs speed" points to partner, ideally with knowledge transfer.

Building something a vendor already sells well, just to "own" it, wastes budget and skilled people better spent on differentiating work. Buying a generic tool for a capability that is core to how you compete gives away your advantage.

Prioritizing initiatives and deciding to scale, pause or terminate

Skill 2.1.3

Budgets, data teams and leadership attention are limited, and most organizations have more AI ideas than they can fund. Prioritization chooses which initiatives go first, using four criteria from the exam guide:

CriterionQuestionWhat to look at
Business valueHow much will it improve outcomes?Savings, revenue, risk reduction, customer experience, in money where possible
FeasibilityCan we actually deliver it?Data availability and quality, technical complexity, skills, integration, regulatory hurdles
SustainabilityCan we keep it running and valuable?Ongoing cost, maintenance and monitoring effort, model upgrades, whether value lasts, and its computing and energy footprint
Strategic alignmentDoes it serve our stated objectives?Link to leadership's objectives, fit with risk appetite and brand

Worked example: scoring Harbor Mutual's candidates

The steering group scores each candidate from 1 (low) to 5 (high) and weights the criteria: value 35%, feasibility 25%, sustainability 15%, alignment 25%. The weighted score is each score multiplied by its weight, added up.

CandidateValueFeasibilitySustainabilityAlignmentWeighted score

The scores feed a value-versus-feasibility view that sorts the portfolio into four groups:

GroupProfileActionHarbor Mutual candidates
Quick winsHigh value, high feasibilityStart now; they build credibility and fund later workAfter-call summaries, repair estimate extraction
Strategic betsHigh value, harder to deliverInvest deliberately, with milestones; fix the blockers (data, skills) firstFraud model upgrade; automated underwriting much later, if ever
Nice to haveLower value, easyOnly if cheap and low risk; often better bought than builtMarketing images
Avoid for nowLower value, hardDon't fundNone in this round

Scores support judgment rather than replace it. The coverage chatbot scores in the middle, but its low feasibility comes from a specific, fixable risk: wrong coverage answers given directly to customers. A sensible plan delivers the agent-facing policy assistant first, then reconsiders the customer-facing version once accuracy is proven with staff in the loop.

Scale, pause or terminate

Prioritization isn't a one-time ranking. Every initiative should pass through stage gates (idea, pilot, limited production, full scale) with criteria agreed before it starts, so the decision is based on evidence, not enthusiasm or sunk cost.

DecisionWhenHarbor Mutual example
ScaleThe pilot met its success criteria, costs are understood, risks are controlled and users adopted itAfter-call summaries saved 95 seconds per call in a 40-agent pilot, with 92% of summaries accepted unedited; roll out to all 400 agents
PauseThere is value, but a fixable blocker: data quality, accuracy, a regulatory question or missing skillsThe coverage chatbot gave wrong answers in 4% of test cases against a 1% limit; pause, add RAG with grounding checks, retest
TerminateThe value isn't there, the problem changed, the risk can't be brought within appetite, or a better option existsMarketing images: few designers used them, legal raised unresolved image-rights questions, and the design tool the team already licenses added a similar feature

Terminating is a valid and valuable decision. Money already spent is gone whichever way the decision goes (the sunk cost), so it shouldn't keep a failing initiative alive. Stopping early frees budget and people for initiatives that work. What the organization learned (data issues found, skills built, components that can be reused) should be recorded.

Prioritize by value and feasibility together, tied to strategic objectives, and decide scale, pause or terminate against success criteria agreed in advance.

Continuing an initiative because "we've already invested so much" is the sunk cost fallacy. And starting with the most ambitious, highest-value idea regardless of feasibility usually means a long, expensive pilot that never reaches production.

When AI isn't the right solution

Skill 2.1.4

Recognizing when not to use AI is as valuable as finding good use cases. AWS's Machine Learning Lens advises starting every review by determining whether ML is the appropriate approach, evaluating the alternatives, and considering cost and accuracy. For an ML approach to succeed, enough relevant, high-quality data must be available (AWS Well-Architected Machine Learning Lens, Business goal identification).

Warning signWhy AI is a poor fitBetter approachExample
The logic is fixed and knownRules give exact, explainable answers at lower costRule-based automationCalculating a policy's cancellation refund from published terms
The real problem is a process or policyAI would speed up a broken processRedesign the processSee the worked example below
There isn't enough good dataThe model can't learn reliable patternsCollect data first; use rules or experts meanwhilePredicting a type of claim seen only 30 times
No error is acceptable and no review is possibleAI is probabilistic; it will sometimes be wrongDeterministic systems, or keep the decision with peopleFinal payment amounts sent without any check
Every decision must be fully explainedSome models can't give a reason a regulator would acceptRules, or simple and explainable modelsStatutory pricing rules that must be filed with a regulator
The cost exceeds the valueLow volume or low value per case can't repay build and running costsManual handling, a simple toolA task done 20 times a month
The question is about reporting, not predictionThe answer already exists in the dataBusiness intelligence dashboards"How many claims did each branch close last month?"
The law restricts itSome jurisdictions restrict solely automated decisions with significant effects on peopleHuman decision, with AI only in supportAutomatically declining insurance applications

Worked example: the claims backlog

Harbor Mutual's claims director asks for "an AI solution to clear the claims backlog". Before choosing any technology, the team maps the process:

  1. Measure where claims waitOf the 12 days, 4 are spent waiting for a second manager to approve any payment above $2,000.
  2. Ask why the step existsThe rule was set 15 years ago, when $2,000 was a much larger share of a typical claim. Today 60% of claims exceed it.
  3. Fix the process firstRaise the second-approval limit to $7,500, with spot checks. Cycle time falls by about 3 days at almost no cost.
  4. Then apply AI where it fitsRepair estimate extraction and claim summaries address the remaining delays.

Had the team started with AI, it might have built a model to prioritize the approval queue: real effort spent speeding up a step that shouldn't exist.

If a scenario describes a fixed rule, a broken process, too little data, a pure reporting need or zero tolerance for error with no review, the right answer is often not AI: rules, process redesign, dashboards, or a human decision.

"Everyone is using AI" is not a business case. An answer that applies AI without a measurable problem, suitable data and an acceptable error rate is usually wrong.

Transitioning to AI or between AI platforms

Skill 2.1.5

Moving a business process onto AI, or moving an AI solution from one platform or model to another, is a change to how the business operates, not just a technical upgrade. A failed cutover can stop claims being paid or calls being answered. Four considerations from the exam guide frame the planning:

ConsiderationQuestions to answerHarbor Mutual example
Business continuityWhat happens if the AI fails or performs badly? Can we fall back to the old way? How long can we run both?Staff keep the ability to key repair estimates manually for three months after extraction goes live
Cost implicationsMigration effort, a period of paying for both systems, retraining and retesting, new pricing model, contract exit termsTwo months of paying both the old vendor and the new Bedrock-based solution
Data readinessIs the data available, clean and allowed to move? Does it need converting or reprocessing?Ten years of scanned estimates must be checked before use as test data
Performance impactIs the new solution at least as accurate and fast? What about peak load? How do users' workloads change?Extraction must handle the post-storm peak of 5,000 estimates a day

Also plan for people and process: updated procedures, staff training, and new review responsibilities. Plan for compliance too: re-approval by risk teams, updated records of processing, and notifying regulators where required.

A safe transition sequence

  1. Baseline the current processMeasure today's accuracy, speed, cost and volumes, so the new solution has something to beat.
  2. Run in shadow modeThe AI processes real work in parallel, but its output isn't used. Compare its results with the existing process.
  3. Pilot with a small groupOne team or one claim type uses it for real, with close review.
  4. Roll out in phasesExpand by team, region or case type. Keep the fallback ready, and set clear rollback triggers (for example, extraction errors above 2%).
  5. Support intensively after go-liveExtra support and daily monitoring for the first weeks.
  6. Decommission the old processOnly after the new one has proven stable, and after records and data are archived as required.

Moving between AI platforms or models

Changing a model or platform is a regular event, not a rare one. Providers release better or cheaper models, and older models are retired. On Amazon Bedrock, models move from Active to Legacy to End-of-Life. Most have a six-month Legacy period, after end-of-life requests to the model fail, and migration doesn't happen automatically (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Model lifecycle). What does and doesn't carry over:

AssetWhat happens when you switchPlanning implication
Application codeCan carry over if built on a common interface. The Amazon Bedrock Converse API works the same way across Bedrock models that support messages, so code can be written once and used with different models.Design for model portability from the start
PromptsCarry over, but a new model may respond differently to the same wordingRe-run the prompt test set and adjust
Fine-tuned modelsDon't carry over to a different base model; the customization must be redoneKeep the training data and process so tuning can be repeated
Embeddings and search indexesEmbeddings from different embedding models aren't compatible; changing the embedding model means re-processing all documentsBudget time and cost to rebuild the index
Evaluation test setsCarry over and become the basis for comparisonMaintain them as a core asset
Data in a vendor's productDepends on the contract and export featuresCheck exit terms before buying

Worked example: replacing a model before end-of-life

Harbor Mutual's policy assistant runs on a model that has entered its Legacy period, with end-of-life in six months. The plan:

  1. Month 1: choose candidatesShortlist two newer models, considering quality, price per token and speed.
  2. Month 2: evaluateRun the 300-question test set with approved answers against both. The leading candidate scores 94% correct against the current model's 91%, and costs 20% less per request.
  3. Month 3: adjustTune the prompts for the new model and retest. The risk team re-approves the solution.
  4. Month 4: shadow and pilotThe new model runs in parallel, then serves one team of 40 agents.
  5. Month 5: phased cutoverSwitch all agents, keeping the old model available as a fallback until its end-of-life.
  6. Month 6: bufferTime reserved for problems before end-of-life.

The organization that starts this work when the end-of-life notice arrives, not in the last month, avoids an emergency migration.

Good transition answers include a baseline, parallel or shadow running, a phased rollout, a fallback and rollback plan, and a check that data, costs and performance have been assessed.

A "big bang" cutover with the old process switched off on day one is a classic wrong answer. So is assuming prompts, fine-tuned models and search indexes move unchanged to a new model.

Key takeaways

  • Start from business objectives, find where value is lost, and map AI capabilities (predict, forecast, extract, classify, summarize, generate, answer, act) to measurable outcomes. High-impact use cases combine volume, value per case, a link to an objective, available data and tolerance for error.
  • Buy for common needs, speed and limited skills; build for differentiating capabilities on proprietary data that need control; partner for strategic needs where skills are missing, with knowledge transfer. On AWS: Amazon Quick and AWS Marketplace (buy), Amazon Bedrock and SageMaker AI (build), AWS Partners and the Generative AI Innovation Center (partner).
  • Compare multi-year total cost of ownership, and note how consumption-, instance- and seat-based pricing scale differently. Test vendor claims on your own data, and check data use, compliance, IP and exit terms.
  • Prioritize by value, feasibility, sustainability and strategic alignment. Start with quick wins, invest deliberately in strategic bets, and decide to scale, pause or terminate against criteria agreed in advance, ignoring sunk cost.
  • AI is the wrong answer for fixed rules, broken processes, too little data, zero error tolerance without review, full explainability needs, pure reporting, low value or legal restrictions. Fix the process before automating it.
  • Transitions need a baseline, shadow running, phased rollout, fallback and rollback triggers, and a review of continuity, cost, data readiness and performance. Model switches are routine: use common interfaces, keep test sets, and plan to redo prompts, fine-tuning and embeddings.
