Overview

Task 1.1

Describe core AI concepts and define terminology.

This task is about literacy, not mathematics. A business leader doesn't build models, but approves budgets for them, sets the rules for how their outputs are used, answers to regulators for their decisions and judges vendor claims. Each of those depends on understanding what a model is, how it learns, how it can be wrong and what that costs. Exam questions describe a business situation in AI terms; you need to know what the terms mean, which kind of AI fits, and why data decides whether it works.

Most examples use Harbor Mutual, a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees, 1.2 million policyholders and a 400-agent contact center. Examples from other industries show that the same ideas apply everywhere.

Fundamental AI concepts in business contexts

Skill 1.1.1

Most AI in business answers one question over and over: given what we know about this case, what is most likely? Is this claim fraudulent? Will this customer cancel? How many umbrellas will this store sell next week? The vocabulary below describes how that answer is produced and how it turns into a business action.

The core terms

TermPlain meaningAt Harbor MutualWhy a leader cares
AlgorithmThe method a system uses to learn patterns from data: the recipeThe learning method the data science team picks for fraud scoringSome algorithms are easy to explain, others more accurate but opaque; regulated decisions may require the explainable kind
ModelWhat the algorithm produces after learning from data: a reusable decision engine that captures the patternsThe fraud model, which has learned what suspicious claims looked likeThe model is the asset that gets deployed, monitored, updated and audited
ParametersThe internal values the model adjusts during training; they hold what it has learnedThousands of values inside the fraud model; billions inside a large language modelMore parameters generally means more capability and higher running cost
TrainingShowing the algorithm many past examples so it builds the modelFive years of claims, each marked fraudulent or legitimateTraining is a project cost, and is repeated whenever the model is refreshed
FeaturesThe facts about a case the model looks atClaim amount, days since the policy started, number of past claims, time of day reportedFeatures decide fairness and legality: a feature such as postal code can act as a stand-in for a protected characteristic
LabelThe known answer for a past example"Fraud confirmed" or "legitimate" after investigationLabels are often the most expensive data: a person had to decide each one
InferenceUsing the trained model on a new caseScoring each new claim as it arrivesInference is the running cost, paid on every use
PredictionThe model's output for that case, usually with a confidence score"82% likely to be fraudulent"A prediction is a probability, never a certainty
ThresholdThe business rule that turns a score into an actionClaims scoring above 70% go to an investigator; the rest are paid normallySetting it is a business decision about cost and customer impact, not a technical detail

The same terms in other industries:

From business question to business decision

  1. Frame the question"Which claims are likely fraudulent?" A precise question decides which data and which kind of AI are needed. A vague one ("use AI on claims") can't be built or measured.
  2. Collect historical examplesPast claims with known outcomes.
  3. Train the modelThe algorithm learns which patterns separated fraud from legitimate claims.
  4. Evaluate itTest on past claims the model hasn't seen: how many frauds does it catch, and how many honest customers does it wrongly flag?
  5. Use it for inferenceEach new claim gets a score within seconds.
  6. Act on the predictionThe threshold routes high scores to people; an investigator makes the final decision.
  7. Monitor and retrainFraud patterns change, so accuracy is tracked and the model is retrained.

Judging a model: the four possible outcomes

Every yes/no prediction ends in one of four outcomes. Leaders need these because each has a different business cost:

OutcomeWhat happenedBusiness effect at Harbor Mutual
True positiveFlagged, and really was fraudFraud caught; the payout is saved
False positive (false alarm)Flagged, but was legitimateInvestigation cost, and an honest customer waits longer for payment
False negative (miss)Not flagged, but was fraudThe fraudulent claim is paid
True negativeNot flagged, and was legitimatePaid quickly, as it should be

Two measures summarize them:

  • Recall: of all the real fraud, how much did the model catch?
  • Precision: of everything the model flagged, how much was really fraud?

Raising recall usually lowers precision, and the reverse. Which matters more depends on what each mistake costs. In cancer screening, a miss is far worse than a false alarm, so recall comes first. In a spam filter, sending a real customer email to spam is worse, so precision comes first.

Worked example: choosing the threshold

Harbor Mutual receives 10,000 claims a month, and about 2% (200) are fraudulent. An investigation costs $150 per flagged claim; an average fraudulent claim pays out $8,000. The data science team reports two candidate thresholds:

MeasureThreshold 70%Threshold 50%
Claims flagged300600
Frauds caught (recall)160 of 200 (80%)185 of 200 (92.5%)
Honest customers flagged140 (precision 53%)415 (precision 31%)
Investigation cost300 × $150 = $45,000600 × $150 = $90,000
Fraud paid out (misses)40 × $8,000 = $320,00015 × $8,000 = $120,000
Total cost of the two errors$365,000$210,000

On cost alone the lower threshold wins by $155,000 a month. But it delays 415 honest customers instead of 140, which affects satisfaction, complaints and possibly regulatory rules on how fast claims must be paid. Investigators also need double the capacity. The model is the same in both columns. Choosing between them is a business decision that weighs money, customer experience and staffing, and it belongs to the business owner, not the data scientists.

Ways to run inference

ModeHow it worksFits whenExample
Real-timeEach request gets an answer within seconds or lessA person or process is waiting for the answerScoring a claim while the customer is on the phone
BatchLarge sets of records are processed on a scheduleNobody is waiting; lower cost matters more than speedScoring every policy overnight for cancellation risk

A prediction is a probability, not a fact. Strategic questions often hinge on what the business does with it: where the threshold sits, who reviews borderline cases, and what each kind of mistake costs.

"98% accurate" can be meaningless. If 2% of claims are fraud, a model that never flags anything is 98% accurate and catches nothing. For rare events, ask for recall and precision, or better, the cost of each kind of error.

AI, machine learning and generative AI

Skill 1.1.2

The terms are nested, each one a subset of the one before (AWS Cloud Adoption Framework for AI, ML and generative AI whitepaper). The distinction matters because each level needs different data, costs differently, fails differently and needs different controls.

TermWhat it isHow it worksAt Harbor Mutual
Artificial intelligence (AI)The broad field of machines performing tasks that normally need human intelligenceMany methods, including hand-written rules, search and machine learningEverything below, plus an older rules engine that checks policy eligibility
Machine learning (ML)AI that learns patterns from data instead of following explicit instructionsTrained on examples for one specific task; outputs come with a probability or confidenceThe fraud-scoring model; a model that predicts which customers will cancel
Deep learningML using many-layered neural networksEspecially good with unstructured data such as images, audio and textEstimating car damage from claim photos; transcribing calls
Generative AIDeep learning that creates new content: text, images, code, audioBuilt on foundation models trained on very broad data, usable for many tasks without task-specific trainingDrafting replies to customer emails; summarizing a claim file for an adjuster

Generative AI terms

  • Foundation model (FM): a model with a very large number of parameters, trained on a massive amount of diverse data, that can handle a wide range of tasks (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Key terminology). Organizations usually use one through a service such as Amazon Bedrock rather than training their own.
  • Large language model (LLM): a foundation model specialized in text: it reads and writes language.
  • Multimodal model: works with more than one kind of content, for example reading a photo of a damaged car and writing a description of the damage.
  • Hallucination: confident, fluent output that is false. It happens because a generative model produces the most plausible continuation, not a verified fact.

Traditional ML and generative AI compared

AspectTraditional MLGenerative AI
OutputA score, class or numberNew text, images, code or audio
Getting startedNeeds your own labeled historical data and a training projectA pre-trained model can be used immediately with instructions (prompts)
ScopeOne model per taskOne model handles many tasks
PredictabilitySame input gives the same outputOutput can vary between runs
Checking qualityCompare predictions to known answers (precision, recall)Harder: is the answer accurate, relevant, safe, on-brand? Often needs human or model-based review
Running costUsually low per predictionHigher per request, charged by the amount of text processed
Typical failureWrong score, often from biased or outdated dataPlausible but false content (hallucination), inappropriate content, leaking information
  • Classify versus create: traditional ML mostly classifies or predicts; generative AI produces new content (AWS, What is generative AI?).
  • Not all AI is ML: a rules engine that applies "decline if the driver is under 18" is AI in the broad sense but learns nothing. AWS puts it plainly: all ML is AI, but not all AI is ML (AWS, The difference between artificial intelligence and machine learning).

Recognizing the category in a scenario

ScenarioCategoryReasoning
Flag insurance claims likely to be fraudulentMLA yes/no prediction from structured history with known outcomes
Estimate repair cost from photos of a damaged carDeep learningInterpreting images needs neural networks
Draft a personalized reply to a complaint emailGenerative AICreating new text in natural language
Decline any policy application from a driver under 18Rules (AI in the broad sense, not ML)A fixed policy; nothing to learn
Forecast call volume for staffing next monthMLA numeric prediction from historical patterns
Answer employee questions about HR policy in plain languageGenerative AI (with company documents supplied to it)Conversational answers grounded in the organization's own text

Scenario wording points to the category: "predict", "score", "classify", "forecast" usually means ML; "draft", "summarize", "generate", "answer in natural language" means generative AI; "a fixed policy that must be applied the same way every time" may need no ML at all.

"Generative AI" isn't a synonym for "AI". An answer that uses a foundation model to make a simple yes/no prediction on tabular data is usually more expensive, less consistent and harder to validate than a purpose-built ML model.

Structured and unstructured data

Skill 1.1.3

The type of data decides which kind of AI can use it, how much preparation it needs, and what governance it requires. Knowing what data an organization has, and in what form, is often the first step in deciding which AI projects are realistic.

TypeWhat it looks likeExamplesTypical AI use
StructuredRows and columns with a fixed meaning; easy to query, count and joinHarbor Mutual's policy and claims tables; a retailer's sales transactions; a bank's account balancesClassic ML: fraud scores, churn prediction, demand forecasts, pricing
Semi-structuredLabeled fields without a fixed table layoutWeb form submissions, app event logs, JSON messages between systemsML after the fields are extracted; also useful as context for generative AI
UnstructuredFree text, documents, images, audio and video; meaning has to be interpretedClaim photos, repair estimates as PDFs, call recordings, emails; medical images; contractsDeep learning and generative AI: document extraction, image analysis, speech-to-text, summarization, question answering

Turning unstructured data into structured data

A common first AI project is extraction: reading documents and producing structured fields that existing systems and ML models can use. Harbor Mutual receives about 2,000 repair estimates a week as PDFs, and staff retype the key fields. The input is free text:

Repair estimate (unstructured) 
Bayside Auto Body - Estimate #4471   Date: 12 March
Vehicle: 2021 Toyota Corolla, plate 8KXR221
Customer reports rear-end collision at a traffic light.
Replace rear bumper cover ........ $640
Repair and paint trunk lid ....... $410
Labor 6.5 hrs @ $95 .............. $617.50
Total including tax .............. $1,812.40

The extraction produces fields that can be checked automatically and fed to the fraud model:

Extracted fields (structured) 
shop_name:     Bayside Auto Body
estimate_no:   4471
estimate_date: 12 March
vehicle:       2021 Toyota Corolla
plate:         8KXR221
cause:         rear-end collision
total_amount:  1812.40
labor_hours:   6.5

On AWS, Amazon Bedrock Data Automation turns documents, images, audio and video into structured output like this, and reports confidence scores so that low-confidence fields can go to a person for checking.

Why the type matters for planning

  • Where the value is: much of an organization's information is unstructured (emails, documents, recordings). It used to be expensive to use; generative AI and document extraction make it usable, which is why many first generative AI projects target documents and conversations.
  • Preparation: structured data needs consistent definitions across systems ("customer since" must mean the same everywhere). Unstructured data often needs extraction, transcription or splitting into sections before AI can use it.
  • Governance: unstructured data is harder to control. A call recording can contain card numbers and health details; a shared folder can mix public brochures with confidential underwriting notes. Permissions and privacy handling must be decided before the data feeds an AI system.

"Thousands of PDFs and scanned forms that staff retype into a system" signals document extraction (unstructured to structured). "Predict which policies will lapse from account history" signals ML on structured data. "Let staff ask questions about our manuals" signals generative AI over unstructured documents.

Why data quality matters for AI outcomes

Skill 1.1.4

A model learns whatever the data shows, including its mistakes. With traditional software, a data error affects one record. With AI, the error is learned and then repeated in every prediction, at the speed and scale of automation. Poor data produces a model that is wrong in confident, systematic ways, and these errors are hard to spot because the output looks authoritative. The ISO/IEC 5259 series (Data quality for analytics and machine learning) exists for this reason.

Quality dimensionQuestion to askHarbor Mutual exampleEffect on the AI
AccuracyDoes the data match reality?Some claims are coded "water damage" when the cause was stormThe model learns false patterns about causes
CompletenessAre values or records missing?Fraud outcomes were never recorded for claims settled by one branchThe model underestimates fraud from that branch
ConsistencyDoes the same thing mean the same in every system?"Customer since" uses different dates in the policy and billing systemsFeatures contradict each other
TimelinessIs it current enough for the decision?Address changes reach the claims system weeks latePredictions are made on stale facts
ValidityDoes it follow the expected format and rules?Policy start dates after the claim dateImpossible records distort training
UniquenessAre records duplicated?The same claim entered twice from two channelsSome patterns count double
RepresentativenessDoes the data cover all the cases the AI will see?History contains almost no claims from a newly entered regionPoor and possibly unfair predictions for that region

Worked example: how one data gap spreads

One Harbor Mutual branch never recorded investigation outcomes, so its 3,000 claims a year all appear as "legitimate" in the training data, including the ones that were actually fraudulent. Follow the effect:

  1. The data is wrongFraudulent claims from that branch are labeled as legitimate.
  2. The model learns the wrong lessonPatterns typical of that branch look "safe", so similar claims anywhere get low fraud scores.
  3. Testing doesn't catch itThe test data has the same mislabeling, so the model appears accurate.
  4. Production losses followFraud rings that learn to file claims resembling that branch's profile pass unchallenged.
  5. The fix is in the data, not the modelRecover the outcomes from investigator files, correct the labels, retrain, and make outcome recording mandatory in that branch's process.

Data quality for generative AI

The same applies to the documents a generative AI assistant draws on. Suppose Harbor Mutual's assistant answers from a shared folder holding the 2023 and 2025 versions of the home policy handbook. It may quote the old deductible to one customer and the new one to the next, fluently and with confidence. Quality work for generative AI means removing outdated versions, resolving contradictions, assigning an owner to each document set, and setting a review date.

Who owns data quality

Data quality is a business responsibility, not only a technical one. The people who create the data (claims handlers, branch staff) decide whether it is recorded correctly. Organizations typically name a data owner for each important data set who is accountable for its definitions and quality, and they measure quality before an AI project begins rather than discovering problems after launch.

When a scenario describes a disappointing AI result and messy, siloed or incomplete data, the first fix is usually improving the data (ownership, cleaning, consistent definitions), not choosing a bigger model.

More data isn't automatically better data. Adding millions of poorly labeled or unrepresentative records can make a model worse. Quality and coverage of the cases that matter come first.

Training a model on historical data

Skill 1.1.5

Training means learning from past examples, which builds in an assumption: the future will resemble the past. That assumption explains most of what can go wrong, and it is why a model's quality has to be checked both before launch and continually afterward.

Ways a model can learn

Learning typeLearns fromBusiness examples
SupervisedHistorical examples with known answers (labels)Claims labeled fraud or legitimate; loans labeled repaid or defaulted; emails labeled by department
UnsupervisedData without labels, finding groups and unusual cases on its ownGrouping customers into segments for marketing; spotting claims unlike any seen before
ReinforcementTrial, error and rewards for good outcomesOptimizing warehouse robot routes; refining how generative models respond, based on human feedback

Supervised learning is the most common in business, and its biggest cost is often the labels. Someone has to decide the right answer for thousands of past cases, sometimes using expensive experts. A model that needs radiologists to label 50,000 scans is a very different budget from one whose labels already sit in a database ("did the customer cancel?").

How a supervised model is trained, in business terms

  1. Gather and label historyFive years of claims (about 600,000) and their investigated outcomes.
  2. Split the history three waysFor example 70% for training, 15% for validation (to compare versions and tune the model during development) and 15% for the final test. The test portion is never shown during development, like an exam paper kept sealed.
  3. TrainThe algorithm adjusts the model's parameters until its predictions match the training labels as well as possible.
  4. Tune on the validation setTry variations and keep the one that does best on data it didn't learn from.
  5. Test once on the held-back dataGood results on unseen claims show the model learned general patterns rather than memorizing examples.
  6. Decide if it's good enoughCompare against the business baseline: does it beat today's manual rules, at an acceptable cost of mistakes? Is it fair across customer groups?

A sensible split also respects time. If the model will score next year's claims, test it on the most recent claims, not a random sample. Fraud schemes from five years ago tell you little about next year.

What can go wrong

RiskWhat happensExampleBusiness response
OverfittingThe model memorizes the training data and fails on new cases99% accurate on training claims, 70% on new onesInsist on results from held-back, recent data before approving launch
UnderfittingThe model is too simple to capture the real patternBarely better than guessing on any dataBetter features or a more capable method, or accept that the problem may not suit ML
Historical biasPast decisions were unfair, and the model learns to repeat themA hiring model trained on past hires favors the groups hired before; an underwriting model repeats past regional prejudiceFairness testing across groups, review of features, human oversight on high-impact decisions
Changing conditionsThe world moves away from the training dataDemand forecasts trained before a pandemic failed when buying patterns changed overnightMonitor accuracy in production and retrain on recent data
Too little dataRare events have too few examples to learn fromOnly 40 known cases of a new fraud schemeCombine with expert rules, collect more examples, or keep the decision with people
Data leakageTraining data contains information that wouldn't exist at prediction timeThe training data includes "investigator assigned", which only appears after a claim is suspectedCheck every feature is available at the moment of prediction; leakage makes test results look better than reality

Labeling services exist to produce training data with human reviewers. Note that Amazon SageMaker Ground Truth, AWS's human labeling service, is no longer open to new customers; existing customers can continue to use it.

"The model was 95% accurate in testing" isn't the end of the story. Ask what it was tested on (recent, representative, held-back data?), what the 5% of errors cost the business, whether it performs equally for all customer groups, and how its accuracy will be checked after launch.

Global frameworks and a unified AI vocabulary

Skill 1.1.6

When business, legal and technical teams and vendors use the same words differently, governance fails quietly. One team's "model validation" is a quick spot check; another's is a formal independent review. One contract's "AI system" includes the data pipeline; another's means only the model. International standards give organizations a shared vocabulary and a recognized structure for discussing, buying, auditing and governing AI, and regulators and auditors increasingly expect them.

StandardTitleWhat it gives an organization
ISO/IEC 22989:2022Artificial intelligence concepts and terminologyStandard definitions of AI terms, and a description of the AI system life cycle, so policies and contracts mean the same thing to everyone
ISO/IEC 23053:2022Framework for AI systems using machine learningA common description of the parts of an ML-based AI system (data, model, training, deployment, monitoring) and how they fit together, useful for describing and comparing solutions
ISO/IEC 42001:2023Artificial intelligence — Management systemRequirements for an AI management system (AIMS) for organizations that provide or use AI. Organizations can be independently certified against it.
ISO/IEC 23894:2023Artificial intelligence — Guidance on risk managementHow to identify, assess and treat AI-specific risks
ISO/IEC 5259 seriesData quality for analytics and machine learningTerms and processes for measuring and managing data quality

The AI system life cycle (ISO/IEC 22989)

ISO/IEC 22989 describes how an AI system evolves through seven stages, and AWS recommends applying governance at every stage (AWS Security Blog, AI lifecycle risk management: ISO/IEC 42001:2023 for AI governance, May 2025). For Harbor Mutual's fraud model:

  1. InceptionDefine the problem, the business owner and the acceptable risks: "reduce fraud losses without delaying honest claims".
  2. Design and developmentChoose data and features, exclude ones that could act as stand-ins for protected characteristics, build the model.
  3. Verification and validationTest accuracy, fairness across groups and robustness before approval.
  4. DeploymentRelease to production with access controls, documented thresholds and human review.
  5. Operation and monitoringTrack accuracy, drift and complaints.
  6. Re-evaluationPeriodic review: is it still accurate, fair and needed?
  7. RetirementWithdraw it safely, replacing it or returning to manual review, and keep the records.

ISO/IEC 42001 in practice

ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving an AI management system (AWS, ISO/IEC 42001 FAQs). A management system is the set of policies, roles and repeatable processes an organization uses to run something responsibly; ISO/IEC 27001 does the same for information security. Its core cycle, as described by AWS:

  1. Assess risksIdentify and assess the risks of each AI system (clause 6.1). For high-risk uses, add an AI impact assessment that looks at societal, ethical and legal effects on people, similar to a data protection impact assessment.
  2. Implement controlsPut operational controls in place to treat those risks (clause 8), choosing from the standard's reference controls in Annex A and recording which apply in a Statement of Applicability.
  3. Monitor, document and improveMeasure how well the controls work, audit them and improve continually (clauses 9 and 10).

Why an organization would pursue it:

  • Trust and sales: customers and partners increasingly ask suppliers how they govern AI; a certificate is independent evidence.
  • Regulatory readiness: a working management system makes it easier to show compliance with AI regulations.
  • Consistency: every AI project follows the same risk assessment and approval process, instead of each team inventing its own.

Certification and suppliers

  • Certification belongs to one organization: AWS holds an ISO/IEC 42001 certification covering its AI management process for a specified scope of services, audited by an accredited third party. A customer isn't certified by association, although AWS's certification can make the customer's own certification easier. The certificate is available in AWS Artifact (AWS, ISO/IEC 42001 FAQs).
  • Using standards with vendors: ask "Is your AI management system certified to ISO/IEC 42001, and which services does the certificate cover?" Use ISO/IEC 22989 terms in contracts so "model", "training data" and "AI system" mean the same thing to both parties.

"A certifiable management system for responsible AI" points to ISO/IEC 42001. "Common terminology for AI" points to ISO/IEC 22989. "A framework describing the components of machine-learning AI systems" points to ISO/IEC 23053. "AI risk management guidance" points to ISO/IEC 23894.

Using a certified cloud provider doesn't make your AI use certified or compliant. Your own policies, risk assessments and controls for your AI systems remain your responsibility.

Key takeaways

  • An algorithm learns from data to produce a model; training uses past examples (features and labels); inference applies the model to new cases; a prediction is a probability that a business threshold turns into an action.
  • Judge models by the business cost of their errors: false positives and false negatives cost different amounts. Recall measures how much is caught, precision how much of what is flagged is right. Accuracy alone misleads for rare events.
  • Setting the threshold is a business decision that weighs cost, customer impact and capacity.
  • AI contains ML, which contains deep learning, which contains generative AI. ML predicts and classifies from your own data; generative AI creates content from pre-trained foundation models and can hallucinate. Not all AI is ML.
  • Structured data suits classic ML; unstructured data (documents, images, audio, text) is where deep learning and generative AI add value, often by extracting structured fields. It needs permission and privacy handling.
  • Data quality (accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, uniqueness, representativeness) decides results, because AI repeats data errors at scale. Fix data before changing models, and give each data set an accountable owner.
  • Training assumes the future resembles the past. Use separate training, validation and recent test data, and watch for overfitting, historical bias, changing conditions, too little data and leakage.
  • ISO/IEC 22989 (terminology and life cycle), 23053 (ML system framework), 42001 (certifiable AI management system: assess risks, implement controls, monitor and improve), 23894 (risk) and 5259 (data quality) give AI governance its shared language. Certification is never inherited from a provider.
