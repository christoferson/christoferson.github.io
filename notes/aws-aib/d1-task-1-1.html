Fundamental AI concepts in business contexts

Skill 1.1.1Explain algorithms, models, training, inference and predictions in business terms

Most AI in business does one thing repeatedly: it looks at a case and estimates what is most likely. Will this customer cancel? Is this transaction fraudulent? How many umbrellas will this store sell next week? The five terms in this skill describe how that kind of AI is built and used.

The five terms named in the exam guide

In the order they happen: an algorithm is applied to past data in training, which produces a model. Using the model on a new case is inference, and its answer is a prediction.

Algorithm

An algorithm is a step-by-step method for solving a problem. In machine learning, it is the method a system uses to learn patterns from data: a general procedure such as "find the combination of facts that best separates customers who cancelled from customers who stayed". The same algorithm can be applied to any suitable data set, so it is like a recipe: it says how to cook, not what the finished dish tastes like.

There are many standard algorithms, and data scientists choose among them. Some, such as decision trees, produce models whose reasoning is easy to follow. Others, such as deep neural networks, are often more accurate on complex data but much harder to explain.

Why it matters to a businessThe choice of algorithm affects accuracy, cost and, above all, explainability. A bank that must tell customers why a loan was declined may need an algorithm whose reasoning can be shown.

Training

Training is the process of feeding an algorithm many past examples so it can learn the patterns in them. During training, the algorithm repeatedly makes guesses about the examples, compares them with the known answers and adjusts itself to make fewer mistakes, until its answers match the past as closely as possible.

A retailer might train on three years of daily sales for every product and store; a card issuer on millions of past transactions, each marked fraudulent or genuine. Training happens before the model is used, and is repeated when the model needs refreshing with newer data.

Why it matters to a businessTraining is a project cost: collecting and preparing the data, computing time and expert effort. It isn't a one-time cost, because models need retraining as the world changes.

Model

A model is what training produces: a reusable decision engine that has captured the patterns in the training data. If the algorithm is the recipe, the model is the finished dish. Two companies using the same algorithm on different data end up with different models, because each model reflects the data it learned from.

Once built, a model can be given a new case and will return an answer in milliseconds, consistently, as many times as needed. Generative AI uses very large models, called foundation models, that were trained by a provider on broad data and can be used for many tasks.

Why it matters to a businessThe model is the asset the business deploys, pays to run, monitors, updates and must be able to defend to auditors and regulators. Owning a model trained on your own data can be a competitive advantage.

Inference

Inference is using a trained model on a new case to get its answer. Training looks backward at the past; inference applies what was learned to the present. A card-fraud model is trained on historical transactions, then performs inference on every new card payment as it happens.

Inference can run in two ways:

  • Real-time inference: each case is sent to the model as it happens and gets an answer in seconds or less, for example scoring a payment while the customer waits at the checkout. The model must be running at all times, which costs more.
  • Batch inference: many cases are collected and processed together on a schedule, for example scoring every customer overnight for cancellation risk. Results arrive later, but processing in bulk is cheaper.

Why it matters to a businessInference is the running cost, paid every time the model is used. For a popular AI service, inference costs usually far exceed training costs over its life.

Prediction

A prediction is the model's answer for one case. It is usually a probability or score rather than a definite yes or no ("this customer is 72% likely to cancel"), or a number ("this store will sell about 140 umbrellas"). The word doesn't always mean forecasting the future; any estimate a model makes about an unknown, such as whether a photo shows a damaged car, is called a prediction.

Why it matters to a businessA prediction is a probability, never a certainty. Some predictions will be wrong, so the business must decide what to do with uncertain answers, who checks them, and what a wrong answer costs.

Extra terms Extra

The exam guide doesn't list these four, but explaining the five terms above properly requires them: features and labels are what training learns from, parameters are where a model stores what it learned, and a threshold turns a prediction into a business action.

Features

The individual facts about a case that the model is given: for a loan applicant, income, existing debt and years at their current job; for a card payment, the amount, the merchant, the country and how it compares with the customer's usual spending. A model can only learn from the facts it is shown, so choosing features is an important design decision.

Why it matters to a businessFeatures decide fairness and legality. A feature such as postal code can act as a stand-in for a protected characteristic like ethnicity, producing unfair decisions even though ethnicity was never used.

Label

The known, correct answer for a past example, which the model learns to predict: "repaid" or "defaulted" for a past loan; "fraud" or "genuine" for a past card transaction, as confirmed by the customer. Labels come from what eventually happened or from people's past decisions.

Why it matters to a businessLabels are often the most expensive data to get: someone had to decide or record each one. If nobody recorded the right answer for past cases, there is nothing to learn from.

Parameters

The internal numbers a model adjusts during training. Together they hold what the model has learned, much as the settings on a mixing desk decide the final sound. Nobody sets them by hand; training finds them. A small fraud model may have thousands; a large language model has billions.

Why it matters to a businessMore parameters generally means more capability, but also more computing power and higher running cost.

Threshold

The cut-off a business sets to turn a prediction into an action: "hold any card payment scoring above 90% for the customer to confirm", "offer a retention discount to customers above 60% likely to leave". The model says how likely something is; the threshold decides what the business does about it.

Why it matters to a businessSetting the threshold is a business decision about cost, risk and customer impact, not a technical detail. The classification section below shows how moving it changes the results.

How the terms fit together

Exam scenarios usually describe one step of this sequence. Knowing the order helps you see which term a question is really about:

  1. Frame the business question"Which customers are likely to cancel?" The question decides what the prediction is and which data is needed.
  2. Collect past examples with features and labelsPast customers, the facts about them, and whether they cancelled.
  3. Training: apply an algorithmThe algorithm learns the patterns that separated customers who left from those who stayed.
  4. A model is produced and testedIt is checked on past cases it never saw during training, to confirm it works on new data.
  5. Inference on new casesEach current customer is scored, in real time or in a nightly batch.
  6. A prediction for each case"72% likely to cancel."
  7. A threshold turns it into an actionCustomers above 60% receive a retention offer.
  8. Monitor and retrainBehavior changes over time, so accuracy is tracked and the model is trained again on newer data.

A prediction is a probability, not a fact. Many strategic questions hinge on what the business does with it: where the threshold sits, who reviews borderline cases, and what each kind of mistake costs.

Classification and how models are judged Extra

A classification model predicts which category a case belongs to: fraud or legitimate, will cancel or will stay, spam or not spam. A regression model predicts a number instead, such as next week's sales. Classification is the most common kind of business ML, and how it is judged comes up often in scenarios about model quality, fairness and cost.

The anatomy of a prediction use case

Every prediction use case, in any industry, can be described with the same four parts. Spotting them helps you recognize a prediction problem in any scenario, and shows quickly whether a use case is ready: with no label, a model can't be trained; with no clear action, even a perfect prediction has no business value.

Card issuer: payment fraud

Question: is this card payment fraudulent? Features: amount, merchant, location, time, and how they compare with the cardholder's usual behavior. Label: whether past payments were confirmed as fraud by cardholders. Action: approve, decline, or hold the payment and ask the cardholder to confirm.

Retail chain: demand forecasting

Question: how many units of each product will each store sell next week? Features: past sales, planned promotions, the weather forecast, local events. Label: units actually sold in past weeks, recorded automatically by the tills. Action: automatic replenishment orders, so shelves aren't empty and stock isn't wasted. (This one is regression: it predicts a number.)

Bank: loan decisions

Question: will this applicant repay? Features: income, existing debt, repayment history. Label: whether past borrowers repaid or defaulted, which can take years to become known. Action: approve, decline, or refer to a human underwriter.

Telecom provider: cancellations

Question: will this subscriber cancel in the next 90 days? Features: usage trend, complaints, contract end date. Label: whether past subscribers cancelled or stayed. Action: a retention offer to those most likely to leave.

Manufacturer: machine failures

Question: will this machine fail in the next 30 days? Features: vibration, temperature, hours since service. Label: whether past machines failed. Action: schedule maintenance before a breakdown stops production.

The four possible outcomes (the confusion matrix)

Every yes/no prediction ends in one of four outcomes. Laid out in a two-by-two grid of predicted against actual, they are called a confusion matrix. Each outcome has a different business cost, which is why leaders need them. Using card-fraud detection as the example:

True positive

The model flagged the case, and it really was fraud. The fraudulent payment is stopped.

False positive (false alarm)

The model flagged the case, but it was legitimate. A genuine customer's payment is held or declined, which is annoying at best and may send them to use a competitor's card.

False negative (miss)

The model didn't flag the case, but it was fraud. The fraudulent payment goes through, and the issuer usually has to refund the cardholder.

True negative

The model didn't flag the case, and it was legitimate. The payment goes through normally, as it should.

Precision and recall

  • Recall: of all the real fraud, how much did the model catch? It answers "how much are we missing?"
  • Precision: of everything the model flagged, how much was really fraud? It answers "how many false alarms are we raising?"

Raising recall usually lowers precision, and the reverse: flag more cases and you catch more fraud, but also flag more honest customers. Which matters more depends on what each mistake costs. In cancer screening, a miss is far worse than a false alarm, so recall comes first. In a spam filter, sending a real customer's email to the spam folder is worse, so precision comes first.

"99.8% accurate" can be meaningless. If 0.2% of card payments are fraud, a model that never flags anything is 99.8% accurate and catches nothing. For rare events, ask for recall and precision, or better, the cost of each kind of error.

Worked example: choosing the threshold

A card issuer processes 1,000,000 card payments a month, and about 0.2% (2,000) are fraudulent. Each flagged payment triggers a text message asking the cardholder to confirm, costing about $4 including support calls; an average fraudulent payment that goes through costs the issuer $400. The data science team reports two candidate thresholds for the same model:

MeasureStrict thresholdLenient threshold
Payments flagged6,00020,000
Frauds caught (recall)1,500 of 2,000 (75%)1,850 of 2,000 (92.5%)
Genuine payments flagged4,500 (precision 25%)18,150 (precision 9.25%)
Confirmation cost6,000 × $4 = $24,00020,000 × $4 = $80,000
Fraud losses (misses)500 × $400 = $200,000150 × $400 = $60,000
Total cost of the two errors$224,000$140,000

On cost alone the lenient threshold wins by $84,000 a month. But it interrupts 18,150 genuine customers a month instead of 4,500, at the checkout or online, and some of them will switch to another card. The model is the same in both columns. Choosing between them is a business decision weighing losses, customer experience and the capacity of the support team, and it belongs to the business owner, not the data scientists.

AI, machine learning and generative AI

Skill 1.1.2Distinguish AI, machine learning and generative AI

The four terms are nested, each a subset of the one before: artificial intelligence contains machine learning, which contains deep learning, which contains generative AI (AWS Cloud Adoption Framework for AI, ML and generative AI). The distinction matters in practice because each level needs different data, costs differently, fails in different ways and needs different controls.

Artificial intelligence (AI)

The broadest term: the field of building machines that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognizing images, making decisions or solving problems. AWS's framework notes that most AI systems produce probabilistic outcomes, predictions or decisions made with a degree of certainty, and that AI is used to automate or augment knowledge work.

AI isn't one technique. It includes hand-written rules ("if the driver is under 18, decline"), search and planning systems, and machine learning. A rules engine that applies fixed business policies is AI in the broad sense, even though it learns nothing.

ExamplesA chess program; a rules engine checking loan eligibility; a voice assistant; every example below.

Machine learning (ML)

The part of AI where systems learn from data instead of being given explicit instructions. Rather than a person writing rules for what fraud looks like, an ML system is shown thousands of past examples and works out the patterns itself. It then applies those patterns to new cases. AWS describes ML models as generalizing from examples rather than relying on explicit programming.

ML is usually built for one specific task: one model for fraud, another for forecasting demand, another for predicting cancellations. Its output is typically a prediction with a probability, and it works best with structured data such as tables of transactions. Building it needs your own historical data, usually with labels, and a training project.

Good atFinding patterns across many factors that people can't track by hand; making consistent predictions at high volume.

LimitationsNeeds enough good historical data; only as good and as fair as that data; can degrade as the world changes; some models are hard to explain.

ExamplesCredit scoring, fraud detection, demand forecasting, product recommendations, predicting machine failures.

Deep learning

A kind of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers. Each layer learns to recognize increasingly complex patterns: in an image, the first layers detect edges, later layers shapes, and the last layers whole objects such as a dented car door. AWS notes deep learning is especially strong with unstructured data such as images and text, and led to breakthroughs in image and speech recognition.

Good atImages, audio, video and language, where hand-crafting features is impossible.

LimitationsNeeds large amounts of data and computing power; its reasoning is hard to explain.

ExamplesRecognizing damage in photos, transcribing calls to text, reading handwriting, detecting tumors in scans.

Generative AI

A kind of deep learning that creates new content, such as text, images, code, audio or video, rather than only classifying or predicting. It is built on foundation models: very large models trained by providers on huge, broad data sets, which can then be used for many different tasks without task-specific training. You instruct them in natural language (a prompt) instead of training them on your own labeled data.

Good atDrafting, summarizing, translating, answering questions in natural language, extracting information from documents, writing code.

LimitationsCan produce confident but false content (hallucinations); output can vary between runs; may reflect bias in its training data; costs more per request than a simple ML model; quality is harder to check.

ExamplesA chatbot answering policy questions, summaries of long documents, draft marketing copy, a coding assistant.

Generative AI terms

Foundation model (FM)

A model with a very large number of parameters, trained on a massive amount of diverse data, that can generate a wide range of responses for many use cases (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Key terminology). Organizations rarely train their own; they use providers' models through services such as Amazon Bedrock.

Large language model (LLM)

A foundation model specialized in language: it reads and writes text. Most chat assistants are built on LLMs.

Multimodal model

A model that works with more than one kind of content, for example reading a photo and writing a description of it, or answering a question about a chart.

Hallucination

Fluent, confident output that is false. It happens because a generative model produces the most plausible response given its training, not a checked fact. Hallucinations are most likely when the model lacks the information needed.

AspectTraditional MLGenerative AI

Two points worth remembering: traditional ML mostly classifies or predicts, while generative AI produces new content (AWS, What is generative AI?). And all ML is AI, but not all AI is ML (AWS, The difference between artificial intelligence and machine learning).

Recognizing the category in a scenario

Flag transactions likely to be fraudulent

Machine learning: a yes/no prediction from structured history with known outcomes.

Estimate repair cost from photos of a damaged car

Deep learning: interpreting images needs neural networks.

Draft a personalized reply to a complaint email

Generative AI: creating new text in natural language.

Decline any application from someone under the legal age

Rules (AI in the broad sense, not ML): a fixed policy; there is nothing to learn.

Forecast next month's call volume for staffing

Machine learning: a numeric prediction from historical patterns.

Answer employees' questions about HR policy in plain language

Generative AI with company documents supplied to it: conversational answers grounded in the organization's own text.

Scenario wording points to the category: "predict", "score", "classify", "forecast" usually means ML; "draft", "summarize", "generate", "answer in natural language" means generative AI; "a fixed policy applied the same way every time" may need no ML at all.

"Generative AI" isn't a synonym for "AI". Using a foundation model to make a simple yes/no prediction on tabular data is usually more expensive, less consistent and harder to validate than a purpose-built ML model.

Structured and unstructured data

Skill 1.1.3Distinguish structured and unstructured data and why the type matters

The type of data decides which kind of AI can use it, how much preparation it needs and what governance it requires. Knowing what data an organization has, and in what form, is often the first step in deciding which AI projects are realistic.

Structured data

Data organized in a fixed format of rows and columns, where every column has a defined meaning and type: a date, an amount, a product code. It is what spreadsheets and databases hold. Because the meaning is fixed, structured data is easy to search, count, sort and combine with other tables.

ExamplesSales transactions, account balances, customer records, order tables, sensor readings logged at fixed intervals.

How AI uses itClassic machine learning: credit scores, fraud scores, demand forecasts, churn prediction and pricing models all learn from structured data.

Preparation neededConsistent definitions across systems ("active customer" must mean the same everywhere), and the ability to join tables, for example matching the same customer across systems.

Semi-structured data

Data with labeled fields but no fixed table layout: each record says what its values are, but records can differ in which fields they contain. It sits between the two other types.

ExamplesJSON or XML messages between systems, web form submissions, application logs, the header fields of an email (sender, date, subject).

How AI uses itUsually converted into structured fields for ML; also useful as context for generative AI.

Unstructured data

Data without a predefined format, whose meaning has to be interpreted: free text, documents, images, audio and video. A computer can store a PDF of a contract or a recording of a call, but can't tell what it says without AI to interpret it.

ExamplesEmails, contracts, scanned forms, photos, call recordings, medical images, social media posts.

How AI uses itDeep learning and generative AI: extracting fields from documents, analyzing images, converting speech to text, summarizing, answering questions over documents.

Preparation neededExtraction, transcription or splitting into sections; permission checks (who may see which document); privacy handling, because recordings and emails often contain personal details.

Turning unstructured data into structured data

A common first AI project is extraction: reading documents and producing structured fields that existing systems can use. Supplier invoices are the classic case. A company's accounts payable team receives thousands of invoices as PDFs or scans, each in the supplier's own layout, and staff type the key figures into the finance system before the invoice can be checked and paid. The input is free text:

Why the type matters for planning

  • Where the value is: much of an organization's information is unstructured: emails, documents, recordings. It used to be expensive to use; generative AI and document extraction make it usable, which is why many first generative AI projects target documents and conversations.
  • Effort: structured data is ready for ML once definitions are consistent; unstructured data usually needs extra processing first.
  • Governance: unstructured data is harder to control. One call recording can contain card numbers and health details; one shared folder can mix public brochures with confidential notes. Permissions and privacy handling must be decided before the data feeds an AI system.

"Thousands of PDFs and scanned forms that staff retype into a system" signals document extraction. "Predict which customers will leave from their account history" signals ML on structured data. "Let staff ask questions about our manuals" signals generative AI over unstructured documents.

Why data quality matters for AI outcomes

Skill 1.1.4Explain why data quality decides AI outcomes

Data quality is how well data fits the purpose it is used for: whether it is correct, complete, consistent, current and representative. It matters more for AI than for ordinary software. With ordinary software, a wrong value affects one record. A model learns from data, so errors become part of the patterns it learns and are repeated in every prediction, at the speed and scale of automation. The result is a model that is wrong in confident, systematic ways that are hard to spot because the output looks authoritative. The ISO/IEC 5259 series (Data quality for analytics and machine learning) exists for this reason.

Accuracy

Whether values match reality. Example: a hospital codes some readmissions under the wrong diagnosis. Effect: a model predicting readmission risk learns false links between conditions and outcomes.

Completeness

Whether required values or records are present. Example: a lender recorded loans sold to a collection agency as "closed" instead of "defaulted". Effect: the model learns that those borrowers were safe.

Consistency

Whether the same thing means the same in every system. Example: "active customer" means someone who bought in the last 12 months in the sales system but anyone with an account in the marketing system. Effect: features contradict each other, and reports disagree.

Timeliness

Whether data is current enough for the decision. Example: a retailer's stock levels update only overnight. Effect: a model recommending products online suggests items that sold out hours ago.

Validity

Whether data follows the expected format and business rules. Example: delivery dates earlier than the order dates, or ages of 150. Effect: impossible records distort training.

Uniqueness

Whether each real-world item appears once. Example: the same customer registered twice, once online and once in a store. Effect: some patterns count double, and the customer gets two sets of marketing.

Representativeness

Whether the data covers all the kinds of cases the AI will meet. Example: a voice assistant trained mostly on one accent, or a lender's history containing almost no customers from a newly entered region. Effect: poor and possibly unfair results for the under-represented group.

Worked example: how one data gap spreads

A consumer lender's collections team sold its worst defaulted loans to a debt-collection agency, and the loan system recorded them as "closed" rather than "defaulted". To a model, those borrowers look like people who repaid:

  1. The data is wrongThousands of defaulted loans are labeled as successfully closed.
  2. The model learns the wrong lessonThe profile of those borrowers looks "safe", so similar new applicants get high approval scores.
  3. Testing doesn't catch itThe test data has the same mislabeling, so the model appears accurate.
  4. Losses follow in productionThe lender approves more of exactly the applicants most likely to default.
  5. The fix is in the data, not the modelCorrect the historical labels from collections records, retrain, and change the process so sold loans are recorded as defaults.

Data quality for generative AI

The same applies to the documents a generative AI assistant draws on. If an assistant answers from a folder holding both last year's and this year's versions of a policy handbook, it may give one customer the old rule and the next customer the new one, fluently and with confidence. Quality work for generative AI means removing outdated versions, resolving contradictions, assigning an owner to each document set and setting review dates.

Who owns data quality

Data quality is a business responsibility, not only a technical one. The people who create the data decide whether it is recorded correctly. Organizations typically name a data owner for each important data set, accountable for its definitions and quality, and measure quality before an AI project begins rather than discovering problems after launch.

When a scenario describes a disappointing AI result together with messy, siloed or incomplete data, the first fix is usually improving the data (ownership, cleaning, consistent definitions), not choosing a bigger model.

More data isn't automatically better data. Adding millions of poorly labeled or unrepresentative records can make a model worse.

Training a model on historical data

Skill 1.1.5Explain how a model is trained on historical data

Training means learning from past examples, which builds in one key assumption: the future will resemble the past. That assumption explains most of what can go wrong, and it is why a model's quality has to be checked both before launch and continually afterward.

Ways a model can learn

Supervised learning

The model learns from past examples that come with the right answer (labels). It studies which features went with which answers, then predicts the answer for new cases. There are two main kinds: classification, which predicts a category (fraud or not, will cancel or not), and regression, which predicts a number (next week's sales, a repair cost).

ExamplesLoans labeled repaid or defaulted; emails labeled by department; card payments labeled fraud or genuine; past sales figures.

Business noteThe most common kind in business. Its biggest cost is often the labels: someone has to decide the right answer for thousands of past cases, sometimes experts. A model needing radiologists to label 50,000 scans has a very different budget from one whose labels already sit in a database ("did the customer cancel?").

Unsupervised learning

The model is given data without labels and finds structure on its own. Clustering groups similar cases together; anomaly detection finds cases unlike the rest.

ExamplesGrouping customers into segments for marketing; spotting a transaction unlike anything seen before; finding unusual machine sensor readings.

Business noteUseful when there are no labels, or when you don't yet know what to look for. Its results need people to interpret them.

Reinforcement learning

The model learns by trial and error: it takes actions, receives rewards for good outcomes or penalties for bad ones, and gradually learns which actions work best.

ExamplesOptimizing warehouse robot routes; controlling energy use in a building; refining how generative models respond, using human feedback on which answers are better.

Business noteRarely built directly by business teams, but it shapes many of the generative AI models they use.

How a supervised model is trained, in business terms

  1. Gather and label historyFor example, five years of loan applications (about 400,000) and whether each loan was repaid.
  2. Split the history three waysFor example 70% for training, 15% for validation (to compare versions and tune the model during development) and 15% for the final test. The test portion is never shown during development, like an exam paper kept sealed.
  3. TrainThe algorithm adjusts the model's parameters until its predictions match the training labels as well as possible.
  4. Tune on the validation setTry variations and keep the one that does best on data it didn't learn from.
  5. Test once on the held-back dataGood results on unseen cases show the model learned general patterns rather than memorizing examples.
  6. Decide if it's good enoughCompare against the business baseline: does it beat today's process, at an acceptable cost of mistakes? Is it fair across customer groups?

A sensible split also respects time. If the model will score next year's cases, test it on the most recent cases, not a random sample: patterns from five years ago may tell you little about next year.

What can go wrong

Overfitting

The model memorizes the training data, including its noise, instead of learning general patterns, so it performs well on past cases and poorly on new ones. Example: 99% accurate on training data, 70% on new cases. Response: insist on results from held-back, recent data before approving launch.

Underfitting

The model is too simple to capture the real pattern and is barely better than guessing on any data. Response: better features or a more capable method, or accept that the problem may not suit ML.

Historical bias

Past decisions were unfair, and the model learns to repeat them. Example: a hiring model trained on past hires favors the groups hired before. Response: fairness testing across groups, review of features, human oversight on high-impact decisions.

Changing conditions

The world moves away from the training data. Example: demand forecasts trained before a pandemic failed when buying patterns changed overnight. Response: monitor accuracy in production and retrain on recent data.

Too little data

Rare events have too few examples to learn from. Example: only 40 known cases of a new fraud scheme. Response: combine with expert rules, collect more examples, or keep the decision with people.

Data leakage

The training data contains information that wouldn't be available at the moment of prediction, making test results look better than reality. Example: training data includes "investigator assigned", which only appears after a case is already suspected. Response: check that every feature is available when the prediction is made.

Labeling services exist to produce training data with human reviewers. Amazon SageMaker Ground Truth, AWS's human labeling service, is no longer open to new customers; existing customers can continue to use it.

"The model was 95% accurate in testing" isn't the end of the story. Ask what it was tested on (recent, representative, held-back data?), what the 5% of errors cost the business, whether it performs equally for all customer groups, and how its accuracy will be checked after launch.

Global frameworks and a unified AI vocabulary

Skill 1.1.6Know the global frameworks that give AI a shared vocabulary (ISO/IEC 23053, ISO/IEC 42001)

When business, legal and technical teams and vendors use the same words differently, governance fails quietly. One team's "model validation" is a quick spot check; another's is a formal independent review. One contract's "AI system" includes the data pipeline; another's means only the model. International standards, published jointly by ISO (the International Organization for Standardization) and IEC (the International Electrotechnical Commission), give organizations a shared vocabulary and a recognized structure for discussing, buying, auditing and governing AI.

ISO/IEC 22989:2022: AI concepts and terminology

The common dictionary of AI. It defines core terms (AI system, machine learning, training data, model, and many more) and describes the life cycle an AI system goes through. Using its definitions in policies and contracts means everyone, including vendors, understands terms the same way.

ISO/IEC 23053:2022: Framework for AI systems using machine learning

A reference description of the parts of an ML-based AI system (data, the model, training, evaluation, deployment, monitoring) and how they fit together. It helps organizations describe their own systems consistently and compare vendors' solutions like for like.

ISO/IEC 42001:2023: AI management system

Requirements for an AI management system (AIMS): the policies, roles and repeatable processes an organization uses to develop, provide or use AI responsibly. It works like ISO/IEC 27001 does for information security: an organization builds the system, and an accredited auditor can certify it.

ISO/IEC 23894:2023: AI risk management

Guidance on identifying, assessing and treating risks specific to AI, building on general risk management practice.

ISO/IEC 5259 series: data quality for analytics and ML

Terms, measures and processes for managing the quality of data used in analytics and machine learning.

The AI system life cycle (ISO/IEC 22989) Extra

ISO/IEC 22989 describes seven stages an AI system passes through, and AWS recommends applying governance at every stage (AWS Security Blog, AI lifecycle risk management: ISO/IEC 42001:2023 for AI governance, May 2025):

  1. InceptionDefine the problem, the business owner and the acceptable risks.
  2. Design and developmentChoose data and features, exclude ones that could act as stand-ins for protected characteristics, and build the model.
  3. Verification and validationTest accuracy, fairness across groups and robustness before approval.
  4. DeploymentRelease to production with access controls, documented thresholds and human review.
  5. Operation and monitoringTrack accuracy, drift and complaints.
  6. Re-evaluationReview periodically: is it still accurate, fair and needed?
  7. RetirementWithdraw it safely, replace it or return to manual handling, and keep the records.

ISO/IEC 42001 in practice Extra

ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving an AI management system (AWS, ISO/IEC 42001 FAQs). Its core cycle, as described by AWS:

  1. Assess risksIdentify and assess the risks of each AI system (clause 6.1). For high-risk uses, add an AI impact assessment covering societal, ethical and legal effects on people, similar to a data protection impact assessment.
  2. Implement controlsPut operational controls in place to treat those risks (clause 8), choosing from the standard's reference controls in Annex A and recording which apply in a Statement of Applicability.
  3. Monitor, document and improveMeasure how well the controls work, audit them and improve continually (clauses 9 and 10).

Organizations pursue it for trust (customers increasingly ask suppliers how they govern AI, and a certificate is independent evidence), regulatory readiness, and consistency, so that every AI project follows the same risk process.

Certification and suppliers

  • Certification belongs to one organization: AWS holds an ISO/IEC 42001 certification covering its AI management process for a specified scope of services. A customer isn't certified by association, although AWS's certification can make the customer's own certification easier. The certificate is available in AWS Artifact (AWS, ISO/IEC 42001 FAQs).
  • Using standards with vendors: ask "Is your AI management system certified to ISO/IEC 42001, and which services does the certificate cover?" Use ISO/IEC 22989 terms in contracts so "model", "training data" and "AI system" mean the same thing to both sides.

"A certifiable management system for responsible AI" points to ISO/IEC 42001. "Common terminology for AI" points to ISO/IEC 22989. "A framework describing the components of machine-learning AI systems" points to ISO/IEC 23053. "AI risk management guidance" points to ISO/IEC 23894.

Using a certified cloud provider doesn't make your own AI use certified or compliant. Your policies, risk assessments and controls remain your responsibility.

Practice questions

Exam-style questions on this task. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
A retailer wants to predict which customers will stop buying in the next three months, using purchase history labeled with who actually left. Which approach fits?
  1. A. Unsupervised learning, because customers are grouped
  2. B. Supervised learning, because past outcomes are labeled
  3. C. Reinforcement learning, because the model learns from rewards
  4. D. Generative AI, because it creates new customer profiles
Answer
B
Why
The data has known outcomes (left or stayed), which is the definition of supervised learning. Grouping without labels would be unsupervised; reinforcement learning needs an environment and rewards; generative AI creates content rather than predicting a labeled outcome.
Question 2
Scenario
A model scores 99% accuracy in testing but performs poorly on new customers. What is the most likely cause?
  1. A. The model is underfitting
  2. B. The model is overfitting or the test data leaked training information
  3. C. The inference endpoint is too slow
  4. D. The data is unstructured
Answer
B
Why
Excellent test results that do not carry over to new data point to overfitting or data leakage. Underfitting would show poor results in testing too; speed and data format do not explain the gap.
Question 3
Scenario
Which statement correctly describes the relationship between AI, machine learning and generative AI?
  1. A. Generative AI includes machine learning, which includes AI
  2. B. Machine learning is a subset of AI, and generative AI is a subset of deep learning within machine learning
  3. C. They are three unrelated technologies
  4. D. AI is a subset of machine learning
Answer
B
Why
AI is the broadest field; machine learning learns from data; deep learning uses neural networks; generative AI is a deep learning approach that creates new content.
Question 4
Scenario
A team plans to train a model on five years of loan decisions. An analyst finds that 8% of records have the wrong outcome label. What should the business leader prioritize first?
  1. A. Train anyway; the model will average out errors
  2. B. Fix label quality before training, because the model will learn the errors
  3. C. Switch to a larger model
  4. D. Use generative AI instead
Answer
B
Why
Models learn whatever the data teaches, including mistakes. Data quality, here label accuracy, comes before model choice.

Key takeaways

  • An algorithm is applied to past data in training, producing a model; inference applies the model to new cases; each answer is a prediction, a probability that a business threshold turns into an action.
  • Features and labels are what supervised models learn from; parameters store what they learned. Inference runs in real time or in batches and is the main running cost.
  • Classification predicts categories and is judged by its errors: false positives and false negatives cost different amounts. Recall measures how much is caught, precision how much of what is flagged is right; accuracy alone misleads for rare events.
  • AI contains ML, which contains deep learning, which contains generative AI. ML learns patterns from your data for one task; deep learning handles images, audio and text; generative AI creates content from foundation models and can hallucinate. Not all AI is ML.
  • Structured data (rows and columns) suits classic ML; unstructured data (text, documents, images, audio) is where deep learning and generative AI add value, often by extracting structured fields, and it needs permission and privacy handling.
  • Data quality (accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, uniqueness, representativeness) decides results because AI repeats data errors at scale. Fix data before changing models, and give each data set an owner.
  • Supervised learning uses labels (classification and regression), unsupervised learning finds structure (clustering, anomalies), reinforcement learning learns from rewards. Training assumes the future resembles the past: use held-back, recent test data and watch for overfitting, bias, changing conditions, too little data and leakage.
  • ISO/IEC 22989 (terminology), 23053 (ML system framework), 42001 (certifiable AI management system), 23894 (risk) and 5259 (data quality) give AI governance a shared language. Certification is never inherited from a provider.
