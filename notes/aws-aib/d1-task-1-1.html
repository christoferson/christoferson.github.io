Fundamental AI concepts in business contexts

Skill 1.1.1

Most AI in business answers one question repeatedly: given what we know about this case, what is most likely? The vocabulary describes how that answer is produced and used.

TermPlain meaningAt Harbor Mutual
AlgorithmThe method a system uses to learn patterns from data; the recipeThe learning method the data science team picks for fraud scoring
ModelThe result of applying the algorithm to data: a reusable decision engine that captures the patterns it learnedThe fraud model, which has learned what suspicious claims have looked like
TrainingShowing the algorithm many past examples so it builds the modelFive years of claims, each marked fraudulent or legitimate
FeaturesThe facts about a case the model looks atClaim amount, days since the policy started, number of past claims
LabelThe known answer for a past example"Fraud confirmed" or "legitimate" after investigation
InferenceUsing the trained model on a new caseScoring each new claim as it arrives
PredictionThe model's output for that case, usually with a confidence score"82% likely to be fraudulent"
ThresholdThe business rule that turns a score into an actionClaims scoring above 70% go to an investigator; the rest are paid normally

From business question to business decision, the steps are always the same:

  1. Frame the question"Which claims are likely fraudulent?" A clear question decides which data and which kind of AI are needed.
  2. Collect historical examplesPast claims with known outcomes.
  3. Train the modelThe algorithm learns the patterns that separated fraud from legitimate claims.
  4. Evaluate itTest on past claims the model hasn't seen: how many frauds does it catch, and how many honest customers does it flag?
  5. Use it for inferenceEach new claim gets a score within seconds.
  6. Act on the predictionThe threshold routes high scores to people; the investigator makes the final decision.
  7. Monitor and retrainFraud patterns change, so accuracy is tracked and the model retrained.

A prediction is a probability, not a fact. Strategic questions often hinge on what the business does with it: where the threshold sits, who reviews borderline cases, and what a wrong prediction costs.

AI, machine learning and generative AI

Skill 1.1.2

The terms are nested, each a subset of the one before (AWS Cloud Adoption Framework for AI, ML and generative AI whitepaper):

TermWhat it isHow it worksAt Harbor Mutual
Artificial intelligence (AI)The broad field of machines performing tasks that normally need human intelligenceMany methods, including hand-written rules, search and machine learningEverything below, plus an older rules engine that checks policy eligibility
Machine learning (ML)AI that learns patterns from data instead of following explicit instructionsTrained on examples for one specific task; outputs come with a probability or confidenceThe fraud-scoring model; a model that predicts which customers will cancel
Deep learningML using many-layered neural networksEspecially good with unstructured data such as images, audio and textEstimating car damage from claim photos; transcribing calls
Generative AIDeep learning that creates new content: text, images, code, audioBuilt on foundation models trained on very broad data, usable for many tasks without task-specific trainingDrafting replies to customer emails; summarizing a claim file for an adjuster
  • Classify versus create: traditional ML mostly classifies or predicts (fraud or not, likely to cancel or not). Generative AI produces new content (AWS, What is generative AI?).
  • Not all AI is ML: a rules engine that applies "decline if the driver is under 18" is AI in the broad sense but learns nothing. AWS puts it plainly: all ML is AI, but not all AI is ML (AWS, The difference between artificial intelligence and machine learning).
  • Typical limitations of generative AI: it can produce confident but inaccurate content (hallucinations), carry over bias from its training data, be hard to explain, and cost more per task than a simple model.

Scenario wording points to the category: "predict", "score", "classify", "forecast" usually means ML; "draft", "summarize", "generate", "answer in natural language" means generative AI; "a fixed policy that must be applied the same way every time" may need no ML at all.

"Generative AI" isn't a synonym for "AI". An answer that uses a foundation model to make a simple yes/no prediction on tabular data is usually more expensive and less reliable than a purpose-built ML model.

Structured and unstructured data

Skill 1.1.3
TypeWhat it looks likeAt Harbor MutualTypical AI use
StructuredRows and columns with a fixed meaning; easy to query and countPolicy table, claims table, paymentsClassic ML: fraud scores, churn prediction, pricing
Semi-structuredLabeled fields without a fixed table layoutWeb form submissions, app event logsML after the fields are extracted
UnstructuredFree text, documents, images, audio and video; meaning has to be interpretedClaim photos, repair estimates as PDFs, call recordings, emailsDeep learning and generative AI: document extraction, image analysis, speech-to-text, summarization
  • Where the value is: most of an organization's information is unstructured. Until recently it was expensive to use; generative AI and document extraction make it usable, which is why many first GenAI projects target documents and conversations.
  • What it means for planning: structured data needs consistent definitions across systems; unstructured data needs permission checks (who may see which document), privacy handling (recordings and emails contain personal data) and often extraction before ML can use it.

"Thousands of PDFs and scanned forms that staff retype into a system" signals document extraction (unstructured to structured). "Predict which policies will lapse from account history" signals ML on structured data.

Why data quality matters for AI outcomes

Skill 1.1.4

A model learns whatever the data shows, including its mistakes. Poor data produces a model that is wrong in confident, systematic ways, and the errors are repeated at the speed of automation. The ISO/IEC 5259 series (Data quality for analytics and machine learning) exists for this reason.

Quality dimensionQuestion to askHarbor Mutual exampleEffect on the AI
AccuracyDoes the data match reality?Some claims are coded "water damage" when the cause was stormThe model learns false patterns about causes
CompletenessAre values or records missing?Fraud outcomes were never recorded for claims settled by one branchThe model underestimates fraud from that branch
ConsistencyDoes the same thing mean the same in every system?"Customer since" uses different dates in the policy and billing systemsFeatures contradict each other
TimelinessIs it current enough for the decision?Address changes reach the claims system weeks latePredictions are made on stale facts
ValidityDoes it follow the expected format and rules?Policy start dates after the claim dateImpossible records distort training
UniquenessAre records duplicated?The same claim entered twice from two channelsSome patterns count double
RepresentativenessDoes the data cover all the cases the AI will see?History contains almost no claims from a newly entered regionPoor and possibly unfair predictions for that region

For generative AI the same applies to the documents it draws on: if the policy handbook used to answer customers is outdated or contradicts itself, the assistant will repeat the outdated or contradictory answer fluently.

When a scenario describes a disappointing AI result and messy, siloed or incomplete data, the first fix is usually improving the data (ownership, cleaning, consistent definitions), not choosing a bigger model.

Training a model on historical data

Skill 1.1.5

Training means learning from past examples, which builds in an assumption: the future will resemble the past. That assumption explains most of what can go wrong.

Learning typeLearns fromHarbor Mutual example
SupervisedHistorical examples with known answers (labels)Claims labeled fraud or legitimate; policies labeled renewed or lapsed
UnsupervisedData without labels, finding groups and unusual casesGrouping customers into segments; spotting claims unlike any seen before
ReinforcementTrial, error and rewards for good outcomesRarely used directly in insurance operations; known for optimization and for refining how generative models respond

How a supervised model is trained, in business terms:

  1. Gather and label historyFive years of claims and their investigated outcomes. Labels often cost the most: someone had to decide each outcome.
  2. Hold some history backPart of the data is kept aside and never shown during training, so the model can be tested fairly.
  3. TrainThe algorithm adjusts the model until its predictions match the training labels as well as possible.
  4. Test on the held-back dataGood results on unseen claims show the model learned general patterns, not just memorized examples.
  5. Decide if it's good enoughCompare against the business baseline: is it better than today's manual rules, at an acceptable cost of mistakes?
RiskWhat happensBusiness consequence
OverfittingThe model memorizes the training data and fails on new casesGreat results in the pilot, poor results in production
Historical biasPast decisions were unfair, and the model learns to repeat themFairness, legal and reputational risk (for example, a model trained on past underwriting decisions)
Changing conditionsThe world moves away from the training data (new fraud schemes, new products, a new region)Accuracy quietly declines unless performance is monitored and the model retrained
Too little dataRare events have too few examples to learn fromThe model can't recognize the cases that matter most

"The model was 95% accurate in testing" isn't the end of the story. Ask what it was tested on (recent, representative data?), what the 5% of errors cost the business, and how its accuracy will be checked after launch.

Global frameworks and a unified AI vocabulary

Skill 1.1.6

When business, legal, technical teams and vendors use the same words differently, governance fails quietly. International standards give organizations a shared vocabulary and a reference structure for discussing, buying and governing AI. The ones to know:

StandardTitleWhat it gives an organization
ISO/IEC 22989:2022Artificial intelligence concepts and terminologyStandard definitions of AI terms, so policies and contracts mean the same thing to everyone
ISO/IEC 23053:2022Framework for AI systems using machine learningA common description of the parts and life cycle of an ML-based AI system, useful for describing and comparing solutions
ISO/IEC 42001:2023Artificial intelligence — Management systemRequirements for an AI management system (AIMS): policies, roles, risk handling and continual improvement for responsible AI. Organizations can be certified against it.
ISO/IEC 23894:2023Artificial intelligence — Guidance on risk managementHow to identify, assess and treat AI-specific risks
ISO/IEC 5259 seriesData quality for analytics and machine learningTerms and processes for measuring and managing data quality
  • Certification is per organization: AWS holds an ISO/IEC 42001 certification for in-scope services, but a customer isn't certified by association; it can make the customer's own certification easier (AWS, ISO/IEC 42001 FAQs).
  • How a strategist uses them: as the shared glossary for an AI policy, as questions to ask vendors ("Is your AI management system certified to ISO/IEC 42001?"), and as the backbone of an AI governance program (roles, risk assessment, review cycles).

"A certifiable management system for responsible AI" points to ISO/IEC 42001. "Common terminology for AI" points to ISO/IEC 22989. "A framework describing the components of machine-learning AI systems" points to ISO/IEC 23053.

Key takeaways

  • An algorithm learns from data to produce a model; training uses past examples (features and labels); inference applies the model to new cases; a prediction is a probability that a business threshold turns into an action.
  • AI contains ML, which contains deep learning, which contains generative AI. ML predicts and classifies; generative AI creates content from foundation models. Not all AI is ML.
  • Structured data suits classic ML; unstructured data (documents, images, audio, text) is where deep learning and generative AI add value, and it needs permission and privacy handling.
  • Data quality (accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, uniqueness, representativeness) decides results; fixing data usually beats changing models.
  • Training assumes the future resembles the past: watch for overfitting, historical bias, changing conditions and too little data, and always compare against a business baseline.
  • ISO/IEC 22989 (terminology), 23053 (ML system framework), 42001 (certifiable AI management system), 23894 (risk) and 5259 (data quality) provide the shared language for governing AI.
