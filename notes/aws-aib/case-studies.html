Case 1: From shadow AI to a first production win

Insurance · Domains 1, 2, 3, 4

Harbor Mutual, a home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees and a 400-agent contact center, has no AI strategy. A staff survey finds that a third of employees already use free public chatbots, some pasting in customer emails. The CEO asks the newly appointed head of AI for a plan.

FactDetail
ObjectivesClaim cycle time from 12 to 7 days; cost per call down 15%; fraud losses down $3 million a year
ReadinessGovernance 1 of 5; data 2; culture 2 (fear of job cuts, a failed 2022 chatbot)
Contact center24,000 calls a day; after-call notes take about 6 minutes
  1. Decision 1: what to do about shadow AIOptions: ban all AI tools; ignore it; classify tools and offer an alternative. Decision: publish a register (approved, under evaluation, blocked), block public chatbots for customer data, and fast-track an approved company assistant. Why: a ban pushes use underground and loses the productivity staff want; the Generative AI Security Scoping Matrix puts public chatbots in Scope 1, where providers may use submitted data.
  2. Decision 2: governance before anything customer-facingDecision: form a cross-functional steering committee, publish an AI policy and risk tiers, and start AI literacy training for leaders. Why: governance scored lowest and blocked the most use cases; it is also the cheapest gap to close.
  3. Decision 3: which use case firstOptions: customer chatbot, automated underwriting, after-call summaries. Decision: after-call summaries. Why: highest weighted score (4.25); internal; agents review every summary (medium risk); self-contained data; a reusable summarization pattern; a willing business owner.
  4. Decision 4: build, buy or partnerDecision: build on Amazon Bedrock with a partner, with handover to two internal engineers. Why: the summaries must fit Harbor Mutual's note format and systems, and the internal team needs the skills for later use cases.
  5. Decision 5: how to measureDecision: a four-week baseline (6.2 minutes), then an eight-week pilot with 40 agents and a comparison group of 40. Why: a new phone system installed at the same time saved 0.3 minutes for everyone, so the AI's real effect is 1.3 minutes, not the 1.6 a before-and-after comparison would claim.
  6. Decision 6: how to communicateDecision: announce the pilot four weeks ahead through team managers: the agent reviews every draft; no job losses from this tool, with fewer new hires as volume grows; no individual rating on edits. Why: fear of job cuts was the main adoption risk; the commitment is one leadership can keep.
  7. Decision 7: scaleEvidence: usage reached 90% by week 8, 92% of summaries accepted unedited, 1.3 minutes saved. Decision: scale to all 400 agents in waves. Value: about $2.4 million a year in avoided hiring against about $405,000 a year in running costs; three-year ROI about 283%.

What this case tests:

  • Shadow AI response.
  • Readiness gaps and their order.
  • Use case prioritization.
  • Sourcing.
  • Baselines and comparison groups.
  • Honest communication.
  • Leading indicators.
  • Conservative ROI that counts only realized cash benefit.

Case 2: The customer chatbot that wasn't ready

Insurance · Domains 1, 3

A year later, Harbor Mutual's marketing team wants a customer-facing assistant on the website before renewal season, answering questions about cover, claims and payments. The policy assistant used internally by agents is the starting point.

  1. Decision 1: risk tier and reviewDecision: medium risk, rising to high if it ever confirms coverage without review. It needs a full review board assessment. Why: it is public-facing, handles personal data, and an incorrect statement may be treated as the company's own commitment, as Air Canada found in 2024.
  2. Decision 2: release criteria, set before buildingDecision: at least 99% correct coverage answers; 100% with citations; under 2 points accuracy gap between languages; zero data disclosures in 5,000 attack tests; AI disclosure in every chat; switch-off in under 15 minutes. Why: the Responsible AI Lens recommends measurable release criteria across each responsible AI dimension.
  3. Decision 3: designDecision: RAG over approved documents only; Guardrails with content filters, denied topics (legal advice, payment promises), masking of personal data, contextual grounding checks and Automated Reasoning checks for deductible rules; identity verification before discussing a claim; written escalation criteria. Why: these are baking (core design), filtering (guardrails) and guiding (disclosure) in the Lens's terms.
  4. Decision 4: the evaluation resultResult: coverage answers are 97% correct, against a limit of 99%. Claim status and document upload are 99.6%. Marketing wants to launch anyway.
  5. Decision 5: the trade-offOptions: launch all; delay all; narrow scope. Decision: launch for claim status and document upload only; coverage questions hand over to agents. Why: wrong coverage answers create customer harm and possible commitments; narrowing the scope keeps most of the benefit within risk appetite. The decision and its reasoning are recorded.
  6. Decision 6: monitoringDecision: daily grounding-block rate, weekly human review of 200 chats, complaints tracking, each with a threshold, owner and response. Why: release criteria must hold in production, not just at launch.
  7. Decision 7: an incidentAfter a deductible change, the internal assistant quoted the old figure in 61 conversations because the old document wasn't removed. Response: contain, assess using the logs, correct the affected customers, and require same-day retirement of old documents with automatic retesting. Why: logs made it possible to identify every affected customer.

What this case tests:

  • Risk classification.
  • Governance by design.
  • Release criteria.
  • Guardrails and hallucination controls.
  • Transparency.
  • Resolving a trade-off by narrowing scope.
  • Production monitoring and incident response.

Case 3: AI in lending decisions

Banking · Domains 2, 3

Northbridge Bank, a regional bank operating in the EU, receives 8,000 personal loan applications a month. Underwriters take two days per decision. A vendor offers an AI credit-decision product that "approves or declines in seconds, with 20% fewer defaults".

  1. Decision 1: regulatory classificationFinding: evaluating creditworthiness of individuals is high-risk under the EU AI Act (Annex III), with obligations including risk management, data quality, logging, documentation, human oversight, robustness and accuracy. Those high-risk obligations apply from December 2027 under the amended timeline. Data protection law also gives people rights about decisions based solely on automated processing with significant effects. Implication: fully automated declines are not acceptable; the governance tier is high.
  2. Decision 2: evaluate the vendor proposalQuestions asked: tested on our applicants? Explanations for each decision? Fairness results by group? Is our data used to train the vendor's model? Documentation to support our obligations as deployer? Exit terms? Finding: the "20% fewer defaults" came from another bank's data; no per-decision explanations.
  3. Decision 3: build, buy or partnerDecision: build an explainable model on Amazon SageMaker AI with a specialist partner, on the bank's own ten years of loan outcomes. Why: credit decisions are core to how the bank competes, rely on proprietary data, and need explanations the bank can defend.
  4. Decision 4: fairnessFinding: the first model approves applicants from two postal areas at lower rates than their repayment records justify. Decision: remove postal area, add features that reflect actual repayment behavior, and accept a small loss of accuracy. Why: legal and ethical limits come first; the loss was quantified and recorded.
  5. Decision 5: human oversight designDecision: the model auto-approves clear approvals within set limits; every decline and every borderline case goes to an underwriter with the explanation. Underwriters' override rates are tracked. Why: oversight proportional to impact; tracking overrides guards against rubber-stamping.
  6. Decision 6: monitoringDecision: monthly approval rates and error rates by group (bias drift), quarterly accuracy against repayment outcomes, annual independent validation. Why: high tier; repayment outcomes arrive months later, so approval-rate gaps serve as an early warning.
  7. Contrast: fraud detectionThe bank's card fraud model falls under the Act's exclusion for AI used to detect financial fraud from the credit scoring category. It still needs data protection compliance, fairness checks and governance, but not the high-risk regime for credit scoring.

What this case tests:

  • EU AI Act tiers and their exceptions.
  • Automated decision rights.
  • Evaluating vendor claims.
  • Build for differentiation.
  • Fairness trade-offs.
  • Proportional human oversight.
  • Bias drift monitoring.

Case 4: Retail data foundations and agentic shoppers

Retail · Domains 2, 4

Meadow & Co, a home goods retailer with 200 stores and an online shop, wants AI demand forecasting to cut stock-outs and waste. It is also noticing that some customers now shop through AI assistants that compare products and buy on their behalf.

  1. Decision 1: data readinessFinding: store sales, online orders, promotions and supplier deliveries sit in four systems, each with its own product codes; one in six products can't be matched across systems. Decision: fix first, building a governed product and sales data product with a named owner, before forecasting work starts. Why: the forecast can't be better than its joined data.
  2. Decision 2: data sharingDecision: publish the data product through a catalog with approval workflows (Amazon DataZone) and fine-grained permissions (AWS Lake Formation); add weather data from AWS Data Exchange. Why: one governed source instead of each team copying data.
  3. Decision 3: competitive positionFinding: forecasting is common among competitors, so it brings parity. The new risk is invisibility to AI shopping assistants. AWS's retail strategists describe the shift as competing for "algorithmic inclusion", where structured product data, real-time availability and reliable fulfilment decide visibility. Decision: treat machine-readable product, price and availability data as a strategic priority.
  4. Decision 4: investment postureDecision: adopt (buy or use proven approaches) for forecasting; differentiate on product data quality and fulfilment reliability; a small staged bet on an agent-friendly ordering interface. Why: forecasting is mature in the industry; agentic commerce is early but could compound.
  5. Decision 5: measuring valueDecision: baseline stock-out rate and write-offs by store for a full year (seasonality); pilot in 20 stores against 20 similar stores. Why: retail is highly seasonal; a comparison group separates the forecast's effect from the weather and promotions.
  6. Decision 6: peopleDecision: store managers keep the ability to override orders, with overrides tracked and reviewed. Why: managers know local events the data doesn't; tracking overrides improves the model and builds trust.

What this case tests:

  • Data silos and data products.
  • Sharing frameworks.
  • Parity versus advantage.
  • New channels created by AI agents.
  • Investment posture by maturity.
  • Seasonal baselines.
  • Human-AI collaboration.

Case 5: From selling machines to selling uptime

Manufacturing · Domains 2, 3, 4

Kestrel Industrial makes packaging machines. Customers lose an average of 40 hours a year to unplanned breakdowns. Its machines already report sensor data. The CEO proposes using AI failure prediction to sell "guaranteed uptime" per month instead of machines.

  1. Decision 1: is this a business model change?Yes. The value proposition (outcome, not product), revenue model (subscription, not one-off sale), customer relationship (ongoing) and risk (Kestrel bears downtime) all change. Implication: higher potential value and higher risk than an efficiency project.
  2. Decision 2: sustainable advantageFinding: Kestrel has sensor data from 6,000 installed machines and 15 years of repair records that competitors don't. Each repair adds a new labeled example (a data flywheel). Implication: the advantage rests on proprietary data and the feedback loop, not on the model.
  3. Decision 3: data rightsFinding: contracts with existing customers don't clearly allow Kestrel to use their machine data to train models. Decision: new contracts grant clear rights, including sharing anonymized fleet insights back to customers; existing customers are asked to opt in. Why: IP and data rights must be settled at the training data stage.
  4. Decision 4: staged investmentDecision: three stages, each funded only if the last met its criteria. First, a prediction proof of concept on historical data. Second, a pilot with 10 volunteer customers on uptime contracts. Third, commercial launch. Why: unproven demand, new pricing and new liability make this a transformational bet.
  5. Decision 5: build or partnerDecision: partner for the first build, with knowledge transfer; build the long-term team in-house. Why: strategic and differentiating, but no in-house ML skills yet.
  6. Decision 6: workforceDecision: field technicians move from reactive repair to planned maintenance and customer advisory, with training; experienced technicians review the model's predictions and label outcomes. Why: their expertise improves the model, and their role shifts toward judgment and customer relationships.

What this case tests:

  • Business model transformation.
  • Data flywheels and sustainable advantage.
  • Training data rights.
  • Staged funding.
  • Partnering with knowledge transfer.
  • Role redesign toward oversight and advice.

Case 6: Clinical notes and a model retirement

Healthcare · Domains 1, 2, 3, 4

Riverside Health, a network of clinics, uses a generative AI assistant built on Amazon Bedrock to summarize patient histories before appointments. Clinicians spend 12 minutes per appointment reading records today. The assistant has been in production for a year when the model it uses enters its Legacy period, with end-of-life in six months.

  1. Earlier decision: oversight and KPIsThe clinician reviews every summary before use (human in the loop), because errors could affect care. KPIs: minutes spent reviewing records (tangible), with clinician burnout scores and patient experience as intangible measures. Guardrail metric: errors found in monthly audits of 100 summaries.
  2. Earlier decision: privacy and securityHealth data is highly sensitive. Bedrock doesn't use prompts or outputs to train models or share them with providers. Riverside adds its own encryption keys, private connectivity, least-privilege access so that the assistant only sees the patient whose record is open, and invocation logging with restricted access.
  3. Decision 1: respond to the Legacy noticeDecision: start migration planning immediately, not near end-of-life. Why: after end-of-life, requests fail and migration isn't automatic; a six-month window is enough only if work starts now.
  4. Decision 2: evaluate replacementsDecision: run the 400-case test set with clinician-approved summaries against two newer models using Amazon Bedrock Evaluations, plus clinician review of a sample. The leading model scores higher and costs less per request. Why: evidence before switching; the test set is the asset that makes migration safe.
  5. Decision 3: what carries overApplication code carries over, because it uses the Converse API. Prompts are retested and adjusted. The document index stays, because the embedding model isn't changing. The responsible AI review is refreshed for the new model.
  6. Decision 4: rolloutDecision: shadow mode for two weeks, one clinic for a month, then phased rollout, with rollback to the old model available until end-of-life. Rollback triggers: audit error rate above the current baseline, or clinician satisfaction falling. Why: continuity for a clinical workflow.
  7. Decision 5: protecting expertiseJunior clinicians still review full records for a share of appointments in their first year. Why: AWS's research flags the risk that AI absorbs the routine work through which junior staff build judgment.

What this case tests:

  • Proportional human oversight.
  • Tangible and intangible KPIs.
  • Data security under shared responsibility.
  • Model life cycle planning.
  • Evaluation before switching.
  • What carries over between models.
  • Phased rollout with rollback.
  • Workforce development.
