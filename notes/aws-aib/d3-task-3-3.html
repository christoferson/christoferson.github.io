Task 3.3: Identify enterprise AI risks and direct mitigation strategies

Managing the risks that appear once AI is in daily use: the controls and monitoring a production system needs, how bias enters at every stage and creeps back over time, how to keep harmful content out and intellectual property safe, and how to keep AI reliable when it invents facts, its data degrades or the world drifts away from its training.

Overview

Task 3.3

Identify enterprise AI risks and direct mitigation strategies.

Testing before launch reduces risk, but it can't remove it. Production brings inputs nobody tested, people deliberately trying to misuse the system, scale that turns rare errors into daily events, and changes in data, models and the world. This task is about directing the mitigation of those risks: knowing which controls are needed, who acts when something goes wrong, and how to tell early that it is going wrong. A leader doesn't configure the controls, but must make sure they exist, are owned and are working.

A useful way to think about any AI risk is defense in depth: several independent layers, so that when one fails another catches the problem. Examples use Harbor Mutual, a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees, 1.2 million policyholders and a 400-agent contact center, plus examples from other industries.

Risk controls and monitoring in production

Skill 3.3.1

A system that passed every test can still fail in production, for reasons testing can't fully reproduce:

  • Real inputs are messier: customers write in ways nobody anticipated.
  • Some users are adversarial: they try to trick the system.
  • Scale multiplies rare errors: a 0.1% error rate on 24,000 interactions a day is 24 errors every day.
  • Things change: documents are updated, data feeds change, model versions are replaced.

Production risk management therefore needs three kinds of control.

Preventive, detective and corrective controls

TypePurposeExamples for AI
PreventiveStop problems before they happenGuardrails on inputs and outputs; least-privilege access; limits on agent tools, steps and spend; validated data inputs; approved model versions only; human approval for high-impact actions
DetectiveNotice problems quicklyMonitoring of quality, fairness, safety and cost; logging and tracing; sampling audits by people; user feedback and complaints; alerts on thresholds
CorrectiveLimit damage and fix the causeA switch to turn the AI off and fall back to the manual process; rolling back to a previous version; incident response; retraining; correcting affected decisions and notifying customers

All three are needed. Preventive controls alone fail silently when something new gets through; detective controls alone find problems but can't stop them; corrective controls only work if someone notices.

What to monitor

AreaExample measures
QualityAccuracy against known outcomes; share of answers passing grounding checks; acceptance rate of AI suggestions
SafetyGuardrail interventions by category; prompt attack attempts; harmful content reports
FairnessOutcomes and error rates by customer group, region and language
SecurityUnusual access patterns; attempts to extract data; changes to prompts or configurations
OperationsResponse time, errors, availability, escalation rate to humans
CostSpend against budget; cost per transaction; runaway agent sessions
People and customersComplaints mentioning the AI; satisfaction scores; staff override rates

A monitoring plan turns these into specific commitments, each with a threshold, an owner and a defined response:

            missing documents

Metric:     Complaints that mention the assistant

Incident response

An AI incident is any event where an AI system causes, or nearly causes, harm: wrong decisions, harmful output, a data leak or a fairness failure. The response should be planned before it's needed:

  1. DetectFrom monitoring, staff, customers or the media.
  2. ContainStop further harm fast: switch off the feature, narrow its scope, or fall back to the manual process.
  3. AssessHow many people were affected, how badly and since when? Logs and traces make this possible.
  4. NotifyLegal decides on obligations to regulators and affected customers; leadership and the review board are informed.
  5. RemediateFix the root cause and correct affected decisions, for example by reprocessing wrongly delayed claims.
  6. LearnA blameless review: what failed, which control should have caught it, and what changes.

Worked example: a wrong deductible

On Monday Harbor Mutual updates its home policy deductibles. On Wednesday a weekly sample finds the customer assistant still quoting the old deductible in some answers.

  • Contain (Wednesday, within an hour): deductible questions are routed to agents.
  • Assess: logs show 340 conversations since Monday that mentioned deductibles; 61 quoted the old amount. The cause was that the old policy document had not been removed from the knowledge base.
  • Notify and remediate: legal decides that the 61 customers will receive a written correction, and any claim already settled is checked. The old document is removed.
  • Learn: document updates now require the old version to be retired the same day. A test set of deductible questions runs automatically after every document change.

Without logs, the company couldn't have identified the 61 customers. Without the weekly sample, it might have found out from complaints weeks later.

Controlling change

Many production incidents come from changes, not from the original design. Models, prompts, guardrail settings and document sets should all be versioned. Changes should be reviewed and tested against the standard test sets, released gradually (to a small share of traffic first), and easy to roll back. Changes made by providers, such as model updates or retirements, need the same attention as changes made in-house.

Production AI needs preventive, detective and corrective controls together, a monitoring plan with thresholds and named owners, a rehearsed incident response with the ability to switch off and fall back, and controlled, reversible changes.

"It passed testing, so it's safe" is wrong. So is monitoring that produces dashboards nobody owns or acts on. A metric without a threshold, an owner and a response is not a control.

Bias across the AI life cycle and bias drift

Skill 3.3.2

Bias isn't a single defect introduced at one point that can be fixed once. It can enter at every stage, from how the problem is framed to how people use the output, and it can appear or grow after launch even if the system was fair at release. AWS's guidance on fairness advises considering fairness at each stage of the ML life cycle: problem formation, dataset construction, algorithm selection, training, testing, deployment, and monitoring and feedback (Amazon SageMaker AI Developer Guide, Fairness, model explainability and bias detection).

Where bias enters

StageHow bias entersExampleMitigation
Problem framingThe target chosen doesn't measure what really mattersA health system predicts "future medical spending" as a stand-in for medical need; groups who historically had less access to care appear to need lessChallenge whether the target is a fair stand-in for the real goal
Data collectionSome groups are under-representedFew claims from a newly entered region; a voice system trained mostly on one accentCheck representation; collect more data; test per group
Historical dataPast decisions were themselves unfairPast hiring favored one group, so a hiring model learns to as wellDon't treat past decisions as ground truth without review
LabelingPeople labeling data apply inconsistent or biased judgmentsInvestigators in different branches label similar claims differentlyClear labeling guidelines; check agreement between labelers
Feature selectionFeatures act as stand-ins for protected characteristicsPostal code standing in for ethnicity or incomeReview features; test outcomes with and without them
Training and optimizationOptimizing overall accuracy can sacrifice minority groupsA model 95% accurate overall but 80% for a small groupInclude fairness measures in the release criteria
EvaluationOverall averages hide group differencesTesting only on the overall populationReport every key metric per group
DeploymentUsed on a population it wasn't built or tested forA model built in one country deployed in anotherRe-evaluate before every expansion
Use and feedbackHow people act on outputs, and what data that generates, reinforces patternsSee the feedback loop belowMonitor outcomes; design the feedback carefully

Generative AI adds its own forms: stereotypes in generated text or images, and differences in tone or helpfulness depending on a user's name, dialect or language. These are tested with paired prompts, identical requests that differ only in one characteristic:

Paired bias test (simplified) 
Prompt A: Draft a reply to a claim enquiry from Mr. James
          Walker, whose roof was damaged in a storm.
Prompt B: Draft a reply to a claim enquiry from Ms. Aisha
          Rahman, whose roof was damaged in a storm.

Compare across 500 such pairs:
  - tone and politeness scores
  - length and level of detail
  - whether next steps and timelines are the same
  - any assumptions about the customer

Bias drift

Bias drift is a change in a model's fairness after deployment. AWS lists it as one of the four types of monitoring for ML models in production, alongside data quality, model quality and feature attribution drift (Amazon SageMaker AI Developer Guide, Model Monitor). It happens because:

  • The population changes: new customer groups, regions or products arrive.
  • Behavior changes: in one group but not another, for example how customers report claims after a local change in repair practices.
  • Feedback loops form: the model's own decisions shape the data it later learns from.

Worked example: a feedback loop

  1. A small initial skewAt launch, the fraud model flags Region B slightly more often than its true fraud rate justifies.
  2. More investigation in Region BInvestigators examine more Region B claims, so they find more of Region B's fraud. Region A's undetected fraud stays undetected.
  3. The data now looks worse for Region BThe new training labels show more confirmed fraud in Region B, partly because it was looked at more.
  4. Retraining amplifies the skewThe next model flags Region B even more often. Each cycle widens the gap.
  5. The fixMonitor flag rates and wrongful flag rates per region over time. Investigate a random sample of unflagged claims in every region, so the labels aren't shaped only by the model's own choices. Review regional gaps before each retraining.

Monitoring bias drift

Calculate the fairness measures chosen at launch (for example, flag rates and wrongful flag rates per group) on a schedule, compare them with the launch baseline, and alert when a gap crosses an agreed threshold. AWS's current approach for ML models, since SageMaker Clarify and Model Monitor closed to new customers, calculates these metrics per segment with its open-source SageMaker AI monitoring solutions. The results are recorded in MLflow and shown in QuickSight dashboards, with alerts through Amazon SNS. As with accuracy, outcome-based fairness measures need the true results, which can arrive months later, so they are paired with earlier signals such as differences in flag rates.

Bias can enter at any life cycle stage (framing, data, labels, features, training, evaluation, deployment, feedback), so checks are needed at each one, and fairness must be monitored continuously after launch because populations, behavior and feedback loops change it.

"We tested for bias before launch, so the model is fair" is wrong. Fairness at release says nothing about fairness six months later.

Harmful content and intellectual property

Skill 3.3.3

Harmful content

Generative AI can produce content that harms people or the organization. Sometimes users deliberately provoke it; sometimes the model produces it unprompted; sometimes it comes from retrieved or uploaded material:

TypeExample
Hate, harassment and insultsAn abusive reply to an angry customer
Sexual or violent contentInappropriate generated images in a marketing tool
Dangerous or illegal guidanceInstructions that facilitate fraud or self-harm
MisinformationConfident false statements presented as fact
Privacy violationsRevealing personal data from retrieved documents
Off-brand or inappropriate adviceLegal or investment advice from an insurance assistant; mentioning competitors

Mitigation uses several layers:

  • Model choice: providers build in their own safety measures. AWS AI Service Cards and model documentation describe them.
  • Amazon Bedrock Guardrails: content filters detect hate, insults, sexual content, violence, misconduct and prompt attacks (attempts to bypass safety controls, also called jailbreaks, or to override the developer's instructions), at configurable strengths. They work on both text and images, in user inputs and model outputs. Denied topics, word filters and sensitive information filters add business-specific limits.
  • Red-teaming before launch: people deliberately try to make the system misbehave, and the results feed into release criteria.
  • Monitoring after launch: guardrail intervention rates, user reports, sampled reviews.
  • Clear user policies: acceptable use terms and a way to report problems.
  • Human review before high-visibility content, such as advertising, is published.

Filter strength is a trade-off. Settings that are too strict block legitimate conversations, for example a customer describing a violent break-in for a theft claim. Settings that are too loose let harmful content through. Strengths are tuned with real examples and reviewed regularly.

Intellectual property risks

IP risk with AI arises at four points:

Point of riskWhat can go wrongMitigation
What goes inConfidential information or trade secrets entered into tools whose terms allow the provider to keep or use themApproved tools only, with contractual data protection; policy on what data may be used
What it's trained onFine-tuning or training on material you have no right to use for that purpose, such as licensed content, scraped websites or customers' data beyond its permitted purposeCheck rights and licences for every training data set; record data provenance
What comes outOutput that closely resembles copyrighted text, images or code, or that copies a living artist's distinctive style or a trademarkPrompting policies (no requests imitating named artists or brands); human review; similarity checks for important uses; licence checks on generated code
Who owns itIn some jurisdictions, content without enough human creative input may not be protected by copyright, so competitors could reuse itKeep meaningful human creative contribution in content that must be protected; check contract terms on output ownership

Provider protections matter in the build-buy decision:

  • Indemnity: AWS offers uncapped IP indemnity for outputs of generally available Amazon Nova models, protecting customers from third-party claims that those outputs infringe or misappropriate IP, including copyright. AWS's standard IP indemnity also covers claims that its services, and the data used to train its models, infringe IP (Amazon Nova User Guide, Responsible use; AWS Service Terms).
  • Provenance: Amazon Nova Canvas adds an invisible watermark to every generated image. It also includes content credentials, so public tools such as Content Credentials Verify can confirm an image was AI-generated, unless the metadata has been removed (Amazon Nova User Guide, Generating images with Amazon Nova Canvas).

Watermarking and provenance help with transparency obligations, such as labeling AI-generated content, and with tracing content if it's misused.

Worked example: an AI-generated marketing campaign

Harbor Mutual's marketing team wants AI-generated images for a home insurance campaign. Before approval, the review covers:

  1. Tool and termsUse an approved model with clear output terms and IP indemnity, not a consumer app.
  2. Prompt rulesNo requests for real people, other brands' logos, or the style of named living artists.
  3. SafetyGuardrail image filters on outputs; a designer reviews every image.
  4. Accuracy and fairnessImages must not misrepresent cover (for example, showing flood damage for a policy that excludes flood), and should represent customers diversely without stereotypes.
  5. Disclosure and provenanceKeep the watermarks and content credentials; follow labeling rules where they apply.
  6. RecordsKeep the prompts, model version and approvals with each asset.

Harmful content is controlled in layers: model choice, guardrails on inputs and outputs, red-teaming, monitoring and human review. IP risk is managed at the input (approved tools), training data (rights), output (review, policies, indemnity, provenance) and ownership (human contribution, contract terms) stages.

Assuming AI-generated content is automatically free to use and owned by you is wrong. So is relying on one filter as the only protection against harmful output.

Reliability risks

Skill 3.3.4

An unreliable AI system is worse than none in one important way: people come to depend on it. Three reliability risks are named in the exam guide, and each has different causes and remedies.

Hallucinations

A hallucination is fluent, confident output that is false: an invented policy clause, a wrong number, a citation to a document that doesn't exist. It happens because a generative model produces the most plausible response, not a verified one. It is most likely when the model lacks the information, the question is ambiguous, or the prompt pressures it to always answer.

Defense in depth for Harbor Mutual's policy assistant:

  1. Supply the factsRAG retrieves the current policy clauses, so the model isn't relying on memory.
  2. Instruct carefullyAnswer only from the extracts; say so when they don't contain the answer; never guess figures.
  3. Check groundingAmazon Bedrock Guardrails contextual grounding checks block answers that aren't supported by the retrieved text or don't address the question.
  4. Check logicAutomated Reasoning checks validate answers against rules such as deductible and eligibility conditions.
  5. Show sourcesEvery answer cites its clauses, so agents can verify.
  6. Keep people on the important decisionsCoverage decisions remain with staff.
  7. Measure continuouslyWeekly sampled reviews and test-set runs after every change.

No single layer is perfect; together they make an undetected hallucination reaching a customer rare.

Data quality degradation

AI systems depend on data pipelines that can break or change without anyone noticing. A model fed bad data produces bad output with the usual confidence.

CauseExample
Upstream system changeA claims system update starts recording amounts in cents, so the fraud model sees every claim as 100 times larger and flags almost everything
Broken feedA data feed fails and a field arrives empty; the model treats missing values as zero
Stale contentKnowledge base documents aren't updated when policies change
Gradual decayAddress data becomes less accurate as fewer customers update their details
Process changeA branch stops recording investigation outcomes

The defense is automated validation of incoming data against agreed expectations, sometimes called a data contract, with alerts and a safe response when checks fail:

Input data checks: fraud model 
claim_amount:      present; between 50 and 500,000;
                   daily average within 30% of 90-day average
policy_start_date: present; not after loss date
loss_date:         not in the future
claim_type:        one of the 14 approved codes
On failure:        hold scoring, route claims to manual
                   triage, alert data engineering

With these checks, the cents error would have been caught on its first day: the daily average would have jumped about 100 times.

Model drift

Even with clean data, the world moves away from what the model learned. Inputs change (data drift), the meaning of patterns changes (concept drift), and fairness can shift (bias drift). The remedy is continuous monitoring against a baseline, with retraining or other fixes when thresholds are crossed. For generative AI, the equivalents are changes in user questions, outdated documents and model version changes. On Amazon Bedrock, models move from Active to Legacy to End-of-Life, so planned, tested upgrades are part of reliability.

Other reliability risks

  • Availability and capacity: a provider outage or usage quota can stop an AI-dependent process. Mitigations include a fallback to the manual process, a fallback model, and Amazon Bedrock cross-Region inference, which spreads requests across AWS Regions within a geography to improve availability.
  • Compounding errors in agents: small error rates multiply across many steps. Mitigations include checks between steps, limits on steps, and human approval for consequential actions.
  • Over-reliance: staff stop checking outputs over time. Mitigations include sampling audits, override-rate tracking and training.
RiskEarly warning signMain mitigations
HallucinationRising grounding-check blocks; errors in sampled answersRAG, careful instructions, grounding and Automated Reasoning checks, citations, human review
Data quality degradationFailed validation checks; sudden shifts in input averagesData contracts, validation, document ownership, safe fallback
Model driftInput distribution shifts; falling accuracy against outcomesMonitoring against baseline, retraining, planned model upgrades
AvailabilityErrors, throttling, rising response timesFallback process, fallback model, cross-Region inference

Hallucinations are reduced by grounding (RAG, grounding checks, citations) and caught by checks and human review. Data degradation is caught by validating inputs. Drift is caught by monitoring against a baseline. All need a safe fallback when the AI can't be trusted.

Choosing a "more powerful model" doesn't fix hallucinations caused by missing information, bad input data or drift. The fixes are grounding, data validation and monitoring.

Key takeaways

  • Production AI needs preventive controls (guardrails, access limits, agent limits, data validation, approvals), detective controls (monitoring with thresholds and owners, logging, sampling, feedback) and corrective controls (switch-off and fallback, rollback, incident response, remediation). Changes to models, prompts and documents are versioned, tested, released gradually and reversible.
  • Incident response: detect, contain, assess, notify, remediate, learn. Logs make it possible to identify who was affected.
  • Bias can enter at problem framing, data collection, historical data, labeling, features, training, evaluation, deployment and feedback. Test per group (and with paired prompts for generative AI), and monitor bias drift continuously, watching for feedback loops.
  • Control harmful content with model choice, Amazon Bedrock Guardrails for text and images, red-teaming, monitoring and human review, tuning filter strength with real examples.
  • Manage IP risk at input, training data, output and ownership. AWS offers uncapped IP indemnity for generally available Amazon Nova outputs, and Nova Canvas adds invisible watermarks and content credentials.
  • Reliability: reduce hallucinations with grounding, grounding and Automated Reasoning checks, citations and human review; prevent data degradation with input validation and data contracts; manage drift with monitoring and planned model upgrades; plan for availability with fallbacks and cross-Region inference.
