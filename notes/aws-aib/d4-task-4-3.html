Task 4.3: Lead enterprise-wide change and build AI-ready workforce capabilities

The people side of AI, which decides whether technology is actually used: sponsors and champions who sustain momentum, teams that combine every needed discipline with clear accountability, honest communication about what AI means for jobs, leadership that removes cultural barriers, learning programs that build AI literacy at scale, and redesigning roles so people and AI each do what they do best.

Overview

Task 4.3

Lead enterprise-wide change and build AI-ready workforce capabilities.

AI changes how people work, and people decide whether that change succeeds. A tool that works perfectly but that staff distrust, avoid or work around delivers nothing. The AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (CAF) says leaders must put as much focus on the people side of change as on the technology, and warns that without an effective blend of technical and business leadership a transformation may slow down or stall.

The CAF's People perspective names seven capabilities that this task draws on (AWS whitepaper, An Overview of the AWS Cloud Adoption Framework):

  • culture evolution;
  • transformational leadership;
  • fluency (for AI, AI literacy);
  • workforce transformation;
  • change acceleration;
  • organization design;
  • organizational alignment.

The running example is a national energy supplier (a company that sells gas and electricity to households) rolling out AI-drafted after-call notes and a knowledge assistant to its 600 contact-center agents, and AI support for its billing specialists, who resolve disputed bills. Its customers include people struggling to pay, so how it treats staff and customers through the change matters as much as the technology.

Executive sponsorship, leadership alignment and AI champions

Skill 4.3.1Establish executive sponsorship and leadership alignment, and empower AI champions to sustain momentum

Executive sponsor

A senior leader who owns the AI program's success: they set its direction, secure its funding and remove obstacles, and are visibly and personally involved throughout, not only at the launch.

AI champion

A respected employee inside a team who adopts AI early, helps colleagues use it, and carries feedback and ideas back to the people running the program.

Enterprise-wide change needs authority from the top and energy throughout the organization. The executive sponsor provides the first; a network of AI champions provides the second. The CAF calls for active and visible executive sponsorship from both technology and business functions: leaders who make critical decisions on strategy, vision, scope and resources, and who communicate, build coalitions and hold teams accountable for results.

What an executive sponsor does

Set direction

In practiceLinks AI to the business strategy and states the outcomes that matter.

Secure resources

In practiceMulti-year funding, people's time, priority over competing projects.

Remove obstacles

In practiceResolves conflicts between departments; fixes slow approvals and policies that block progress.

Model the behavior

In practiceUses AI tools visibly; talks openly about what was learned from setbacks.

Hold people accountable

In practiceReviews progress against outcomes regularly; asks who owns turning time saved into results.

Communicate

In practiceExplains why, repeatedly, and listens to concerns.

A sponsor who announces an AI program and then disappears is a common reason programs stall. Visible, sustained involvement is what signals that the change is real.

Leadership alignment

One committed sponsor isn't enough if other leaders pull in different directions. At the energy supplier, the Head of Billing sees AI as a way to resolve disputed bills faster. The Head of Customer Service wants to cut costs. The Chief Risk Officer worries about the energy regulator's rules on treating customers in financial difficulty fairly. Without alignment, each blocks the others' priorities. Ways to align leaders:

  • Agree on a small set of shared AI objectives and measures in the leadership team.
  • Give leaders joint goals, so success depends on cooperation.
  • Involve every affected leader in the steering committee.
  • Make sure each layer of management, especially middle managers, delivers clear and consistent messages. The CAF stresses that business and technology leaders should co-develop, co-lead and co-deliver culture change.

Middle managers matter most for adoption. They translate strategy into daily work, and their teams follow what they actually do, not what the executive said.

AI champions

Champions use AI early, help colleagues, collect feedback and spot new use cases. They spread change faster than any central team because colleagues trust them, and they bring problems back that leaders would otherwise never hear about.

Who

Champion programOne volunteer per contact-center team of 15 to 20 agents (about 35 champions), plus volunteers in billing and metering; chosen for peer respect, not seniority or technical skill.

Time

Champion programFour hours a week released from call targets, agreed with their managers.

Preparation

Champion programEarly access to tools, deeper training including responsible use, a direct line to the delivery team.

Role

Champion programCoach colleagues; run short team sessions; collect issues and ideas; test new features before release.

Recognition

Champion programNamed in leadership updates; counted in performance reviews; first consideration for new AI-related roles.

Feedback loop

Champion programMonthly session with the delivery team and the sponsor; issues raised get a visible response.

"Empower" is the key word. Champions without time, access or recognition become unpaid help desks and lose enthusiasm within months.

Sustaining momentum

  • A regular rhythm: monthly showcases of what teams have built, and quarterly results against the objectives.
  • Celebrating early wins publicly, with real numbers.
  • A visible pipeline of next use cases, many suggested by staff.
  • Re-confirming the sponsor's commitment when leadership changes.

Sustained change combines an active, visible executive sponsor, aligned leaders with shared goals (including middle managers), and empowered champions who have time, access, training and recognition.

Delegating AI transformation entirely to IT, or relying on a launch announcement without ongoing sponsor involvement, are common wrong answers.

Cross-functional teams and clear accountability

Skill 4.3.2Build cross-functional teams (business, technical, legal, compliance) with clear accountability

An AI initiative needs business knowledge, technical skill, legal and compliance judgment, and an understanding of the people who will use it. When these sit in separate departments that hand work to each other, problems are discovered late: legal finds an issue a week before launch; users reject a tool designed without them. A cross-functional team brings the disciplines together from the start. The CAF recommends cross-functional teams to drive ownership and autonomy, enable rapid decisions and minimize the need for excessive approvals.

Who's on the team

The team for the energy supplier's knowledge assistant:

Business lead (product owner)

ContributionOwns the outcome and priorities; decides trade-offs.

InvolvementFull-time.

Domain experts and end users

ContributionExperienced agents and a tariff expert who know the real questions and what a good answer is.

InvolvementPart-time, every week.

Technical experts

ContributionEngineers, a data engineer and an ML specialist who design and build it.

InvolvementFull-time.

Legal and compliance

ContributionRegulatory requirements, disclosures, record-keeping.

InvolvementRegular reviews from the start.

Privacy and security

ContributionData use, access control, threat review.

InvolvementDesign reviews and before release.

Risk

ContributionRisk classification, release criteria, monitoring.

InvolvementAt each stage gate.

Change and training lead

ContributionCommunication, training, adoption tracking.

InvolvementFrom the pilot onward.

Finance partner

ContributionBusiness case, cost tracking.

InvolvementMonthly.

Accountability standards

  • One accountable owner: a single business leader answers for the initiative's results. Amazon calls a leader dedicated to one initiative a "single-threaded" leader: someone not split across many competing priorities.
  • Clear decision rights: who decides scope, release, trade-offs and spending, written down (for example, in a RACI matrix).
  • Measures owned by name: each KPI has an owner, including the one who turns time saved into business results.
  • A shared definition of done: "done" includes the release criteria for quality, fairness and safety, training delivered and monitoring live, not just "the code works".
  • Regular, visible reviews: progress against outcomes, not just activity.

Teams also need to stay small enough to move fast. AWS's research notes that small groups can now do with AI what once took a department, but that coordination between teams, approvals and decisions increasingly dominates delivery time (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Announcing the new AWS Reimagine report on AI, September 2026). Including legal and risk inside the team, rather than as a queue at the end, is one of the most effective ways to reduce that delay.

Effective AI teams include business, domain experts and end users, technical experts, legal, compliance, privacy, security and risk from the start, with one accountable business owner, written decision rights and owned measures.

A purely technical team that "hands over" the finished system to the business, or involves legal only at the end, is the classic pattern behind AI that never gets adopted or gets blocked before launch.

Transparent communication about AI

Skill 4.3.3Create transparent communication that addresses workforce concerns: timelines, expected outcomes, effects on roles

When people hear "AI" at work, they ask the same few questions. If leaders don't answer them, rumors will, and rumors are usually worse than the truth. Transparent communication addresses concerns directly, early and honestly, including the uncomfortable parts.

The questions staff are asking

"Will I lose my job?"

What a good answer coversAn honest statement of the expected effect on roles and headcount, and what commitments the company is making.

"What changes for me, and when?"

What a good answer coversThe implementation timeline, which teams first, what training and when.

"What's this supposed to achieve?"

What a good answer coversThe outcome expected, in realistic terms, including what the AI won't do.

"Will it be used to monitor me?"

What a good answer coversWhat data is collected about staff use, and how it will and won't be used.

"Can I trust it? What if it's wrong?"

What a good answer coversIts limits, who is accountable for decisions, how to report problems.

"Will I get help?"

What a good answer coversTraining, champions, support channels.

Principles

  • Early: before the pilot starts, not when the tool appears on screens.
  • Honest and specific: don't promise "no changes" if there will be changes; don't overstate what the AI can do.
  • Consistent through every layer: executives, middle managers and champions give the same message.
  • Two-way: forums, surveys and champions collect questions, and answers are published.
  • Repeated: people need to hear important messages several times, through several channels.
  • Delivered by the right person: strategy from the sponsor; "what this means for your day" from the employee's own manager.

Worked example: announcing after-call summaries

Message from the Head of Contact Center Operations
Starting 3 March, 40 agents in two teams will pilot a tool that drafts your after-call notes. You review and edit every draft before saving; nothing is saved without you. Why: wrap-up takes about 6 minutes per call today. We expect this to save around a minute per call, time we want you to spend on customers who need more help. Your job: no agent will lose their job because of this tool. As call volumes grow, we expect to hire fewer new agents than we otherwise would. Monitoring: we will measure how the tool performs, not rate individual agents on how often they edit drafts. Next steps: two-hour training before the pilot; your team champion is your first contact; Q&A sessions every Thursday. Results will be shared with everyone in May, before any decision to expand.

Every concern listed above is addressed:

  • the human stays in control;
  • the expected outcome is realistic;
  • the job commitment is honest, with no promise of no change at all;
  • monitoring is explained;
  • support and the timeline are clear;
  • results will be shared before any expansion.

A commitment like the one on jobs must be one leadership can keep. A broken promise destroys trust for every later change.

A communication plan

All staff

MessageWhy AI, the principles, what it means for jobs overall.

Channel and messengerCEO town hall, intranet FAQ.

WhenProgram start, then quarterly.

Contact-center agents

MessageWhat changes, when, training, how to give feedback.

Channel and messengerTeam meetings led by their own managers; champions.

WhenFour weeks before the pilot, then weekly.

Middle managers

MessageTheir role in the change; answers to hard questions.

Channel and messengerBriefing session with the sponsor.

WhenBefore the staff announcement.

Works council or unions, where applicable

MessagePlans, effects on roles, consultation.

Channel and messengerFormal consultation led by HR.

WhenBefore decisions are final.

Good AI communication is early, honest, specific, consistent through every management layer, and two-way, and it covers timelines, realistic outcomes and the effect on roles directly.

Avoiding the job question, or over-promising ("AI will never change your job"), damages trust. Announcing AI only after it's built leaves people feeling it was done to them.

Cultural barriers and leadership interventions

Skill 4.3.4Recognize cultural barriers (risk aversion, resistance to change, fear of failure) and the leadership interventions that address them

Culture, meaning the behaviors an organization actually rewards and punishes, often decides AI adoption more than strategy does. The CAF advises evolving culture deliberately: using the organization's heritage and values while building new behaviors, encouraging rapid experimentation and cross-functional teams, and minimizing the need for excessive approvals or bureaucracy.

Risk aversion

How it shows up"What if it goes wrong?" stops every proposal; approvals take months.

Leadership interventionRisk-tiered governance so low-risk uses move fast; sandboxes with approved tools and test data; small, reversible pilots.

Resistance to change

How it shows up"We've always done it this way"; workarounds; quiet non-use.

Leadership interventionInvolve users in design; show what's in it for them; champions; leaders using the tools themselves.

Fear of failure

How it shows upNobody proposes ideas; pilots are never stopped because stopping looks like failure.

Leadership interventionCelebrate what was learned from stopped pilots; blameless reviews; judge people on learning, not only success.

Fear of job loss

How it shows upStaff withhold knowledge the AI needs; low adoption.

Leadership interventionHonest commitments on jobs; reskilling paths; visible redeployment into new roles.

Distrust from a past failure

How it shows up"The 2022 chatbot proved AI doesn't work here."

Leadership interventionAcknowledge what went wrong and what is different now; start with a use case people can see working.

Siloed incentives

How it shows upDepartments refuse to share data or staff because they're measured only on their own targets.

Leadership interventionShared goals across departments; sponsor resolves conflicts.

Perfectionism

How it shows upWaiting for 100% accuracy before any use.

Leadership interventionCompare against today's human error rate; human review for the remaining risk.

Worked example: the failed chatbot's legacy

In 2022, the energy supplier launched a customer chatbot that gave confusing answers and was withdrawn after two months. Staff still cite it. The sponsor addresses it head-on:

  1. Acknowledge itIn the town hall: "The 2022 chatbot failed. It was launched to customers without being tested on real questions, and agents weren't involved."
  2. Show what's differentThis time the first tool is for agents, not customers. Agents help design it. It cites its sources. Nothing reaches customers without an agent's review.
  3. Make progress visiblePilot results, good and bad, are shared openly with all agents.
  4. Reward participationAgents whose feedback improved the tool are thanked by name.

Leaders' behavior is the strongest intervention. If an executive punishes a team for a pilot that was stopped for good reasons, every other team learns not to try. If executives use AI tools themselves and talk about their own learning, staff see that experimenting is safe.

Address cultural barriers with leadership behavior (modeling use, rewarding learning, blameless reviews), safe spaces to experiment (sandboxes, small pilots, risk-tiered approvals), user involvement, honest job commitments and shared incentives.

Mandating AI use without addressing fear, trust or incentives produces compliance on paper and workarounds in practice.

Workforce development and AI literacy

Skill 4.3.5Choose workforce development approaches that accelerate AI literacy: POC programs, hackathons, training, responsible AI training

AI literacy is the ability to understand what AI can and can't do, use it effectively and responsibly, and recognize its risks. Everyone who uses, buys, manages or oversees AI needs some of it, at different depths. The CAF recommends assessing existing skills to build a targeted training strategy; combining virtual, classroom, hands-on and just-in-time learning; and adding mentoring, communities of practice and formal certifications.

Development approaches

Structured training

What it isCourses and learning paths by role, such as those in AWS Skill Builder.

Best forBuilding a common foundation at scale.

Responsible AI training

What it isData handling rules, approved tools, recognizing hallucinations and bias, when to escalate.

Best forEveryone who uses AI; deeper versions for builders and approvers.

Hands-on learning

What it isLearning by doing, for example with PartyRock, AWS's no-code tool for building generative AI apps that teaches concepts such as prompt engineering.

Best forTurning concepts into confidence.

Hackathons

What it isShort, intense events where mixed teams build prototypes for real business problems.

Best forGenerating ideas, energy and cross-department connections.

Proof-of-concept (POC) programs

What it isSmall funded projects that test an idea on real data over a few weeks.

Best forMoving the best ideas toward production; learning by delivering.

Communities of practice

What it isOngoing groups that share techniques and lessons.

Best forSustaining learning after training ends.

Mentoring and rotations

What it isPairing with experts; time on AI teams.

Best forGrowing specialists and future leaders.

Certifications

What it isFormal validation of skills.

Best forSpecialists and roles that need demonstrated competence.

A tiered learning plan

All employees

GoalUse approved AI tools safely and well.

ContentWhat AI is, approved tools, data rules, spotting errors, how to escalate.

FormatTwo-hour online course plus team session; refreshed yearly.

Managers

GoalLead teams through change.

ContentAbove, plus redesigning work, supporting staff, judging use cases.

FormatHalf-day workshop.

Executives and board

GoalSet strategy and oversee risk.

ContentStrategy, value, governance, regulation, responsible AI.

FormatBriefings with outside experts; hands-on session.

Power users and champions

GoalGet more from AI and help others.

ContentPrompting techniques, building simple workflows, coaching skills.

FormatHands-on labs; community of practice.

Builders

GoalBuild and run AI systems.

ContentGenerative AI and ML engineering, evaluation, security, responsible AI in depth.

FormatTechnical courses, certifications, partner-led projects with knowledge transfer.

Designing a hackathon that leads somewhere

  1. Start from business problemsBusiness leaders submit real problem statements, such as "field engineers wait two days for answers about older meter models".
  2. Mix the teamsEach team has business staff, technical staff and someone from risk or compliance.
  3. Provide a safe sandboxApproved tools, synthetic or anonymized data, guardrails in place.
  4. Judge on business criteriaValue, feasibility and responsible AI, not just technical flair.
  5. Follow throughThe top two ideas get a funded six-week POC with a business sponsor. Without this step, hackathons build excitement and then disappointment.

Measuring workforce development

Course completion is the starting point, not the goal. Better measures:

  • assessed proficiency;
  • active use of approved tools;
  • a falling share of shadow AI use;
  • the number and quality of use-case ideas coming from staff;
  • how many internal people can lead AI projects without outside help.

Build literacy at scale with tiered, role-based training (including responsible AI for everyone), reinforced by hands-on learning, hackathons and POC programs that connect to real business problems and have a path to funding.

One generic AI course for everyone, or training only the technical team, leaves most of the workforce unprepared to use or oversee AI responsibly.

Shifting human roles toward oversight and collaboration

Skill 4.3.6Shift human roles from manual work to oversight and collaboration with AI, balancing human strengths with AI capabilities

AI changes jobs one task at a time, rarely by replacing whole jobs at once. The leadership skill is to look at roles as bundles of tasks, decide which tasks AI should do, which it should support and which should stay human, and redesign roles so that people move from manual processing toward judgment, relationships, oversight and improvement.

Human and AI strengths

Humans are stronger atAI is stronger at
Critical thinking and judgment in new or ambiguous situationsProcessing large volumes quickly and consistently
Empathy, trust and handling emotional situationsFinding patterns across millions of records
Creativity and framing the right problemDrafting, summarizing and searching text
Ethical reasoning and accountabilityWorking around the clock without fatigue
Understanding context outside the dataApplying the same criteria every time

Breaking a role into tasks

The energy supplier's billing specialists, who resolve disputed bills, before and after AI support, as a share of their working time:

TaskBeforeAfterAI's role
Reading account history and retyping meter readings35%10%Automate: extraction and summaries, with the specialist checking
Checking tariffs and billing rules20%15%Augment: the relevant rules retrieved and cited; the specialist decides
Talking with customers15%30%Human: more time for distressed customers and those struggling to pay
Agreeing payment plans15%15%Augment: AI suggests an affordable plan as a starting point
Deciding complex and disputed cases10%20%Human: judgment and accountability
Reviewing AI output and reporting errors0%5%Human oversight
Administration5%5%Mixed

The billing specialist's job doesn't disappear. It shifts toward the work only people can do well: customer conversations, complex decisions and oversight.

New and changed roles

  • AI reviewers and quality leads: sample and audit AI output; former senior agents and specialists are well suited.
  • Content owners: keep the documents that AI answers from accurate and current.
  • AI operations: monitor systems, handle alerts and incidents.
  • Data stewards: maintain the quality of business data.
  • Agent and workflow designers: people who know the business process and shape how AI fits into it.

Reskilling paths from existing roles into these are often faster and better received than hiring from outside, because the people already know the business.

Decisions that keep the balance right

  • Keep people where empathy and accountability matter: a bereavement, a customer struggling to pay, or a decision about a disputed debt should be handled by a person, even if AI could draft the response.
  • Make oversight meaningful: reviewers need time, evidence and authority to disagree with the AI; otherwise oversight becomes a rubber stamp.
  • Protect the path to expertise: AWS's research raises a problem it says nobody it interviewed had solved. If AI absorbs the routine work through which junior staff build judgment, it is unclear how they develop expertise (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Announcing the new AWS Reimagine report on AI). The energy supplier's response is deliberate: new billing specialists resolve a share of simpler disputes fully by hand during their first year, review the AI's reasoning as a learning tool, and spend time with the metering team.
  • Assign the freed time: time saved should go somewhere intended, such as more customer contact or quality work, with a named owner; otherwise it is simply absorbed.

Redesign roles task by task: automate high-volume routine tasks, augment judgment tasks, and keep empathy, complex judgment, creativity and accountability with people. Create oversight and quality roles, offer reskilling paths, and protect how junior staff build expertise.

"Automate the whole role" and "change nothing" are both wrong. So is putting a human "in the loop" without the time, information or authority to actually exercise judgment.

Key takeaways

  • The AWS CAF People perspective covers culture evolution, transformational leadership, fluency, workforce transformation, change acceleration, organization design and organizational alignment. Leaders must focus on people as much as technology.
  • Sustain change with an active, visible executive sponsor from both business and technology; aligned leaders with shared goals, especially middle managers; and empowered champions with time, access, training and recognition.
  • Build cross-functional teams (business owner, domain experts and users, technical experts, legal, compliance, privacy, security, risk, change) from the start, with one accountable owner, decision rights, owned measures and a definition of done that includes responsible AI.
  • Communicate early, honestly, specifically and consistently through every management layer, two-way, covering jobs, timelines, realistic outcomes, monitoring and support. Make only commitments that can be kept.
  • Address risk aversion, resistance, fear of failure, fear of job loss, past failures, siloed incentives and perfectionism through leadership behavior, safe experimentation, user involvement, honest commitments and shared goals.
  • Develop AI literacy with tiered, role-based training (responsible AI for everyone), hands-on learning, hackathons and POC programs linked to real problems with a path to funding; measure proficiency and use, not just completion.
  • Redesign roles task by task: AI handles volume and routine, people keep empathy, judgment, creativity and accountability and take on oversight. Create new roles, offer reskilling, and protect the path to expertise for junior staff.
