Task 1.2: Identify and select appropriate AI solution types

Choosing the right kind of solution for a problem: when fixed rules beat AI, what makes an AI agent different from a model or a chatbot, why every AI solution needs monitoring after launch, and how a clear list of approved, blocked and under-evaluation tools keeps employees from using AI the organization can't see.

Overview

Task 1.2

Identify and select appropriate AI solution types.

Questions in this task describe a business problem and ask which type of solution fits, or what the organization must do to keep a solution trustworthy after it goes live. The right answer is often the simplest option that meets the need. Examples use Harbor Mutual, a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees, 1.2 million policyholders and a 400-agent contact center.

Rule-based automation or AI

Skill 1.2.1

Rule-based automation follows logic that people write in advance: if the driver is under 18, decline; if the claim is under $500 and the policy is active, pay it automatically. It does the same thing every time, and anyone can read why it decided. AI learns patterns from data (or, for generative AI, from very broad training) and returns its best judgment, usually with a confidence level. It copes with variety that no one could write rules for, but it is probabilistic: it is sometimes wrong, and its reasoning is harder to explain.

ConsiderationFavors rule-based automationFavors AI
LogicCan be written down completely and stays stableToo many factors or interactions to write down, or the pattern isn't known in advance
InputsStructured fields with known valuesFree text, documents, images, speech, or inputs that vary widely
ConsistencySame input must always give the same output (regulated pricing, eligibility, compliance checks)An occasional wrong answer is acceptable and can be caught by review
ExplainabilityEvery decision must be traced to a written ruleA confidence score and supporting evidence are enough
ChangeChanges come from policy decisions and are rareThe patterns shift often (new fraud schemes, new customer language) and rules would always lag
DataLittle or no historical data is neededEnough good-quality examples exist, or a foundation model already understands the content
CostCheap to run and maintain while the rule count stays smallWorth the extra cost of data, monitoring and oversight because the value is higher

At Harbor Mutual the same claims process uses both:

StepSolutionWhy
Check that the policy was active on the loss dateRuleA yes/no fact from the policy table; must be exact and auditable
Pay small glass claims automaticallyRuleClear thresholds set by the business; easy to explain to regulators
Read the customer's description and repair estimate PDFAI (document extraction and generative AI)Unstructured, varied inputs that rules can't parse
Score the claim for fraud riskAI (ML model)Hundreds of interacting signals, and fraud patterns keep changing
Decide whether to deny a claimA person, supported by AIHigh impact on the customer; regulators expect accountable human decisions

A practical way to decide, step by step:

  1. Can the logic be written as clear rules?If yes, and it rarely changes, use rules. This is also the right choice when the outcome must be identical every time.
  2. Are the inputs unstructured or highly varied?Text, documents, images and speech usually need AI to interpret them.
  3. Is the pattern learned from data?If the answer depends on many factors and good historical examples exist, an ML model fits.
  4. Is the task to create or converse?Drafting, summarizing and answering in natural language point to generative AI.
  5. What does a wrong answer cost?The higher the cost, the more you need human review, rule-based guardrails around the AI, or no AI at all.

Many good answers are hybrid: AI handles the judgment on messy inputs, and rules set hard limits around it (for example, AI suggests a payout amount, but a rule stops any payout above $10,000 without approval).

Don't pick AI just because it's available. If a scenario describes a stable, fully defined policy with structured inputs and a strict need for consistency, the better answer is rule-based automation: it's cheaper, predictable and easier to audit.

AI agents and their core capabilities

Skill 1.2.2

AWS defines an AI agent as a software program that can interact with its environment, collect data and use that data to perform self-directed tasks toward goals that people set. Humans set the goal; the agent chooses the actions to reach it (AWS, What are AI agents?). That is the difference from other AI solutions: a model answers one question, a chatbot replies to one message, and an agent works through a multi-step task and takes actions in other systems.

Solution typeWhat it doesWho decides the stepsAt Harbor Mutual
Rule-based automationRuns a fixed, pre-written workflowThe people who wrote the rulesAuto-pays small glass claims
ML modelReturns one prediction for one inputThe surrounding applicationFraud score for a claim
Generative AI assistant (chatbot)Answers or drafts content in reply to each request; takes no actions by itselfThe user, one prompt at a timeHelps an adjuster summarize a claim file
AI agentPlans a multi-step task, calls tools and systems, checks results and continues until the goal is met or it needs a personThe agent, within limits people setHandles first notice of loss: collects details, checks the policy, opens the claim, books a repair shop
Multi-agent systemSeveral specialized agents work together, coordinated by an orchestratorThe orchestrating agent and the specialistsIntake, coverage, fraud and payment agents each handle part of a claim

Autonomy

Autonomy means the agent decides its next action without step-by-step human direction. Traditional software follows hard-coded instructions; an agent identifies the next appropriate action (AWS, What are AI agents?). Autonomy is a dial, not a switch, and the business sets it per task:

LevelWhat the agent may doHarbor Mutual example
SuggestRecommends an action; a person carries it outProposes a reserve amount for a new claim
Act with approvalPrepares the action and waits for a person to confirmDrafts the settlement letter; the adjuster approves before sending
Act within limitsActs on its own inside set boundaries and escalates anything outside themBooks a rental car up to $40 a day; anything above goes to a person

The main risk is excessive agency: an agent decides that the best way to solve a problem is to take actions beyond its intended scope. AWS notes this is usually not malicious but an unintended result of automation, and recommends least-privilege permissions for agents and user confirmation for their actions (AWS Well-Architected Generative AI Lens, GENSEC05-BP01).

Tool use

Tools are what let an agent act rather than just talk: APIs, databases, email, search, code execution and other business systems. The agent recognizes when a step needs a tool and calls it (AWS, What are AI agents?). An agent can only do what its tools allow, so the tool list is also its permission list. The open Model Context Protocol (MCP) is a common standard for connecting agents to tools and data.

Harbor Mutual's claims-intake agent might be given these tools: look up a policy, create a claim record, request photos from the customer, search the repair-shop network, and send a text message. It is deliberately not given a "make payment" tool.

Memory and planning

Agents also rely on planning (breaking a goal into ordered steps) and memory: short-term memory of the current conversation and long-term memory that persists across sessions (AWS, What are AI agents?). Memory is what lets the agent remember that a customer already uploaded photos yesterday, and it is also personal data that must be governed.

Agent-to-agent communication

In a multi-agent system, agents pass tasks and results to each other. Open protocols such as Agent2Agent (A2A) standardize how agents from different teams or vendors talk to each other, in the same way that MCP standardizes how an agent talks to a tool (AWS Prescriptive Guidance, Agentic AI frameworks, platforms, protocols, and tools on AWS). For a business, open protocols reduce vendor lock-in and let agents built by different teams work together.

Orchestration strategies

Orchestration is how the work is divided and coordinated. The main strategies:

StrategyHow it worksGood for
Single agentOne agent with a set of tools handles the whole taskContained tasks; the simplest to build and govern
Sequential workflowAgents or steps run in a fixed order, each passing its result to the nextPredictable processes where the order never changes
Supervisor (hierarchical)A supervisor agent breaks the task down, assigns parts to specialist agents and combines their resultsComplex tasks that need several kinds of expertise
RoutingA front agent decides which specialist should handle the request and hands it overMany request types arriving at one entry point
ParallelSeveral agents work on parts of the task at the same timeIndependent subtasks where speed matters

AWS's own multi-agent example has a supervisor agent that receives a customer's question, works out whether it is about an existing mortgage, a new mortgage or a general topic, and routes it to the right specialist agent. Each specialist should have a clearly defined role, with overlapping responsibilities kept to a minimum (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, multi-agent collaboration).

At Harbor Mutual, a supervisor handles a new home claim like this:

  1. Intake agentTalks to the customer, collects the loss details and photos.
  2. Coverage agentChecks the policy and which parts of the loss are covered.
  3. Fraud agentRequests a fraud score and gathers any red flags.
  4. SupervisorCombines the results. Low-risk, covered claims go to the fast-track queue; anything else goes to an adjuster with a summary.
  5. AdjusterMakes the payment or denial decision. No agent has a payment tool.

Building agents on AWS

Amazon Bedrock AgentCore is AWS's platform for building, deploying and operating agents with any framework and any foundation model. Its services can be used together or separately:

AgentCore serviceBusiness purpose
Runtime and HarnessRuns agents securely, each session isolated; the harness lets teams define an agent by its model, instructions and tools without writing the agent loop
GatewayTurns existing APIs and Lambda functions into MCP tools that agents can call
MemoryShort-term and long-term memory for context-aware agents
IdentityControls which users and systems an agent acts for, using the company's existing identity provider
PolicyDeterministic rules on which tools an agent may call and under what conditions, checked before every tool call

Amazon Bedrock Agents (launched November 2023) is now Amazon Bedrock Agents Classic and stopped accepting new customers on July 30, 2026. Existing users keep it; AWS recommends AgentCore for new agents.

Scenario wording that signals an agent: "completes multi-step tasks", "takes actions in other systems", "decides which tool to use", "works with minimal supervision". Wording that signals a chatbot or assistant: "answers questions", "drafts", "summarizes" with a person acting on the result.

More autonomy isn't automatically better. For high-impact or irreversible actions (payments, denials, account changes), the right answer limits the agent's tools and permissions and keeps a person in the loop.

Monitoring, drift and ongoing updates

Skill 1.2.3

Traditional software keeps working the way it was written until someone changes it. An AI solution can get worse without anyone touching it, because the world it was trained on moves on. A model that was 90% accurate at launch may be 75% accurate a year later, and nothing breaks loudly: decisions just get quietly worse. This gradual decline is called drift.

Type of changeWhat changesHarbor Mutual example
Data driftThe inputs the model sees no longer look like the training dataHarbor Mutual expands into a coastal region; claim amounts and causes look nothing like the training years
Concept driftThe relationship between inputs and the right answer changesFraudsters learn which claims get flagged and change their behavior; old signals stop predicting fraud
Bias driftResults become less fair for some groups over timeClaims from one region start being flagged far more often than others
Feature attribution driftThe model starts relying on different factors than it did at launchThe fraud score suddenly leans heavily on postal code, which needs investigating
Generative AI quality changesAnswers degrade because source documents go stale, users ask new kinds of questions, or the provider updates or retires the model versionThe customer assistant quotes last year's deductible rules after a policy update

The first four are the monitoring types AWS names for ML models in production: data quality, model quality, bias drift and feature attribution drift (Amazon SageMaker AI Developer Guide, Model Monitor).

Monitoring works as a continuous loop:

  1. Set a baseline at launchRecord what the training data and early results look like: accuracy, input ranges, outcome rates by group.
  2. Capture production inputs and outputsKeep a record of what the model received and what it predicted or generated.
  3. Compare against the baseline on a scheduleLook for shifts in inputs, accuracy, fairness and, for generative AI, quality measures such as relevance and safety.
  4. Bring in the real outcomesAccuracy can only be measured once the truth is known, which often arrives late. A fraud investigation can take months.
  5. Alert when a threshold is crossedFor example, an alert when fraud-catch rate drops below 70% or flag rates differ by more than a set margin between regions.
  6. RemediateRetrain on recent data, fix the upstream data source, adjust the decision threshold, update the documents behind a generative AI assistant, roll back, or retire the solution.

Monitoring has an owner and a budget. A business case that counts only the build cost and not the ongoing monitoring, retraining and review effort understates the real cost of AI.

On AWS:

  • Traditional ML models: AWS's replacement for SageMaker Model Monitor combines open-source SageMaker AI monitoring solutions, Amazon QuickSight governance dashboards and Amazon CloudWatch metrics and alarms (Amazon SageMaker AI Developer Guide, Model Monitor availability change).
  • Agents: AgentCore Observability traces each step, and AgentCore Evaluations measures task quality before and after launch.

Amazon SageMaker Model Monitor is no longer open to new customers. Existing customers can keep using it, but no new features are planned.

When a scenario says a model "performed well at launch but results have declined", the answer involves monitoring for drift and retraining (or fixing the data), not replacing the model with a bigger one.

"The model passed testing, so the project is done" is wrong for AI. Plans that have no monitoring, no named owner and no retraining process are a classic wrong answer.

Classifying AI tools to reduce shadow AI

Skill 1.2.4

Shadow AI is the use of AI tools that the organization hasn't reviewed or approved: an employee pastes a customer complaint into a free public chatbot, or a team signs up for an AI note-taker with a personal credit card. It usually starts with good intentions (people want to work faster), which is why banning everything rarely works.

RiskWhat can happen at Harbor Mutual
Data leakagePolicyholder names, medical details from injury claims or claim photos are sent to a provider whose terms allow it to keep or train on submitted data
CompliancePersonal data leaves approved regions or systems, breaking privacy and insurance regulations
Intellectual propertyUnderwriting guidelines are pasted into an external tool; generated content may raise ownership questions
Quality and accountabilityAn agent sends a customer an inaccurate coverage answer drafted by an unvetted tool, and no one can trace where it came from
Cost and duplicationDozens of overlapping subscriptions bought on expense reports

The fix is a transparent classification: one published list that tells every employee which tools they may use, for what, and how to request something new.

StatusMeaningHarbor Mutual example
ApprovedReviewed and allowed, often with conditions on what data may be usedThe company assistant built on Amazon Bedrock, approved for customer and claims data; a coding assistant, approved for internal code only
Under evaluationBeing reviewed or piloted by a named team; not for general use, and only with test or public dataAn AI meeting note-taker, piloted by the marketing team with no customer data
BlockedNot allowed, with the reason stated and an approved alternative namedFree public chatbots for any company or customer data. Alternative: the company assistant.

Each entry in the register records the tool, its status, who owns it, what data it may handle, the reason for the decision and the next review date. Running the process:

  1. Discover what's in useSurvey teams, check expense reports and software purchases, and review network and browser logs for AI services.
  2. Assess each toolHow does the provider use submitted data? Where is it stored? Does it meet security, privacy and legal requirements? What does it cost?
  3. Classify and publishPut the decision in the register, in plain language, where every employee can find it.
  4. Offer approved alternativesEvery blocked tool should point to an approved one that meets the same need.
  5. Give a fast path for requestsA simple form and a target turnaround, so people ask instead of working around the process.
  6. Enforce and reviewTechnical controls back up the policy, and the list is reviewed regularly because tools and their terms change.

The AWS Generative AI Security Scoping Matrix helps decide how much review a tool needs by how much of it you own. It defines five scopes:

  1. A public consumer app.
  2. An enterprise app with generative AI features, under a business agreement.
  3. Your own application built on a pre-trained model.
  4. A fine-tuned model.
  5. A model you trained yourself.

For Scope 1, AWS notes that providers may use submitted data to improve their services, and advises using public, non-proprietary data. Proprietary data belongs in tools with contractual protections (Scope 2) or in applications the organization builds itself (Scopes 3 to 5).

The best answer to shadow AI combines a clear, published list, approved alternatives that are as easy to use as the public tools, training on what data may go where, and a fast review process. Each part on its own is weaker.

A blanket ban on all AI tools is usually the wrong answer: it pushes use further out of sight and gives up the productivity the employees were looking for. Leaving it to each employee's judgment is wrong too.

Key takeaways

  • Use rules when the logic is stable, fully defined and must be identical and auditable every time; use AI for unstructured inputs, many interacting factors or patterns that keep changing. Combine them: AI for judgment, rules as guardrails.
  • An AI agent pursues a goal through multiple steps and takes actions with tools; a model returns one prediction and a chatbot replies to one request. Agents rely on autonomy, tool use, memory, planning, agent-to-agent communication and orchestration.
  • Autonomy is set per task (suggest, act with approval, act within limits). Limit agent tools and permissions to prevent excessive agency, and keep people on high-impact decisions.
  • Orchestration strategies include single agent, sequential, supervisor, routing and parallel. MCP connects agents to tools; A2A connects agents to each other. On AWS, build agents on Amazon Bedrock AgentCore.
  • AI degrades silently through data drift, concept drift, bias drift and stale content. Set a baseline, monitor against it, alert on thresholds and remediate by retraining, fixing data or rolling back, with a named owner and budget.
  • Reduce shadow AI with a published register of approved, under-evaluation and blocked tools, approved alternatives, training, a fast request path and regular review.
