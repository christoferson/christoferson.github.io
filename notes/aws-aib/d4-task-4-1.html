Task 4.1: Assess AI business readiness and maturity

Knowing where an organization really stands before deciding how fast to go: assessing readiness across leadership, data, culture, technology and governance, placing the organization on a maturity scale, pinpointing the gaps that would stop AI from scaling, and turning that picture into a sequenced plan of investments.

Overview

Task 4.1

Assess AI business readiness and maturity.

Organizations often overestimate their readiness for AI. A successful demo makes the technology look like the hard part, when the real obstacles are usually elsewhere: data spread across systems no one can join, no one accountable for AI risk, staff worried about their jobs, or leaders who agree on "doing AI" but not on what for. An honest assessment prevents the most common pattern of failure: many pilots, few production systems.

The AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (AWS CAF) is AWS's structure for this kind of assessment. It groups the capabilities an organization needs into six perspectives, each owned by different stakeholders (AWS whitepaper, An Overview of the AWS Cloud Adoption Framework):

PerspectivePurposeCommon stakeholders
BusinessEnsures investments accelerate business outcomesCEO, CFO, COO, CIO, CTO
PeopleBridges technology and business; culture, organizational structure, leadership and workforceCIO, COO, CTO, enterprise-wide leaders
GovernanceOrchestrates initiatives while maximizing benefits and minimizing risksChief transformation officer, CIO, CTO, CFO, chief data officer, chief risk officer
PlatformBuilds a scalable platform and modernizes workloadsCTO, technology leaders, architects, engineers
SecurityConfidentiality, integrity and availability of data and workloadsCISO, chief compliance officer, internal audit, security architects
OperationsServices delivered at the level the business needsInfrastructure and operations leaders, site reliability engineers, IT service managers

AWS's CAF for AI, ML and generative AI keeps these foundational capabilities, enriches many of them with what AI demands, and adds new AI-specific ones. Examples use Harbor Mutual, a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees, 1.2 million policyholders and a 400-agent contact center, plus examples from other industries.

Assessing readiness across critical dimensions

Skill 4.1.1

Readiness is the organization's ability to adopt AI successfully now. It is assessed across several dimensions, because a weakness in any one of them can stop progress, however strong the others are. An organization with excellent data scientists but no executive sponsor, or strong leadership commitment but no usable data, is not ready.

DimensionQuestions to askSigns of readinessSigns of trouble
Leadership alignmentDo leaders agree on why AI matters and which outcomes it should improve? Is there an executive sponsor with budget and authority?AI objectives in the business strategy; a named sponsor; multi-year fundingEach executive has a different AI agenda; funding is ad hoc; AI is "an IT project"
Data quality and accessIs the data needed for priority use cases available, accurate, connected and allowed to be used?Data owners named; key data sets documented and accessibleData in silos; months to get access; unknown quality
Cultural preparednessAre people willing to experiment, use data in decisions, and change how they work?Teams try new tools; failures are discussed openly; staff ask for AI trainingFear of job loss; failures are punished; "we've always done it this way"
Technical infrastructureCan the organization build, deploy, integrate, secure and run AI at scale?Cloud platform in place; modern integration; ability to deploy and monitorCore systems can't be integrated; every project builds its own infrastructure
Governance frameworksAre there policies, roles and processes to approve, monitor and be accountable for AI?AI policy, inventory and risk-based review processNo one knows which AI tools are in use; approvals depend on who you ask
Skills and talentAre the needed skills present, from AI literacy for everyone to specialist roles?Training program; a core of experienced practitionersTotal dependence on one or two experts or on vendors

How to run an assessment

  1. Agree the scope and purposeWhole enterprise or one division? The aim is to decide what to invest in, not to grade people, which keeps answers honest.
  2. Gather evidence from several sourcesInterviews with executives and front-line staff, surveys, reviews of data and systems, and of existing policies. Self-assessment alone tends to be optimistic.
  3. Score each dimension against defined criteriaFor example 1 (absent) to 5 (embedded), with a written description of each level so scores mean the same to everyone.
  4. Test against real use casesAsk "could we deliver our top three use cases today?" That turns abstract scores into concrete blockers.
  5. Report gaps and their consequencesNot just "data scores 2" but "the fraud upgrade can't start until claims and investigation data are joined".

Worked example: Harbor Mutual's readiness scores

DimensionScore (1 to 5)Evidence
Leadership alignment3CEO sponsor and three stated objectives; but business unit heads compete for AI budget and disagree on priorities
Data quality and access2Claims, policy and billing data in separate systems with different customer IDs; investigation outcomes missing for one branch; access requests take 6 to 8 weeks
Cultural preparedness2Contact-center staff worried about job cuts; a failed 2022 chatbot is still cited as proof "AI doesn't work here"; but a third of staff already use public AI tools
Technical infrastructure3Most systems run on AWS; the core policy system is old and hard to integrate; no shared AI platform
Governance frameworks1No AI policy, no inventory, no review process; shadow AI widespread
Skills and talent2Four data scientists, all on the fraud model; no generative AI experience; no AI literacy training

Testing against the top use cases makes the scores concrete:

  • The after-call summary use case could start now: its data is self-contained, but it needs a governance decision on call data.
  • The fraud upgrade is blocked by data.
  • Any customer-facing generative AI is blocked by governance.

The lowest score, governance, is also the cheapest to fix and blocks the most, so it comes first.

Readiness covers leadership, data, culture, technology and governance (plus skills), assessed with evidence from several sources and tested against real use cases. The weakest dimension usually determines what the organization can actually deliver.

Treating readiness as purely technical ("we're on the cloud, so we're ready") is wrong. Leadership, culture, data and governance gaps stop more AI programs than technology does.

Applying AI maturity models

Skill 4.1.2

A maturity model describes the stages organizations typically pass through as their AI capability grows. Readiness asks "can we do this now?"; maturity asks "how far along are we, and what does the next stage require?" Placing the organization on a maturity scale helps set realistic ambitions. A company that has never put a model into production shouldn't plan as if it can transform its business model next year.

Many consultancies and vendors publish maturity models with different names and numbers of stages. The exam guide's examples, from "experimentation" to "enterprise-scale deployment", fit a common five-stage pattern:

StageWhat it looks likeTypical limitsExample
1. AwarenessInterest and discussion; individuals try public AI tools on their ownNo strategy, budget or governance; shadow AIA regional retailer whose staff use free chatbots
2. ExperimentationPilots and proofs of concept in several teams, often uncoordinatedFew reach production; results not measured; each pilot builds its own toolingHarbor Mutual today: one production fraud model, several stalled pilots
3. OperationalA handful of use cases in production with measured value; basic governance and a shared platform emergingSuccess depends on a few people; scaling each new use case is still slowA bank with AI in fraud detection and customer service, run by a central team
4. Systematic (enterprise-scale)A portfolio of AI across functions; a center of excellence; reusable platforms; risk-based governance; widespread AI literacyCoordination across many teams; managing costs and risks at scaleA global manufacturer with AI in planning, maintenance, quality and finance
5. TransformationalAI shapes strategy, products and business models; continuous improvement is built inSustaining advantage as the technology and competitors moveA company whose core product is AI-driven

Using a maturity model well

  • Maturity varies inside an organization: Harbor Mutual's fraud team is at stage 3 while the rest of the company is at stage 2. Assess by function as well as overall.
  • Stages can't be skipped: each builds the capabilities the next needs. Enterprise-scale deployment without the shared platforms, governance and skills of the operational stage produces a larger version of the experimentation stage's problems.
  • Higher isn't always the goal: the target stage depends on strategy. A business where AI is a support tool may rightly aim for stage 3 or 4, not 5.
  • It's a conversation tool: a shared picture of where the organization is helps align leaders who otherwise talk past each other. One may think "we're behind", another "we're fine", because each sees only their own area.

The AWS CAF describes the journey as four iterative phases: Envision (identify and prioritize opportunities linked to business outcomes), Align (identify capability gaps across the six perspectives and align stakeholders), Launch (deliver pilots in production that demonstrate incremental value) and Scale (expand to the desired scale and sustain the benefits). Organizations repeat these phases as they mature.

"Many pilots, little in production, results not measured" signals the experimentation stage. "A portfolio across functions, shared platforms, a CoE, risk-based governance" signals enterprise scale. The right next step builds the capabilities of the next stage, not the one after.

Planning a transformational AI program from the experimentation stage, without first building production, governance and data capabilities, is a classic wrong answer.

Identifying capability gaps

Skill 4.1.3

A capability gap is the difference between what the organization can do today and what its AI ambitions require. Gaps are grouped into four areas, which roughly correspond to the CAF perspectives. Naming them precisely is what makes them fixable: "we need better data" can't be acted on; "claims and policy data use different customer IDs" can.

AreaTypical gapsHow they show up
PeopleAI literacy across the workforce; specialist skills (ML engineering, data engineering, AI product management); leadership understanding of AI; change readinessLeaders can't judge proposals; staff misuse or avoid tools; projects wait for scarce experts
ProcessNo way to choose and prioritize use cases; no path from pilot to production; work processes not redesigned around AI; no measurement of valuePilots that never end; AI added to old processes with little effect; benefits unclaimed
TechnologyData silos and integration limits; no shared AI platform; no monitoring; legacy systems that can't connectEach project rebuilds the same plumbing; months spent getting data; production issues found by users
GovernanceNo AI policy or inventory; unclear accountability; no risk classification; no responsible AI reviewShadow AI; launches blocked late by legal; nobody owns incidents

Worked example: Harbor Mutual's gap analysis

The analysis lists each gap against the use cases it blocks, so the cost of leaving it open is visible:

GapAreaBlocksRemedy
No AI policy, inventory or review processGovernanceAll customer-facing generative AI; safe use of call recordingsSteering committee, AI policy, tool register, risk-tiered review (3 months)
Customer data split across systems with different IDsTechnologyFraud upgrade, retention predictionCustomer ID matching and a governed claims data set (6 to 9 months)
Investigation outcomes not recorded in one branchProcessFraud model accuracy and fairnessMandatory outcome recording; recover past outcomes from files
No generative AI experiencePeopleContact-center assistantPartner-led build with knowledge transfer; train two internal engineers
Fear of job losses in the contact centerPeopleAdoption of after-call summariesClear communication on role changes; involve agents in the pilot design
No path from pilot to productionProcessEvery new use caseStage gates with production criteria; a shared platform owned by a small central team

Notice that only one of the six gaps is purely technical. That is typical, and it is why readiness programs led only by IT tend to fail.

Express gaps across people, process, technology and governance, specifically enough to act on, and link each to the use cases it blocks.

Closing only the technology gaps (buying a platform) while ignoring skills, processes and governance is a common and expensive mistake.

Prioritizing investments and creating progression pathways

Skill 4.1.4

An assessment usually finds more gaps than can be closed at once. Prioritization decides which capabilities to build first, and a progression pathway sequences them into a roadmap that moves the organization from its current maturity stage toward its target, delivering value along the way.

Principles for prioritizing

  • Follow the strategy: invest first in capabilities that unblock the use cases tied to the most important objectives.
  • Respect dependencies: some capabilities are prerequisites. Governance must exist before customer-facing AI; a joined data set before the fraud upgrade.
  • Build "minimum viable" foundations: enough governance, data and platform to deliver the next use cases safely, not a perfect enterprise program before anything ships.
  • Pair foundations with visible wins: each phase should deliver a business result, so support and funding continue.
  • Match the current stage: an organization in the experimentation stage needs to build the capabilities for its first few production successes, not those for enterprise scale.

Worked example: Harbor Mutual's 18-month pathway

Harbor Mutual is at stage 2 (experimentation) and targets stage 4 (enterprise scale) in about three years. The first 18 months:

  1. Months 0 to 3: foundations and a quick winForm the steering committee; publish the AI policy and tool register; start AI literacy training for leaders. Launch the after-call summary pilot with 40 agents. Exit criteria: policy approved; pilot meets its targets.
  2. Months 4 to 9: first production systemsRoll out after-call summaries to all agents. Start customer data matching. Build the policy assistant with a partner, training two internal engineers. Set up shared monitoring. Exit criteria: two systems in production with measured value; risk-tiered review working.
  3. Months 10 to 18: repeatable deliveryForm a small center of excellence and a shared AI platform; start the fraud upgrade on the joined data; extend literacy training to all staff. Exit criteria: a new use case can move from approved idea to production in under three months.

Each phase has exit criteria, so the next phase's investment depends on evidence of progress. The pathway also links every capability investment to a business result:

  • Governance enables the policy assistant.
  • Data matching enables the fraud upgrade, which serves the $3 million fraud objective.
  • The CoE and platform cut the cost and time of every later use case.

Reassessing

Readiness and maturity should be reassessed at least yearly, and after major changes such as an acquisition, a new strategy or a significant new technology. The roadmap is updated accordingly; it is a living plan, not a fixed contract.

Good progression pathways follow strategic objectives, respect dependencies, build minimum viable foundations alongside quick wins, match the current maturity stage, and use exit criteria to release each phase's investment.

Two opposite mistakes: spending a year building foundations with no business result (support collapses), and scaling use cases without foundations (risk and rework explode).

Key takeaways

  • The AWS CAF groups capabilities into six perspectives: Business, People, Governance, Platform, Security and Operations. Its journey phases are Envision, Align (where capability gaps are identified), Launch and Scale.
  • Assess readiness across leadership alignment, data quality and access, cultural preparedness, technical infrastructure, governance frameworks and skills, using evidence from several sources and testing against real use cases. The weakest dimension usually limits delivery.
  • Maturity models place the organization on a path such as awareness, experimentation, operational, systematic (enterprise scale), transformational. Maturity varies by function, stages can't be skipped, and the target depends on strategy.
  • Name capability gaps precisely across people, process, technology and governance, and link each to the use cases it blocks. Most gaps aren't purely technical.
  • Prioritize capability investments by strategy and dependencies; build minimum viable foundations paired with visible wins; sequence them into a phased pathway with exit criteria; reassess regularly.
