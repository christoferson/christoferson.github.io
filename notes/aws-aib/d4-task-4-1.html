Task 4.1: Assess AI business readiness and maturity

Knowing where an organization really stands before deciding how fast to go: assessing readiness across leadership, data, culture, technology and governance, placing the organization on a maturity scale, pinpointing the gaps that would stop AI from scaling, and turning that picture into a sequenced plan of investments.

Overview

Task 4.1

Assess AI business readiness and maturity.

Organizations often overestimate their readiness for AI. A successful demo makes the technology look like the hard part, when the real obstacles are usually elsewhere: data spread across systems no one can join, no one accountable for AI risk, staff worried about their jobs, or leaders who agree on "doing AI" but not on what for. An honest assessment prevents the most common pattern of failure: many pilots, few production systems.

The AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (AWS CAF) is AWS's structure for this kind of assessment. It groups the capabilities an organization needs into six perspectives, each owned by different stakeholders (AWS whitepaper, An Overview of the AWS Cloud Adoption Framework):

Business

PurposeEnsures investments accelerate business outcomes.

Common stakeholdersCEO, CFO, COO, CIO, CTO.

People

PurposeBridges technology and business; culture, organizational structure, leadership and workforce.

Common stakeholdersCIO, COO, CTO, enterprise-wide leaders.

Governance

PurposeOrchestrates initiatives while maximizing benefits and minimizing risks.

Common stakeholdersChief transformation officer, CIO, CTO, CFO, chief data officer, chief risk officer.

Platform

PurposeBuilds a scalable platform and modernizes workloads.

Common stakeholdersCTO, technology leaders, architects, engineers.

Security

PurposeConfidentiality, integrity and availability of data and workloads.

Common stakeholdersCISO, chief compliance officer, internal audit, security architects.

Operations

PurposeServices delivered at the level the business needs.

Common stakeholdersInfrastructure and operations leaders, site reliability engineers, IT service managers.

AWS's CAF for AI, ML and generative AI keeps these foundational capabilities, enriches many of them with what AI demands, and adds new AI-specific ones.

The running example is a regional hospital group with six hospitals. Its leaders want AI to cut emergency readmissions (patients returning to hospital within 30 days of being sent home) by 10%, and to give doctors and nurses back time now spent on paperwork. Healthcare is a demanding test of readiness: the data is sensitive and spread across systems, the staff are skeptical with good reason, and mistakes affect patients directly.

Assessing readiness across critical dimensions

Skill 4.1.1Assess readiness across critical dimensions: leadership alignment, data quality, cultural preparedness, technical infrastructure, governance frameworks

AI readiness

The organization's ability to adopt AI successfully now: whether its leaders, data, people, technology and governance could support the AI it wants to deliver next. Readiness is about the present and about specific ambitions; an organization can be ready for an internal summarization tool but not for an AI that makes decisions about customers.

Readiness is assessed across several dimensions, because a weakness in any one of them can stop progress, however strong the others are. An organization with excellent data scientists but no executive sponsor, or strong leadership commitment but no usable data, is not ready.

Leadership alignment

Questions to askDo leaders agree on why AI matters and which outcomes it should improve? Is there an executive sponsor with budget and authority?

Signs of readinessAI objectives in the business strategy; a named sponsor; multi-year funding.

Signs of troubleEach executive has a different AI agenda; funding is ad hoc; AI is "an IT project".

Data quality and access

Questions to askIs the data needed for priority use cases available, accurate, connected and allowed to be used?

Signs of readinessData owners named; key data sets documented and accessible.

Signs of troubleData in silos; months to get access; unknown quality.

Cultural preparedness

Questions to askAre people willing to experiment, use data in decisions, and change how they work?

Signs of readinessTeams try new tools; failures are discussed openly; staff ask for AI training.

Signs of troubleFear of job loss; failures are punished; "we've always done it this way".

Technical infrastructure

Questions to askCan the organization build, deploy, integrate, secure and run AI at scale?

Signs of readinessCloud platform in place; modern integration; ability to deploy and monitor.

Signs of troubleCore systems can't be integrated; every project builds its own infrastructure.

Governance frameworks

Questions to askAre there policies, roles and processes to approve, monitor and be accountable for AI?

Signs of readinessAI policy, inventory and risk-based review process.

Signs of troubleNo one knows which AI tools are in use; approvals depend on who you ask.

Skills and talent

Questions to askAre the needed skills present, from AI literacy for everyone to specialist roles?

Signs of readinessTraining program; a core of experienced practitioners.

Signs of troubleTotal dependence on one or two experts or on vendors.

How to run an assessment

  1. Agree the scope and purposeWhole enterprise or one division? The aim is to decide what to invest in, not to grade people, which keeps answers honest.
  2. Gather evidence from several sourcesInterviews with executives and front-line staff, surveys, reviews of data and systems, and of existing policies. Self-assessment alone tends to be optimistic.
  3. Score each dimension against defined criteriaFor example 1 (absent) to 5 (embedded), with a written description of each level so scores mean the same to everyone.
  4. Test against real use casesAsk "could we deliver our top three use cases today?" That turns abstract scores into concrete blockers.
  5. Report gaps and their consequencesNot just "data scores 2" but "the readmission model can't start until admission, lab and pharmacy data are joined".

Worked example: the hospital group's readiness scores

Leadership alignment: 3 of 5

The CEO sponsors AI and has set two clear objectives. But the six hospital directors compete for the AI budget and disagree on priorities.

Data quality and access: 2 of 5

Patient records, lab results and pharmacy data sit in separate systems with different patient IDs. One hospital still records discharge details on paper forms, scanned as images. Access requests take 6 to 8 weeks.

Cultural preparedness: 2 of 5

Nurses worry that AI will be used to cut staffing, and a failed 2022 scheduling tool is still cited as proof "AI doesn't work here". Yet many doctors already use public chatbots to draft letters.

Technical infrastructure: 3 of 5

Most systems run on AWS, but the core clinical record system is old and hard to integrate, and there is no shared AI platform.

Governance frameworks: 1 of 5

No AI policy, no inventory and no review process. Shadow AI is widespread, including doctors pasting patient notes into public tools, which is a serious privacy breach.

Skills and talent: 2 of 5

Three data analysts, who run the one model in production (a bed-demand forecast); no generative AI experience; no AI literacy training.

Testing against the top use cases makes the scores concrete:

  • Drafting discharge summaries (the letter that tells a patient's own doctor what happened in hospital) could start now: its data is all in the clinical record, but it needs a governance decision on using patient data with AI.
  • Readmission prediction is blocked by data.
  • Any patient-facing generative AI is blocked by governance.

The lowest score, governance, is also the cheapest to fix and blocks the most, so it comes first.

Readiness covers leadership, data, culture, technology and governance (plus skills), assessed with evidence from several sources and tested against real use cases. The weakest dimension usually determines what the organization can actually deliver.

Treating readiness as purely technical ("we're on the cloud, so we're ready") is wrong. Leadership, culture, data and governance gaps stop more AI programs than technology does.

Applying AI maturity models

Skill 4.1.2Apply AI maturity models to place an enterprise on its AI journey, from experimentation to enterprise-scale deployment

AI maturity model

A description of the stages organizations typically pass through as their AI capability grows, with what each stage looks like and what moving to the next requires.

Readiness asks "can we do this now?"; maturity asks "how far along are we, and what does the next stage require?" Placing the organization on a maturity scale helps set realistic ambitions. A company that has never put a model into production shouldn't plan as if it can transform its business model next year.

Many consultancies and vendors publish maturity models with different names and numbers of stages. The exam guide's examples, from "experimentation" to "enterprise-scale deployment", fit a common five-stage pattern:

1. Awareness

What it looks likeInterest and discussion; individuals try public AI tools on their own.

Typical limitsNo strategy, budget or governance; shadow AI.

ExampleA regional retailer whose staff use free chatbots.

2. Experimentation

What it looks likePilots and proofs of concept in several teams, often uncoordinated.

Typical limitsFew reach production; results not measured; each pilot builds its own tooling.

ExampleThe hospital group today: one production forecast, several stalled pilots.

3. Operational

What it looks likeA handful of use cases in production with measured value; basic governance and a shared platform emerging.

Typical limitsSuccess depends on a few people; scaling each new use case is still slow.

ExampleA bank with AI in fraud detection and customer service, run by a central team.

4. Systematic (enterprise-scale)

What it looks likeA portfolio of AI across functions; a center of excellence; reusable platforms; risk-based governance; widespread AI literacy.

Typical limitsCoordination across many teams; managing costs and risks at scale.

ExampleA global manufacturer with AI in planning, maintenance, quality and finance.

5. Transformational

What it looks likeAI shapes strategy, products and business models; continuous improvement is built in.

Typical limitsSustaining advantage as the technology and competitors move.

ExampleA company whose core product is AI-driven.

Using a maturity model well

  • Maturity varies inside an organization: the hospital group's radiology department, which runs an approved AI tool that puts urgent scans first in the reading queue, is at stage 3 while the rest of the group is at stage 2. Assess by function as well as overall.
  • Stages can't be skipped: each builds the capabilities the next needs. Enterprise-scale deployment without the shared platforms, governance and skills of the operational stage produces a larger version of the experimentation stage's problems.
  • Higher isn't always the goal: the target stage depends on strategy. A business where AI is a support tool may rightly aim for stage 3 or 4, not 5.
  • It's a conversation tool: a shared picture of where the organization is helps align leaders who otherwise talk past each other. One may think "we're behind", another "we're fine", because each sees only their own area.

The AWS CAF describes the journey as four iterative phases: Envision (identify and prioritize opportunities linked to business outcomes), Align (identify capability gaps across the six perspectives and align stakeholders), Launch (deliver pilots in production that demonstrate incremental value) and Scale (expand to the desired scale and sustain the benefits). Organizations repeat these phases as they mature.

"Many pilots, little in production, results not measured" signals the experimentation stage. "A portfolio across functions, shared platforms, a CoE, risk-based governance" signals enterprise scale. The right next step builds the capabilities of the next stage, not the one after.

Planning a transformational AI program from the experimentation stage, without first building production, governance and data capabilities, is a classic wrong answer.

Identifying capability gaps

Skill 4.1.3Identify capability gaps across people, process, technology and governance that block AI transformation

A capability gap is the difference between what the organization can do today and what its AI ambitions require. Gaps are grouped into four areas, which roughly correspond to the CAF perspectives. Naming them precisely is what makes them fixable: "we need better data" can't be acted on; "the lab system and the patient record use different patient IDs" can.

People

Typical gapsAI literacy across the workforce; specialist skills (ML engineering, data engineering, AI product management); leadership understanding of AI; change readiness.

How they show upLeaders can't judge proposals; staff misuse or avoid tools; projects wait for scarce experts.

Process

Typical gapsNo way to choose and prioritize use cases; no path from pilot to production; work processes not redesigned around AI; no measurement of value.

How they show upPilots that never end; AI added to old processes with little effect; benefits unclaimed.

Technology

Typical gapsData silos and integration limits; no shared AI platform; no monitoring; legacy systems that can't connect.

How they show upEach project rebuilds the same plumbing; months spent getting data; production issues found by users.

Governance

Typical gapsNo AI policy or inventory; unclear accountability; no risk classification; no responsible AI review.

How they show upShadow AI; launches blocked late by legal; nobody owns incidents.

Worked example: the hospital group's gap analysis

The analysis lists each gap against the use cases it blocks, so the cost of leaving it open is visible:

No AI policy, inventory or review process

AreaGovernance.

BlocksAll patient-facing generative AI; any safe use of clinical notes with AI.

RemedySteering committee including the chief medical officer, AI policy, tool register, risk-tiered review (3 months).

Patient data split across systems with different IDs

AreaTechnology.

BlocksReadmission prediction, missed-appointment prediction.

RemedyPatient ID matching and a governed clinical data set (6 to 9 months).

Discharge details recorded on paper at one hospital

AreaProcess.

BlocksReadmission model accuracy and fairness, because that hospital's patients would be missing.

RemedyElectronic discharge forms; extract past forms from the scans.

No generative AI experience

AreaPeople.

BlocksThe discharge summary assistant.

RemedyPartner-led build with knowledge transfer; train two internal engineers.

Fear that AI will be used to cut nursing staff

AreaPeople.

BlocksAdoption of AI documentation tools on the wards.

RemedyA clear statement on how freed time will be used; involve nurses in the pilot design.

No path from pilot to production

AreaProcess.

BlocksEvery new use case.

RemedyStage gates with production criteria; a shared platform owned by a small central team.

Notice that only one of the six gaps is purely technical. That is typical, and it is why readiness programs led only by IT tend to fail.

Express gaps across people, process, technology and governance, specifically enough to act on, and link each to the use cases it blocks.

Closing only the technology gaps (buying a platform) while ignoring skills, processes and governance is a common and expensive mistake.

Prioritizing investments and creating progression pathways

Skill 4.1.4Prioritize development investments and create progression pathways based on strategic objectives and current maturity

An assessment usually finds more gaps than can be closed at once. Prioritization decides which capabilities to build first, and a progression pathway, a phased roadmap of capability building with conditions for moving from one phase to the next, sequences them into a roadmap that moves the organization from its current maturity stage toward its target, delivering value along the way.

Principles for prioritizing

  • Follow the strategy: invest first in capabilities that unblock the use cases tied to the most important objectives.
  • Respect dependencies: some capabilities are prerequisites. Governance must exist before patient-facing AI; a joined data set before the readmission model.
  • Build "minimum viable" foundations: enough governance, data and platform to deliver the next use cases safely, not a perfect enterprise program before anything ships.
  • Pair foundations with visible wins: each phase should deliver a business result, so support and funding continue.
  • Match the current stage: an organization in the experimentation stage needs to build the capabilities for its first few production successes, not those for enterprise scale.

Worked example: the hospital group's 18-month pathway

The hospital group is at stage 2 (experimentation) and targets stage 4 (enterprise scale) in about three years. The first 18 months:

  1. Months 0 to 3: foundations and a quick winForm the steering committee; publish the AI policy and tool register, with an approved alternative to public chatbots; start AI literacy training for leaders. Pilot discharge summary drafting with the doctors on two wards. Exit criteria: policy approved; pilot meets its targets.
  2. Months 4 to 9: first production systemsRoll out discharge summaries across the group. Start patient ID matching. Build an assistant that answers staff questions from clinical guidelines, with a partner, training two internal engineers. Set up shared monitoring. Exit criteria: two systems in production with measured value; risk-tiered review working.
  3. Months 10 to 18: repeatable deliveryForm a small center of excellence and a shared AI platform; start the readmission model on the joined data; extend literacy training to all staff. Exit criteria: a new use case can move from approved idea to production in under three months.

Each phase has exit criteria, so the next phase's investment depends on evidence of progress. The pathway also links every capability investment to a business result:

  • Governance enables the guideline assistant and stops patient notes going into public tools.
  • Data matching enables the readmission model, which serves the 10% readmission objective.
  • The CoE and platform cut the cost and time of every later use case.

Reassessing

Readiness and maturity should be reassessed at least yearly, and after major changes such as an acquisition, a new strategy or a significant new technology. The roadmap is updated accordingly; it is a living plan, not a fixed contract.

Good progression pathways follow strategic objectives, respect dependencies, build minimum viable foundations alongside quick wins, match the current maturity stage, and use exit criteria to release each phase's investment.

Two opposite mistakes: spending a year building foundations with no business result (support collapses), and scaling use cases without foundations (risk and rework explode).

Key takeaways

  • The AWS CAF groups capabilities into six perspectives: Business, People, Governance, Platform, Security and Operations. Its journey phases are Envision, Align (where capability gaps are identified), Launch and Scale.
  • Assess readiness across leadership alignment, data quality and access, cultural preparedness, technical infrastructure, governance frameworks and skills, using evidence from several sources and testing against real use cases. The weakest dimension usually limits delivery.
  • Maturity models place the organization on a path such as awareness, experimentation, operational, systematic (enterprise scale), transformational. Maturity varies by function, stages can't be skipped, and the target depends on strategy.
  • Name capability gaps precisely across people, process, technology and governance, and link each to the use cases it blocks. Most gaps aren't purely technical.
  • Prioritize capability investments by strategy and dependencies; build minimum viable foundations paired with visible wins; sequence them into a phased pathway with exit criteria; reassess regularly.
