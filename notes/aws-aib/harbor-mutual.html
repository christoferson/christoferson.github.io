The company

Harbor Mutual is a mid-size insurer. It sells two kinds of insurance to individuals and families:

  • Home insurance covers damage to a house and its contents from events such as fire, storms, burst pipes, theft and vandalism.
  • Auto insurance covers damage to the customer's car, injuries, and the customer's legal liability if they damage someone else's car or property.

Customers buy directly (online or by phone) or through independent brokers, intermediaries who compare several insurers on a customer's behalf and are paid a commission by the insurer they choose.

  • Size: 3,000 employees and 1.2 million policyholders, operating in several countries including EU member states.
  • Departments that appear in examples: Claims, Customer Service (the contact center), Underwriting, Marketing, IT, Risk, Legal and Compliance, HR, the fraud investigation unit, and regional branches that handle claims locally.

Insurance is a useful example for AI strategy because the business runs on documents and data, makes many decisions that affect people's finances, and is closely regulated. Most AI opportunities, and most AI risks, appear somewhere in an insurer.

How an insurer makes money

Customers pay a premium, usually yearly or monthly, in exchange for the insurer's promise to pay for covered losses. The insurer pools the premiums of many customers and uses them to pay the claims of the few who suffer a loss in a given year. Most customers never claim in a year; a few have large losses. Pooling makes that affordable for everyone.

The insurer makes a profit when the premiums it collects are larger than the claims it pays plus its running costs. Insurers track this with a few ratios:

Loss ratio

Claims paid divided by premiums collected. If Harbor Mutual collects $100 in premiums and pays $65 in claims, its loss ratio is 65%. Paying fraudulent claims, or overpaying genuine ones, pushes it up.

Expense ratio

Running costs (staff, systems, broker commissions) divided by premiums. A slow, manual claims process and an expensive contact center push it up.

Combined ratio

Loss ratio plus expense ratio. Below 100% means the insurance business itself makes a profit; above 100% means it loses money on insurance and must rely on investment income from the premiums it holds.

Three things therefore matter most to Harbor Mutual:

  • Paying the right claims, the right amount: claims are the biggest cost, so every dollar of fraud or overpayment avoided goes straight to profit.
  • Running efficiently: a cheaper, faster operation lets it charge competitive prices.
  • Keeping customers: winning a new customer costs far more than keeping one, and slow claims and poor service are the main reasons customers leave at renewal.

That is why its AI ambitions focus on claims speed, contact-center cost and fraud.

Who does what

Contact-center agents (400)

Answer customers' calls and messages: claim status, new claims, billing, policy changes and complaints. They search policy documents to answer questions and write notes about each call.

Claims processing clerks

Register new claims, attach documents and type information from documents such as repair estimates into the claims system.

Adjusters

Claims professionals who assess each claim: they read the claim file, check the evidence against the policy, decide whether the loss is covered and how much to pay, and negotiate with repair shops. Complex claims may need an inspector to visit.

Fraud investigators

Examine claims flagged as suspicious, gather evidence and record whether fraud was confirmed.

Underwriters

Decide whether to accept unusual risks and at what price, and maintain the pricing rules used for standard cases.

Repair shops and contractors (outside partners)

Inspect damaged cars and homes, send repair estimates, carry out repairs and invoice for them. Harbor Mutual has a network of approved repair shops.

Selling and renewing a policy

A policy is the insurance contract. Its policy wording, a long legal document organized in numbered clauses, sets out what is covered, what isn't (the exclusions), the maximum amounts paid (the limits) and the deductible: the part of each claim the customer pays themselves, for example the first $500. The life of a policy:

  1. QuoteThe customer asks for a price, online, by phone or through a broker, and provides details: the car or the house, where they live, their driving or claims history.
  2. UnderwritingThe insurer decides whether to accept the risk and at what price, using rating factors such as location, the car's value and past claims. Standard cases follow pricing rules automatically; unusual ones go to an underwriter.
  3. BindingThe customer accepts the price; the policy starts on a set date and is recorded in the policy system.
  4. Paying and servicingPremiums are billed monthly or yearly. During the year customers call or email to change details, such as a new address or an extra driver, which can change the price.
  5. RenewalBefore the policy ends, the insurer offers a new price for another year. Customers who don't renew are said to lapse. Retention efforts aim to keep the good customers who are likely to leave.

Handling a claim

A claim is a customer's request to be paid for a loss. Harbor Mutual handles about 10,000 claims a month, most of them car damage and home water or storm damage. Each claim builds up a claim file: the customer's description, photos, repair estimates, notes from calls, investigator reports and decisions. A complex claim file can run to 40 pages or more. The steps:

  1. First notice of lossThe customer reports the loss by phone, app, email or through a broker: what happened, when, and what was damaged.
  2. RegistrationA clerk opens a claim in the claims system and links it to the policy. Reports that arrive outside office hours wait until the next working morning.
  3. Coverage checkWas the policy active on the date of loss? Is the cause of loss (the peril) covered? Does an exclusion apply, for example because the home had been empty for months?
  4. Triage and fraud screeningSimple claims go to a fast track; each claim is checked against fraud rules and a fraud model; suspicious ones go to investigators.
  5. Gathering evidencePhotos from the customer; for injuries, medical reports; and a repair estimate. The customer takes the car to a repair shop, or a contractor inspects the home. The shop sends a quote listing the parts to replace, labor hours and hourly rate, paint and materials, tax and a total, almost always as a PDF or scanned paper attached to an email, in the shop's own layout.
  6. Entering the estimateThe claims system can't read a PDF, so a clerk opens each estimate and types its key figures into the claim record: shop, vehicle and plate, cause, labor hours, parts and total. Only then can the claim be compared with typical repair costs, scored and paid.
  7. Adjuster reviewAn adjuster reads the claim file, checks the evidence against the policy wording and decides how much to pay. For large or complex claims, an inspector visits.
  8. ApprovalPayments above $2,000 need a second manager's approval. The limit was set 15 years ago, when $2,000 was a large claim; repair costs have risen since, and today about 60% of claims exceed it.
  9. Settlement and paymentThe agreed amount, minus the deductible, is paid to the customer or directly to the repair shop.
  10. ClosingThe claim is closed and its outcome recorded, including whether fraud was confirmed. After the repair, the shop's invoice is checked against the approved estimate.

The time from first notice of loss to payment is the claim cycle time. Harbor Mutual's average is 12 days, and much of it is waiting rather than work, as the pain points below show.

Fraud investigation

About 2% of claims, around 200 a month, are fraudulent. Typical forms:

  • exaggerated damage or inflated repair bills;
  • damage that happened before the policy started;
  • staged accidents;
  • organized fraud rings that file similar claims with several insurers.

An average fraudulent claim would cost about $8,000 if paid.

  1. FlagEach claim is scored. Today this uses one older ML model plus about 400 hand-written rules, such as "claim filed within 30 days of the policy starting".
  2. InvestigateFlagged claims go to a fraud investigator, who checks records, contacts the repair shop and may interview the customer. Each investigation costs about $150 in staff time, and the honest customer's payment is delayed while it happens.
  3. Record the outcome"Fraud confirmed" or "legitimate". These recorded outcomes become the labels that ML models learn from, so recording them consistently matters.

Two kinds of mistake pull in opposite directions:

  • False positive: an honest customer is wrongly flagged, which costs an investigation and delays their payment.
  • False negative: a fraud is missed, which costs the payout.

Balancing the two is a recurring example in the guide.

The contact center

400 agents handle about 24,000 calls a day over about 220 working days a year. About 30% of calls are customers asking where their claim is; the rest are new claims, billing questions, policy changes and complaints. A typical call:

  1. Identify the customerConfirm identity and open their record.
  2. Handle the requestAnswer the question, often by searching long policy wordings and procedure documents, which takes about 2 minutes per call.
  3. Wrap upAfter the customer hangs up, the agent writes after-call notes in the customer record: what was asked, what was done, what happens next. This after-call work (also called wrap-up) takes about 6.2 minutes per call, during which the agent can't take the next call.

A central team also reads about 3,000 customer emails a day and forwards each to the right department.

Contact-center measures used in the guide

  • Average handling time: talk time plus after-call work, per call.
  • First-contact resolution: the share of issues solved in the first call, without the customer having to call back.
  • CSAT (customer satisfaction score): from a short survey after the call. Harbor Mutual's is 82%.
  • Cost per call: the contact center's total cost divided by calls handled.
  • Loaded hourly cost: an agent's pay plus benefits and overheads, about $35 an hour, or about $60,000 a year per agent.

Systems and data

Policy system

Holds every policy, its cover, price and history. It is old and hard to connect to new tools, which slows several AI projects.

Claims system

Holds claim records, payments and outcomes. Repair estimate figures are typed into it by hand.

Billing system

Premium payments. Each of the policy, claims and billing systems assigns its own customer ID.

Contact-center system

Records calls and their timestamps; produces call recordings and transcripts.

Document repository

Policy wordings, the home policy handbook (in more than one version), procedures and underwriting guidelines, some of them confidential.

Most systems run on AWS; the old policy system runs too, but connecting new tools to it is difficult.

Pain points and AI use case candidates

These are the places where Harbor Mutual loses time, money or quality today. Each one is a candidate for improvement; some are good AI use cases, and some are better fixed without AI. The figures are the guide's illustrative numbers and are used consistently across all pages.

1. Repair estimates are retyped by hand

Today: about 2,000 repair estimates arrive each week as PDFs or scans, each in the shop's own layout. A clerk opens each one and types about ten fields into the claims system, taking about 8 minutes per estimate. Estimates wait in a queue until a clerk gets to them.

ImpactAbout 3 of the 12 days of claim cycle time spent waiting; about 270 clerk-hours a week (roughly seven full-time clerks); typing errors that occasionally cause overpayments and rework. After a storm, estimates arrive at up to 5,000 a day, and the queue grows much longer.

CandidateDocument extraction: AI reads each PDF and fills in the fields, with confidence scores, so clerks check only uncertain fields. Objective served: claim cycle time.

2. Adjusters read long claim files

Today: before deciding a complex claim, an adjuster reads the whole file (notes, photos, estimates, reports), often 40 pages, taking about 45 minutes. Files wait until an adjuster has that time.

ImpactAbout 2 days of claim cycle time spent waiting; adjusters' time spent reading rather than deciding and talking to customers.

CandidateSummarization: generative AI condenses the file into the points an adjuster needs, with links to the source documents. Objective served: claim cycle time.

3. An outdated approval rule

Today: every payment above $2,000 needs a second manager's signature, a limit set 15 years ago. About 60% of claims now exceed it.

ImpactAbout 4 days of claim cycle time spent waiting for the second approval.

CandidateNot an AI use case. The fix is a process change: raise the limit, for example to $7,500, with random spot checks. This alone saves about 3 days.

4. After-call notes take over 6 minutes

Today: after every call, the agent types notes from memory, taking about 6.2 minutes. Note quality varies: 18% of notes fail the monthly quality audit.

Impact24,000 calls × 6.2 minutes, about 2,480 agent-hours a day spent on wrap-up. Incomplete notes mean the next agent has to ask the customer again.

CandidateGenerative AI drafts the notes from the call transcript; the agent reviews and saves. Objective served: contact-center cost per call.

5. Agents search long documents during calls

Today: to answer coverage questions, agents search policy wordings and procedure documents while the customer waits, about 2 minutes per call. Answers vary between agents, and some are wrong.

ImpactAbout 800 agent-hours a day; inconsistent answers that cause complaints and repeat calls.

CandidateA policy assistant: answers from approved documents with clause citations (RAG). Objective served: contact-center cost per call, and service quality.

6. Simple status calls

Today: about 30% of calls, around 7,200 a day, are customers asking where their claim is. The answer is in the claims system.

ImpactAgent time on questions that need no judgment; customers wait on hold for a simple answer.

CandidateSelf-service claim status on the website or app, possibly through an AI assistant. A simple status page with notifications might solve much of it without AI.

7. Emails sorted by hand

Today: a central team reads about 3,000 customer emails a day and forwards each to billing, claims, policy changes or complaints, about a minute each. Some go to the wrong team.

ImpactAbout 50 staff-hours a day; misrouted emails add a day or more to the reply.

CandidateClassification and routing: AI sorts each email into the right category.

8. Fraud detection relies on 400 rules and an old model

Today: the 400 rules were written by different people over eight years; some contradict each other, nobody knows which still work, and fraudsters learn to stay just under each rule's limits. Honest customers are flagged by mistake and wait longer for payment.

ImpactAbout 200 fraudulent claims a month at about $8,000 each are at stake. Leadership wants fraud losses $3 million a year lower. Every wrongly flagged honest claim costs a $150 investigation and a delayed payment.

CandidateA fraud model upgrade trained on 25 years of investigated claims. It is currently blocked by data problems (pain points 9 and 10). Objective served: fraud losses.

9. Customer data can't be joined across systems

Today: the policy, claims and billing systems each use their own customer ID. About 18% of customers can't be matched with certainty, and getting access to data for a new project takes 6 to 8 weeks.

ImpactThe fraud model can't see, for example, that the same phone number appears on claims under three different policies; every new data project starts with weeks of matching.

CandidateA data foundation, not an AI use case in itself: one matched, governed customer and claims data set that many AI use cases reuse.

10. Investigation outcomes missing in one branch

Today: one branch has never recorded whether its investigated claims were fraud, so its roughly 3,000 claims a year all appear "legitimate".

ImpactAny model trained on this data learns that claims like that branch's are safe.

CandidateA data quality and process fix: make outcome recording mandatory and recover past outcomes from investigators' files.

11. Claims reported at night wait until morning

Today: about 15% of claims, roughly 1,500 a month, are reported outside office hours, often right after an accident. They wait until the next working morning to be registered.

ImpactUp to 12 hours added to cycle time; customers left without a claim number, a repair shop or a rental car overnight.

CandidateA claims-intake AI agent that registers the claim, collects photos and offers approved repair shops around the clock, with no ability to make payments.

12. Staff use public AI tools on their own

Today: about a third of staff use free public chatbots, some pasting in customer emails.

ImpactCustomer data may be kept by providers that have no contract with Harbor Mutual; a privacy and compliance risk nobody can measure.

CandidateGovernance rather than a use case: a published register of approved and blocked tools, and an approved company assistant as the alternative.

Insurance terms used in the guide

Adjuster

A claims professional who assesses a claim, checks it against the policy and decides the payment.

Broker

An intermediary who compares insurers and arranges policies for customers, paid by commission.

Claim file

Everything collected about one claim: descriptions, photos, estimates, notes and decisions.

Claim cycle time

Days from first notice of loss to payment.

Combined ratio

Loss ratio plus expense ratio; below 100% means the insurance business is profitable.

Coverage (cover)

What the policy pays for. A "coverage decision" is deciding whether a particular loss is covered.

Deductible

The amount of each claim the customer pays themselves, for example the first $500.

Exclusion

Something the policy specifically doesn't cover, such as escape-of-water damage while a home is unoccupied for more than 60 days.

First notice of loss

The customer's first report of a loss, which starts a claim.

Lapse

A policy that isn't renewed.

Limit

The maximum the policy pays for a claim or type of loss.

Loss ratio

Claims paid divided by premiums collected.

Peril

A cause of loss, such as fire, storm, theft or escape of water.

Policy wording

The full legal text of the policy, organized in numbered clauses.

Premium

The price the customer pays for the policy.

Rating factors

The facts used to set a price, such as location, car value and claims history.

Repair estimate

A repair shop's or contractor's quote for fixing the damage: parts, labor, materials, tax and total.

Reserve

The amount the insurer sets aside for a claim it expects to pay.

Settlement

The agreed payment that closes a claim.

Underwriting

Deciding whether to insure someone and at what price.

Where Harbor Mutual starts with AI

  • Existing AI: a few analytics dashboards, one fraud-scoring ML model maintained by four data scientists, and about 400 fraud rules.
  • Shadow AI: about a third of staff already use free public chatbots on their own.
  • A past failure: a customer chatbot launched in 2022 without testing on real questions gave confusing answers and was withdrawn after two months; staff still cite it as proof that "AI doesn't work here".
  • Readiness: governance is weakest (no AI policy, inventory or review process); data is split across systems; staff worry about job cuts; the internal team has no generative AI experience.

At the start of the guide, Harbor Mutual is at the experimentation stage of AI maturity: some pilots, little in production, results not measured.

Business objectives

Leadership has set three objectives for the next two years. Most AI decisions in the guide are judged against them:

1. Cut the average claim cycle time from 12 days to 7

Faster settlement keeps customers at renewal and lowers handling cost. Pain points 1, 2, 3 and 11 sit here.

2. Reduce contact-center cost per call by 15% without lowering satisfaction

CSAT must stay at or above 82% while costs fall. Pain points 4, 5, 6 and 7 sit here.

3. Reduce fraud losses by $3 million a year

Better detection, without delaying more honest customers. Pain points 8, 9 and 10 sit here.

AI initiatives that appear in the guide

How these are designed, measured and governed is worked through on Harbor Mutual in practice.

After-call summaries (pain point 4)

The first quick win. Piloted with 40 agents, it saved 1.3 minutes per call against a comparison group and was scaled to all 400 agents.

Policy assistant for agents (pain point 5)

Answers agents' questions from approved policy documents, with clause citations, using RAG on Amazon Bedrock.

Customer-facing assistant (pain point 6)

A website assistant, launched only for claim status and document upload after coverage answers missed the release criteria.

Repair estimate extraction (pain point 1)

Reads repair estimate PDFs and fills in the claim fields.

Fraud model upgrade and fraud flywheel (pain points 8 to 10)

A better fraud model trained on investigated claims, improving as each new outcome is recorded.

Claims-intake agent (pain point 11)

An AI agent that registers new claims around the clock and offers repair shops, with no payment tool.

Home Protect

A possible new business model: water-leak sensors and AI alerts that prevent damage instead of paying for it.
