Task 4.4: Scale AI from pilots to enterprise-wide deployments

Getting out of "pilot purgatory": moving through envision, experiment, launch and scale in repeated cycles; starting with quick wins and reusing what works; setting up a center of excellence that speeds teams up rather than slowing them down; measuring and learning continuously; turning experiments into production-grade systems; and managing everything that changes when AI goes from one team to the whole enterprise.

Overview

Task 4.4

Scale AI from pilots to enterprise-wide deployments.

Many organizations get stuck in what is often called pilot purgatory: plenty of promising pilots, few systems in production, and even fewer delivering measured value at scale. The reasons are rarely about the model:

  • pilots built without a path to production;
  • no budget or owner after the pilot ends;
  • governance and security reviews that start too late;
  • every team building its own foundations;
  • benefits nobody tracks.

This task is about the methods that move AI from isolated experiments to an enterprise capability. The running example is a mobile and broadband telecom operator with a 400-agent contact center, field engineers, a network operations team and a large back office. It has proved AI in one pilot and now wants AI working across the business.

Iterative transformation phases

Skill 4.4.1Apply iterative transformation approaches through phases such as envision, experiment, launch and scale

An AI transformation can't be planned in full detail up front. Nobody knows in advance which use cases will deliver the most, how users will respond, or what the technology will be able to do in a year. An iterative approach moves through short phases, learns from each one and adjusts. The AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (CAF) recommends iterative, incremental phases because transforming incrementally demonstrates value quickly, avoids having to make far-reaching predictions, and keeps momentum while the roadmap evolves with experience.

The four phases

Envision

PurposeIdentify and prioritize AI opportunities linked to business outcomes, with sponsors.

Typical outputA prioritized set of use cases with value hypotheses and owners.

Exit criteriaSponsor and owner agreed; success measures defined; data available.

Experiment

PurposeTest feasibility and value quickly and cheaply, usually as a proof of concept.

Typical outputEvidence: does it work on our data, and is it worth it?

Exit criteriaMeets agreed quality and value thresholds, or is stopped.

Launch

PurposeRun a pilot in production with real users and real data, under governance.

Typical outputMeasured results against the baseline; operational lessons.

Exit criteriaKPIs met; release criteria met; users adopting; costs understood.

Scale

PurposeExpand to all intended users, teams or regions, and sustain the benefits.

Typical outputFull deployment; benefits tracked; foundations reused.

Exit criteriaBenefits realized and sustained; next cycle planned.

The CAF's own version of the cycle is envision, align, launch and scale. Its align phase focuses on identifying capability gaps, dependencies and stakeholder concerns before launch. Whichever version is used, two points matter. Each phase ends with a decision based on evidence. And the cycle repeats, with each scaled use case making the next one faster.

Worked example: the agent knowledge assistant through the phases

  1. Envision (3 weeks)Agents spend about 2 minutes per call searching for plan, device and roaming information. Hypothesis: an assistant that answers with citations cuts that to 30 seconds. Owner: Head of Contact Center Operations.
  2. Experiment (6 weeks)A proof of concept on 300 real questions answers 91% correctly with citations. Agents in a usability test rate it useful. Decision: proceed, but improve the handling of questions the documents don't answer.
  3. Launch (8 weeks)A production pilot with 40 agents, under the medium-risk review, with guardrails and monitoring. Search time falls to 40 seconds; correct-answer rate in sampled reviews is 97%. Decision: scale, after adding two missing document sets.
  4. Scale (3 months)Rolled out to all 400 agents in four waves. Benefits are tracked monthly. The RAG pattern built here becomes the template for an assistant that answers field engineers from equipment manuals, starting a new cycle.

Iterative transformation moves through envision, experiment, launch and scale (the CAF uses envision, align, launch, scale), with evidence-based decisions at the end of each phase and repeated cycles that build on each other.

A single, multi-year "big bang" AI program planned in full before anything is delivered is the wrong answer. So is endless experimentation with no criteria for moving to launch.

Phase names differ by source: the exam guide says envision, experiment, launch and scale; the AWS CAF says envision, align, launch and scale. If an answer uses the CAF version, align is the phase where stakeholders agree and capability gaps are found.

From short-term wins to enterprise-wide deployment

Skill 4.4.2Scale from short-term wins toward enterprise-wide deployment

Early wins matter for more than their direct value:

  • they prove AI works in this organization;
  • they build trust among sceptical staff;
  • they give leaders evidence to keep funding;
  • they teach the organization how to deliver.

The CAF advises tracking clearly defined metrics and celebrating early wins. The skill is choosing wins that also build toward enterprise scale, rather than one-off successes that lead nowhere.

What makes a good first win

  • Visible value in a measure leaders care about, within a few months.
  • Low to medium risk: internal, with human review, reversible.
  • Data available without major new work.
  • Reusable: it builds a pattern or foundation other use cases can use.
  • Willing users and an engaged business owner.

The operator's after-call summaries meet all five: measurable time saved, agent review of every summary, self-contained call data, a pattern reusable for any summarization task, and a contact-center leader keen to sponsor it.

Scaling by reusable patterns

The fastest way to scale is to recognize that many use cases share the same underlying pattern. Build the pattern once, with its governance, security and monitoring, then reuse it:

Summarize long content for a reviewer

First useAfter-call summaries.

Reused forComplaint summaries for the complaints team; network incident reports for managers.

Answer questions from approved documents, with citations

First useKnowledge assistant for agents.

Reused forEquipment manuals for field engineers; HR policy assistant; IT help desk.

Extract fields from documents

First useIdentity documents and contract forms for new customers.

Reused forSupplier invoices; number-transfer requests from other operators.

Classify and route incoming items

First useCustomer emails.

Reused forField engineers' job notes; internal service tickets.

Each reuse is faster and cheaper than the first, because the security review, guardrail configuration, monitoring and cost tracking already exist. That is how a handful of wins turns into an enterprise portfolio.

Planning in waves

  1. Wave 1 (months 0 to 6): prove itTwo quick wins (after-call summaries, new-customer document extraction) and the minimum foundations: governance, shared platform basics, monitoring.
  2. Wave 2 (months 6 to 12): reuse itApply the proven patterns in more departments (complaint summaries, the field engineer assistant, invoice extraction), each delivered by a business team with CoE support.
  3. Wave 3 (months 12 to 24): differentiateLarger, strategic initiatives that rely on the foundations and skills built so far: predicting network faults from equipment data, then customer-facing services.

Escaping pilot purgatory

Pilots designed as demos, with no production path

FixDesign every pilot on production-grade foundations, with production criteria agreed at the start.

No funding or owner after the pilot

FixFund through to launch, with a business owner committed before the pilot starts.

Governance and security start at the end

FixRisk tier and review path decided in the envision phase.

Every team builds its own stack

FixShared platform and reusable patterns.

Too many pilots at once

FixFewer pilots, chosen for value and reuse, carried through to the end.

Value never measured

FixBaselines and KPIs defined before the pilot.

Partners measure this too. The AWS Generative AI Innovation Center, for example, reports how many of its proofs of concept move to production, which shows that this conversion rate is the measure that matters, not the number of pilots started.

Start with visible, low-risk, reusable quick wins, build their foundations properly, then scale through reusable patterns and planned waves toward larger strategic initiatives.

Launching dozens of disconnected pilots to "see what sticks" usually spreads resources thin and produces few production systems.

AI centers of excellence and collaboration

Skill 4.4.3Establish AI centers of excellence and cross-functional collaboration to support scaling

AI center of excellence (CoE)

A small, cross-functional team that helps the whole organization adopt AI well, by providing shared expertise, standards, platforms and support rather than building everything itself.

A CoE concentrates scarce expertise, sets standards, builds shared foundations and spreads good practice, so each business team doesn't have to work everything out alone. The CAF describes a center of excellence that evangelizes and drives transformation with codified patterns for consistency and scale, and that evolves incrementally as the organization progresses.

What a CoE does

Strategy and portfolio support

ExamplesHelps the steering committee assess and prioritize use cases; tracks the portfolio.

Platform and patterns

ExamplesOwns the shared AI platform and reusable patterns (RAG assistant, extraction, summarization).

Standards and best practices

ExamplesApproved models, evaluation methods, prompt guidelines, release criteria templates.

Enablement

ExamplesTraining, office hours, hackathons, coaching business teams through their first projects.

Governance support

ExamplesHelps teams prepare risk assessments and reviews; maintains the AI inventory with the governance function.

Vendor and partner management

ExamplesEvaluates AI products and partners; negotiates enterprise terms.

Community

ExamplesRuns the champions network and communities of practice; showcases results.

Operating models

Centralized

How it worksThe CoE builds and runs most AI solutions for the business.

StrengthsConsistent quality and control; good when expertise is scarce.

WeaknessesBecomes a bottleneck; distant from business needs.

Hub and spoke (federated)

How it worksA central hub provides platform, standards and expertise; business units have their own AI teams (spokes) that deliver.

StrengthsBalances consistency with speed and business closeness.

WeaknessesNeeds clear division of responsibilities.

Decentralized

How it worksEach business unit runs its own AI with light central coordination.

StrengthsFast and close to the business.

WeaknessesDuplication, inconsistent standards, higher risk.

Organizations often start centralized, when skills are scarce, and move toward hub and spoke as business units build their own capability. That is why the CAF stresses evolving the CoE over time.

The operator's CoE charter at the start of wave 2:

AI CoE charter (summary)
Mission
Help every business unit deliver safe, valuable AI faster, by providing a shared platform, patterns, standards and support
Model
Hub and spoke; spokes in Customer Service and Network Operations; other units supported directly by the hub
Team
CoE lead, 2 ML engineers, 2 platform engineers, 1 AI product manager, 1 responsible AI specialist, 1 enablement lead (8 people)
Owns
AI platform, reusable patterns, approved model list, evaluation standards, AI training curriculum
Does not own
Business outcomes (business owners do); final risk approval (the review board does)
Measures
Time from approved idea to production; share of projects on shared patterns; adoption; business teams' satisfaction with CoE support
Reports to
CTO, with a monthly update to the steering committee

The "does not own" entry matters. A CoE that owns business outcomes takes accountability away from the business. A CoE that holds final approval becomes a gatekeeper that teams avoid.

Collaboration mechanisms

  • A single intake process: any team can propose a use case, with a clear response time.
  • Office hours and embedded experts: CoE members join business teams for their first projects.
  • Communities of practice and champions: people across departments share what works.
  • A shared library: patterns, prompts, evaluation sets and lessons learned that any team can reuse.
  • Regular showcases: teams demonstrate results to each other and to leadership.
  • Joint forums: the CoE, governance, security and business leaders review the portfolio together.

A CoE enables: shared platform, patterns, standards, training and support. It usually evolves from centralized to hub and spoke, while business owners keep accountability for outcomes.

A CoE that insists on building everything itself becomes a bottleneck; one that only writes standards without helping teams deliver becomes an ivory tower that teams bypass.

Continuous feedback and success metrics

Skill 4.4.4Establish continuous feedback and success metrics to track progress and long-term value

Scaling AI is a continuous process, not a project with an end date. AI systems drift, users find new needs, technology improves, and benefits can quietly fade. Continuous feedback keeps each system improving; portfolio metrics show whether the whole program is delivering. The CAF advises making changes stick with continuous feedback mechanisms, and AWS's agentic AI guidance recommends feedback loops from business metrics, such as cost savings, conversion and time to resolution, to drive continuous improvement.

Feedback mechanisms

In-product feedback

What it capturesUsers' judgment of individual outputs.

ExampleThumbs up or down and a comment box on each assistant answer.

Usage data

What it capturesWhat people actually do.

ExampleWhich features are used; where users abandon or override.

Quality sampling

What it capturesExpert judgment of accuracy and safety.

Example200 sampled conversations reviewed weekly.

Champions and surveys

What it capturesExperience, frustrations, ideas.

ExampleMonthly champion session; quarterly staff survey.

Incident and complaint reviews

What it capturesFailures and near misses.

ExampleBlameless reviews feeding the improvement backlog.

Business reviews

What it capturesValue against targets.

ExampleQuarterly review of each system's KPIs with its owner.

Feedback only matters if it changes something. Each mechanism should feed a backlog that is prioritized, acted on and reported back. When users see that their feedback led to a change, they keep giving it.

Portfolio success metrics

Value

MetricBenefits realized against the business case, per system and in total.

Why it mattersShows whether AI is paying off.

Delivery

MetricNumber of systems in production; time from approved idea to production; share of pilots that reach production.

Why it mattersShows whether the organization can scale.

Reuse

MetricShare of new systems built on shared patterns and platform.

Why it mattersShows whether scaling is getting cheaper.

Adoption

MetricActive users; usage per user; acceptance rates.

Why it mattersPredicts value.

Cost

MetricTotal AI spend; cost per transaction per system.

Why it mattersKeeps economics in check.

Risk

MetricIncidents; release criteria breaches; fairness alerts; shadow AI trend.

Why it mattersShows whether scaling stays safe.

People

MetricAI literacy levels; staff sentiment; internal people able to lead AI projects.

Why it mattersShows whether capability is growing.

Tracking long-term value

Benefits should be tracked after launch, not just at the go-live review. Value can decay:

  • Usage falls when the novelty wears off.
  • Drift lowers quality.
  • Saved time gets absorbed into other work.
  • A process change elsewhere makes the AI less relevant.

The operator's after-call summaries saved 80 seconds per call at rollout. A year later, monthly tracking shows 60 seconds, because calls about a newly launched fiber product aren't summarized well. The feedback loop catches it, the prompt and test set are updated, and the saving returns to 80 seconds. When value can't be restored, the system should be retired and its running costs stopped.

Combine system-level feedback (in-product feedback, usage, sampling, champions, incident reviews) with portfolio metrics (value, delivery speed, reuse, adoption, cost, risk, capability), and keep tracking benefits long after launch.

Measuring success by the number of pilots started or employees given access measures activity, not value.

Adoption metrics at a glance

  • active users and frequency of use, against the target population;
  • acceptance rate of AI suggestions and how much they are edited;
  • share of pilots that reach production, and time from idea to production;
  • business KPIs moved by each use case (Task 2.2);
  • falling use of unapproved shadow AI tools as approved options spread.

From experimental to production-grade

Skill 4.4.5Move AI from experimental to production-grade, including governance and operational requirements

A proof of concept answers "can this work?" A production system must work every day, for every user, safely, at scale, for years. The gap between them is large, and underestimating it is one of the main reasons pilots stall.

AreaProof of conceptProduction-grade
DataA one-off extract or sampleLive, governed pipelines with validation and owners
Scale and performanceA few usersPeak volume, response-time targets, capacity and quotas planned
SecuritySandbox accessLeast-privilege access, encryption, private connectivity, security review passed
GovernanceInformalRisk classification, review and approval, release criteria met, documentation, inventory entry
ReliabilityBest effortService targets, fallback process, tested rollback
MonitoringManual checksQuality, safety, fairness, cost and health monitoring with thresholds and owners
Change managementEdits made directlyVersioned models, prompts, guardrails and documents; tested, staged releases
Operations and supportThe buildersA named operating team, runbooks, incident response, support for users
PeopleEnthusiastic testersAll users trained; procedures updated; communication done
CostSmall and untrackedBudget, cost tagging, unit cost tracking, alerts

AWS's guidance on operationalizing agentic AI lists managing the life cycle as a focus area: continuous integration and deployment pipelines, prompt versioning, telemetry and continuous retraining. It also describes organizations building dedicated operations teams for agents (AWS Prescriptive Guidance, Operationalizing agentic AI on AWS).

A production readiness review

Before scaling, a short review confirms each area is in place. The checklist for the operator's knowledge assistant:

Production readiness checklist: knowledge assistant
Governance
  • ✓ Risk tier: medium; review board approval recorded
  • ✓ Release criteria met (accuracy 97%, citations 100%)
  • ✓ Entered in the AI inventory with a business owner
Data and content
  • ✓ Document sets owned; retirement of old versions automated
  • ✓ Document permissions applied in the knowledge base
Security
  • ✓ Security review passed; private connectivity; own keys
  • ✓ Guardrails configured and tested
Operations
  • ✓ Monitoring plan live with thresholds and owners
  • ✓ Runbook and incident process; switch-off tested
  • ✓ Fallback: agents can search the documents directly
  • Open: peak test at new-handset launch volume (scheduled week 2)
People
  • ✓ Training for all 400 agents scheduled by wave
  • ✓ Procedures updated; champions briefed
Cost
  • ✓ Budget and alerts set; application inference profile tagged
Decision
Approved for wave 1; the peak test must pass before wave 2

The review doesn't demand perfection before anything moves. It records what's missing and ties it to a condition, here a peak test before the second wave.

Production-grade AI needs governance approval, live governed data, security, reliability with fallbacks, monitoring, controlled change, an operating team, trained users and cost controls. Plan these from the start of the pilot, not after it.

Scaling a successful proof of concept "as is" to all users skips the work that makes it safe and reliable, and is a common cause of high-profile AI failures.

Factors to manage while scaling

Skill 4.4.6Evaluate the factors throughout scaling that protect business continuity and performance

What works for 40 users in one team can break at 4,000 users across regions. Scale changes the volume, the variety of users and cases, the regulatory picture and the organization's dependence on the system. Several factors need attention throughout, not just at the start:

Business continuity

What changes at scaleMore of the business depends on the AI; an outage stops more work.

How to manage itFallback processes kept practised; phased rollouts; resilience such as cross-Region inference; exit plans for vendors.

Performance and capacity

What changes at scalePeak loads, response times and service quotas.

How to manage itLoad testing at peak volume; capacity and quota planning; the right pricing model for steady load.

Cost

What changes at scaleUsage-based costs grow with every user and request.

How to manage itUnit cost tracking, budgets and alerts, optimization (model size, prompts, caching).

Quality across new populations

What changes at scaleNew teams, case types, languages and regions the system wasn't tested on.

How to manage itRe-evaluate, including fairness, before each expansion; extend test sets.

Data at scale

What changes at scaleMore sources, more pipelines, more ways to break.

How to manage itData contracts, validation, ownership.

Governance load

What changes at scaleMany more systems to review and monitor.

How to manage itRisk tiers, reusable approved patterns, automation of routine checks.

Regulation across regions

What changes at scaleDifferent laws, data residency and language requirements.

How to manage itMap rules per country before launch; regional deployment choices.

People and change

What changes at scaleChange fatigue; many teams at different stages.

How to manage itWave planning, champions in each area, paced communication.

Concentration risk

What changes at scaleHeavy dependence on one model, vendor or key person.

How to manage itCommon interfaces that allow model switching; documentation; skills spread across the team.

Technical debt

What changes at scaleMany one-off solutions that each need maintaining.

How to manage itShared platform and patterns; retire low-value systems.

Model life cycle

What changes at scaleMore systems affected by each model retirement.

How to manage itAn inventory of which systems use which models; planned, tested upgrades.

Worked example: expanding the knowledge assistant to a new country

The operator acquires a smaller operator in another EU country and wants the knowledge assistant for its 120 agents:

  1. Language and contentThe new country's plans and terms are in a different language and use different names. The documents are added, and a new test set of 300 local questions is built with local agents.
  2. Regulation and dataLocal telecom and consumer rules and data residency are checked; data stays in an EU Region where the chosen models are available.
  3. EvaluationAccuracy on local questions is 93% against the 97% release criterion, so prompts and content are improved before launch.
  4. Integration and continuityThe acquired company's billing and customer system needs its own connector. Until it's ready, agents keep their current search tool as the fallback.
  5. PeopleLocal champions are recruited; communication respects local works council consultation requirements.
  6. RolloutOne team first, with rollback triggers agreed.
Rollout gates and rollback triggers
Gate to the next wave (all must hold for 2 weeks)
  • Correct-answer rate in sampled reviews at least 97%
  • Escalations to supervisors no higher than the baseline
  • Agent satisfaction with the assistant at least 4 of 5
  • No unresolved high-severity incidents
Roll back to the previous tool if
  • Correct-answer rate below 94% in any week
  • Any confirmed disclosure of another customer's data
  • Availability below 99% in a week

Throughout scaling, manage business continuity, performance, cost, quality on new populations, data, governance load, regional regulation, people, concentration risk, technical debt and model life cycle, using phased rollouts with gates, practised fallbacks and rollback triggers.

Assuming a system validated in one team, language or country will perform the same everywhere is a classic scaling mistake. Each expansion needs re-evaluation.

Practice questions

Exam-style questions on this task. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
Many pilots succeed but few reach production. What is this called, and what helps?
  1. A. Concept drift; retraining
  2. B. Pilot purgatory; production criteria, owners, funding paths and reusable patterns from the start
  3. C. Shadow AI; banning tools
  4. D. Hallucination; guardrails
Answer
B
Why
Pilot purgatory comes from missing production criteria, ownership and funding; planning for production from the start helps.
Question 2
Scenario
A central AI team sets standards and shared platforms, while business units build their own use cases with embedded specialists. Which model is this?
  1. A. Fully centralized
  2. B. Hub and spoke (federated)
  3. C. Fully decentralized
  4. D. Outsourced
Answer
B
Why
A central hub with spokes in business units is the hub-and-spoke model, combining consistency with speed.
Question 3
Scenario
In the AWS CAF, which phase identifies capability gaps and aligns stakeholders?
  1. A. Envision
  2. B. Align
  3. C. Launch
  4. D. Scale
Answer
B
Why
The CAF phases are envision, align, launch and scale; align is where stakeholders agree and gaps are found. The exam guide uses experiment in place of align.
Question 4
Scenario
What distinguishes a production-grade AI solution from a proof of concept?

Why
Production needs operations, governance and support around the model, not just a working demo.

Key takeaways

  • Transform iteratively through envision, experiment, launch and scale (the CAF uses envision, align, launch, scale), with evidence-based decisions at each phase and repeated cycles that build on each other.
  • Start with visible, low-risk, reusable quick wins; scale through reusable patterns (summarize, answer from documents, extract, classify) and planned waves toward strategic initiatives. Escape pilot purgatory by designing pilots for production, funding through launch and starting governance early.
  • An AI CoE provides platform, patterns, standards, enablement, governance support and community. Models range from centralized to hub and spoke to decentralized; many evolve toward hub and spoke. Business owners keep accountability for outcomes.
  • Use continuous feedback (in-product, usage, sampling, champions, incident and business reviews) feeding an improvement backlog, and portfolio metrics for value, delivery speed, reuse, adoption, cost, risk and capability. Track benefits long after launch; retire systems that no longer deliver.
  • Production-grade AI needs governance approval, governed live data, security, reliability with fallbacks, monitoring, versioned and tested changes, an operating team, trained users and cost controls, planned from the pilot onward.
  • While scaling, manage continuity, performance, cost, quality on new populations, data, governance load, regional rules, people, concentration risk, technical debt and model life cycle, with gated rollouts and rollback triggers.
