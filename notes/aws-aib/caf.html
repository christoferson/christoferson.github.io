What the CAF is

AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (AWS CAF)

AWS's structured guide, built from its experience with thousands of customers, for transforming an organization with the cloud. AWS describes three uses: identify and prioritize transformation opportunities, evaluate and improve cloud readiness, and iteratively evolve the transformation roadmap (AWS whitepaper, An Overview of the AWS Cloud Adoption Framework, November 2021).

The CAF is deliberately about the organization, not the technology. It asks whether leaders, people, governance, platforms, security and operations are ready, because those, not the tools, decide whether a transformation succeeds.

AWS CAF for AI, ML and generative AI (CAF-AI)

A companion framework that applies the CAF to AI (AWS whitepaper, AWS Cloud Adoption Framework for Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, and Generative AI, February 2024). It keeps the CAF's foundational capabilities, enriches many of them with what AI demands, and adds new AI-specific ones. AWS describes it as a mental model, a starting point for mid-term AI planning, and an index of what to consider to "go beyond a single proof of concept".

AWS now labels the CAF-AI whitepaper "for historical reference only": some content may be outdated and some links unavailable. The AWS CAF web page still links to it as the CAF guidance for AI, and its structure is what this page follows. Treat its service references (for example, SageMaker Clarify and Model Monitor, both now closed to new customers) as dated; the principles remain AWS's stated guidance.

The exam guide uses the CAF's ideas directly: readiness across perspectives (Task 4.1), the people side of change (Task 4.3) and the iterative envision-to-scale journey (Task 4.4). This page goes deeper than those tasks need, to show what AWS recommends in full.

The value chain: from capabilities to outcomes

The CAF explains why capabilities matter by working backwards through a chain. CAF-AI describes it in four links:

  1. AI provides new capabilitiesMachine learning lets an organization not only describe the past but also predict the future and prescribe actions; generative AI performs domain tasks with little additional data.
  2. Those capabilities create business outcomesCAF-AI names four, each with an AI example: reduced business risk (detecting faulty parts on a production line), improved environmental, social and governance (ESG) performance (summarizing and flagging environmental compliance reports), growing revenue (personalized product recommendations) and increased operational efficiency (classifying travel receipts to internal booking codes).
  3. Outcomes need transformation in four domainsTechnology, process, organization and product, described below.
  4. The domains depend on foundational capabilitiesGrouped into six perspectives: business, people, governance, platform, security and operations.

The four transformation domains

Technology

Establishing the technical capability for AI, then enabling its use and adoption. Example: a shared platform with approved models, a governed data catalog and monitoring.

Process

Digitizing, automating, optimizing and innovating business operations with AI. Example: an insurer's claims intake or a bank's loan document checks rebuilt around extraction and summaries.

Organization

How business and technology teams orchestrate their efforts to create customer value, driven by AI. Example: product teams that own an AI-enabled service end to end, supported by a center of excellence.

Product

Reimagining the business model with new value propositions and revenue models that use AI. Example: an equipment maker that sells guaranteed uptime instead of machines.

The domains build on each other: technology change enables process change, which enables organizational change, which makes new products possible. CAF-AI's advice for planning follows the chain backwards:

  1. Work backwards from what AI enables you to doStart from the new capabilities, not a technology wish list.
  2. Define the expected business outcomes over timeWhich outcomes, how big, and when.
  3. Carve out the transformation the business must go throughAcross technology, process, organization and product.
  4. Develop the foundational capabilities that enable itThe perspectives and capabilities below.

The journey and the AI flywheel

Four critical elements

CAF-AI says to consider four elements when starting or advancing an AI journey:

Business outcomes: the destination

What you seek to achieve, and the point you work backwards from.

The AI flywheel: the motor

A virtuous cycle in which high-quality data (timely, relevant, valuable and valid) trains or tunes an AI system, the system's predictions improve business outcomes, better outcomes bring more or deeper customer relationships, and those create more and better data. Each turn makes the next easier.

Data and data strategy: what keeps the flywheel moving

Without a deliberate data strategy, the flywheel stalls: outcomes aren't recorded, data stays in silos, and models stop improving.

Foundational capabilities: what decides success or failure

CAF-AI is blunt: these, "above all else", drive success or failure when adopting AI.

A flywheel in practice, at a streaming service:

  1. DataWhat each subscriber watches, finishes and abandons.
  2. ModelA recommendation model trained on that viewing history.
  3. OutcomeBetter recommendations mean more viewing and fewer cancellations.
  4. More dataMore viewing produces more, and more varied, viewing data, which improves the next model.

The four phases

The CAF recommends four iterative, incremental phases, because transforming incrementally demonstrates value quickly while minimizing the need for far-reaching predictions. CAF-AI adds AI-specific emphasis to each:

Envision

CAFShow how the technology will accelerate business outcomes: identify and prioritize transformation opportunities in line with strategic objectives, and attach each to senior stakeholders who can drive change and to measurable outcomes.

For AIAlso identify, this early, which data assets and sources each opportunity relies on: work backwards from the opportunity to its data requirements.

Align

CAFIdentify capability gaps across the six perspectives and cross-organizational dependencies, and surface stakeholder concerns, so readiness can be improved and change management planned.

For AIAI adoption is "a cross-functional effort, much more so" than other technologies, so aligning internally on the goals set in envision is critical for future buy-in.

Launch

CAFDeliver pilots in production that demonstrate incremental business value, and learn from them before scaling.

For AITake pilots from early proofs of concept to production. Choose pilots that are highly impactful and genuinely benefit from AI. Successful or not, they inform the next direction.

Scale

CAFExpand production pilots to the desired scale and make sure the business benefits are realized and sustained.

For AIScale means both the technical capability of a solution and its reach through the business and to customers.

Two pieces of advice run through both documents. First, you don't need every capability at once: evolve capabilities and readiness as you go. Second, recognize the limits of one cycle. CAF-AI warns that trying to do everything in the same cycle "can lead to discouragement in the organization", and recommends pairing a large ambition with many small, measurable steps.

The CAF phases are envision, align, launch, scale. Capability gaps across the six perspectives are identified in align; pilots in production happen in launch. The exam guide's version (envision, experiment, launch, scale) emphasizes the proof-of-concept step.

Capabilities and perspectives

Capability

In CAF terms, "an organizational ability to leverage processes to deploy resources (people, technology, and any other tangible or intangible assets) to achieve a particular outcome". Having data scientists isn't a capability; being able to take a use case from idea to a monitored production model, repeatedly, is.

Perspective

A group of related capabilities owned or managed by a set of stakeholders. The CAF has six, with 47 capabilities between them: business (8), people (7), governance (7), platform (7), security (9) and operations (9).

For each perspective below, every capability is labeled with what CAF-AI does to it:

  • New for AI a capability CAF-AI adds;
  • Enriched for AI an existing CAF capability with AI-specific guidance;
  • Unchanged CAF-AI refers back to the original CAF.

CAF-AI also suggests a natural order for building capabilities within each perspective, and recommends assessing, with experienced implementers, which capabilities already exist and how mature they are.

Business perspective

Makes sure AI investments accelerate the organization's transformation ambitions and business outcomes, and, in CAF-AI's words, makes "AI center-stage" in an AI strategy. Common stakeholders: CEO, CFO, COO, CIO and CTO.

What AWS recommends for AI

Strategy management

Define a business- and customer-centric "north star" for AI, backed by a step-by-step strategy based on tangible or at least aspirational business impact. Work backwards from existing business and customer problems. Design for a data flywheel from the start, and ask whether the data you gather can become a "defensive moat", something rare and costly for competitors to copy. Expect customers' expectations of AI in your products to rise. For each opportunity, decide whether to build, tune or adopt an existing AI system.

Product management

AI products carry uncertainties that ordinary software doesn't, so map the customer's expected value gain to the individual decisions AI will support or automate, and translate each into ML terms. CAF-AI's example: the money saved by detecting fraudulent transactions translates into the precision and recall a fraud classifier must reach. Check the data each solution needs (its volume, velocity, variety and veracity). Design for how users react to probabilistic output, such as failing gracefully when confidence is low. De-risk the ML part with time-bound experiments, and plan from the start how lessons become a production system.

Business insights

Business intelligence is descriptive and diagnostic (what happened, and why); ML adds predictive and prescriptive analytics (what will happen, and what to do). Avoid analytics maturity being siloed in one department, and cross-pollinate mature teams with less mature ones. An analytics center of excellence can deliver early value. Above all, "create a rhythm of using AI to inform major business decisions".

Portfolio management

Show short-term results without sacrificing long-term value: technical proofs of concept focused on irrelevant problems never reach production. Build a layered portfolio where lower layers enable higher ones. CAF-AI's banking example: a bank must classify transactions before it can recommend a next-best offer. Embed the flywheel across the portfolio, decide deliberately what to buy and what to build, and push back on "not invented here" and on analysis paralysis.

Innovation management

AI can disrupt existing value chains, so question long-held market assumptions. Distinguish innovation for cost reduction (process improvements), for revenue and profit (product improvements) and for new income channels (new products). Use domain knowledge captured in your data, accept that some AI products get better only over time, and pair top-down direction with a grassroots movement of internal AI champions.

Generative AI (new)

Foundation models reduce the cost of knowledge work dramatically. CAF-AI frames three choices: build a model from scratch, fine-tune a pre-trained model, or use an existing model without further tuning. Very often the value lies in contextualizing a pre-trained model with domain-specific data, because emergent abilities such as reasoning are costly to create from scratch. The effectiveness of generative AI still rests on the data strategy and flywheel, and CAF-AI notes that "establishing guardrails around generative AI systems is significantly hard".

People perspective

The bridge between AI technology and the business: evolving a culture of continuous growth and learning, where change becomes business as normal. CAF-AI's position is that AI mostly enriches and augments human work rather than replacing it, and that "culture is king, even more so when adopting AI". Common stakeholders: chief human resources officer, CIO, COO, CTO and enterprise-wide leaders.

What AWS recommends for AI

ML fluency (new)

"AI" and "ML" mean different things to different people and carry emotional weight, so first align on what the words mean, and spot the people who are intrigued: they are future champions. Then align practices technically, defining "interface words" between them, such as how success is measured in ML versus in the business. Run fluency and culture training to win buy-in. Finally, think about how AI outputs are communicated to colleagues and customers, who have their own mental models.

Workforce transformation

AI strategy leaders must sit inside the business; that role can seldom be outsourced. Build both technical roles (data scientists, applied scientists, ML engineers) and non-technical ones (ML product managers, ML strategists, evangelists). Match hiring to ambition, move some existing staff into AI roles, and bring in partners early so the agenda doesn't fizzle out. Retention is hard, so plan for attrition. CAF-AI also notes that "a small team of strong practitioners typically outperforms larger teams".

Organizational alignment

An AI center of excellence is a typical first step. Give it short reporting paths to the C-suite and, crucially, incentives tied to business value: "a common mistake is to evolve AI units that do not deliver on business value". Over time, enable the wider organization to use the CoE and existing AI services, avoid rebuilding what the cloud already offers, and build an engineering (MLOps) mindset and a culture of shared data products.

Culture evolution

Ordinary software culture codifies rules; AI culture searches for the inputs that produce the desired output. Work backwards from customer needs. The cornerstones of an AI-first culture: an experimental mindset with agile engineering, collaboration across teams, opportunity discovery both top-down and bottom-up, and solution design driven by customer value. Accept that complex AI work can't be estimated precisely, treat data as the interface between teams, and build an environment where concerns about AI's impact "are not just heard but influence the decision-making process".

Governance perspective

Orchestrates AI initiatives to maximize benefits and minimize risks, with special attention to AI's changing risk profile and cost patterns. Common stakeholders: chief transformation officer, CIO, CTO, CFO, chief data officer and chief risk officer.

CAF-AI recommends starting with a team representing several business units, responsible for four things:

  • defining governance goals, including compliance and ethical goals, and identifying risk areas;
  • developing policies and guidelines on data, transparency, responsible AI and compliance;
  • defining how AI systems are monitored for performance, compliance and bias, with actions at predefined thresholds;
  • continuously revising results and policies to stay aligned with business goals and AI safety.

What AWS recommends for AI

Cloud financial management

AI costs follow a "zig-zag" pattern across the life cycle, which budgets must expect:

  1. Data preparationPossibly high, to build or improve the data; low if the data is already ready.
  2. Proof of conceptUsually modest, but volatile: training large models or constant retraining can quickly become expensive.
  3. Minimum viable productOften high again, to generalize the system and gather edge-case data that users need. Using or fine-tuning an existing foundation model can cut this, because the supplier has absorbed the training cost.
  4. InferenceDriven by request volume and often relatively low; monitor drift, because retraining adds cost.

Tag every AI resource, analyze data, training and inference costs over time, weigh model cost against model performance, and translate business value into AI metrics. CAF-AI also warns of the "often-underestimated cost" of neglecting responsible AI.

Data curation

When a model's accuracy stalls, improving the data is often easier than re-engineering the model. Ask: are we democratizing data, do we treat data as a product, is it discoverable? Balance quality rules so they enable rather than block, name owners or stewards for data sets, and avoid "shadow DataOps". Decide deliberately when to buy external data, create synthetic data, use labeling teams or change business practices to capture data automatically.

Risk management

AI's non-deterministic nature makes risk harder to manage. Some risks are financial: an AI project's outcome can't be guaranteed up front, so factor in sunk cost and control it with proofs of concept, minimum lovable products and minimum viable products. Others are legal and ethical, from local legislation such as the EU's to risks inherent to AI: hidden feedback loops, misread uncalibrated outputs, and unexpected harm to groups of people. Constrain systems with safeguards, stop failures propagating to downstream AI systems, plan for drift, and "don't minimize the complexity of reaching human-level parity".

Responsible use of AI (new)

Address responsible AI early and throughout the life cycle; the broader the impact, the more it matters. Establish an AI governance board spanning research, HR, diversity and inclusion, legal, regulatory affairs, procurement and communications, to oversee ethical development and compliance with AI law. Build in the responsible AI dimensions (explainability, fairness, governance, privacy, security, robustness, transparency), with explainability by design and bias detection. For foundation models, watch for hallucinations, copyright infringement, data leakage and jailbreaks, and ask how the vendor approached responsible AI, because it "trickles down directly into your business case". The cost comes early; it pays off by preventing damage.

Platform perspective

Builds an enterprise-grade, scalable platform both to run AI-enabled services and to develop new custom AI. CAF-AI compares it to manufacturing: the platform should ensure a uniform, consistent product, here an algorithmic result that drives business value. Common stakeholders: CTO, technology leaders, ML operations engineers and data scientists.

What AWS recommends for AI

Platform architecture

First decide whom the platform serves: non-experts consuming ready-made AI services and low-code tools, or experts customizing models with direct infrastructure access. Then design three layers:

  • Compute: training and inference can need very large resources; set standards on price-performance, including specialized AI chips.
  • ML and AI services: ML services let experts train or tune custom models; AI services let teams consume ready models.
  • Consumption: where downstream users meet AI, from a dashboard to prompt engineering or a RAG application.

Build in industry-specific legal requirements as guardrails, plan human-in-the-loop and human-on-the-loop checkpoints, use a modular design, and publish standards centrally.

AI lifecycle management and MLOps (new)

Three components: delivering business results and customer value; building and evolving the solution's technical components; and operating it over time (MLOps, or FMOps for foundation models). Use central repositories and version control for AI artifacts, track model and data lineage, automate deployment, monitoring and retraining, and assess MLOps maturity. CAF-AI names a common failure: data teams "overly focusing on hard ML metrics instead of how these metrics affect a business metric".

Data architecture and data engineering

AI needs large, diverse, high-quality data from many sources, so architectures must make data movement easy. CAF-AI describes three movement patterns: inside-out (from the data lake to specialized stores), outside-in (from application stores into the lake) and around the perimeter (between specialized stores). Treat data as a product, make data sets discoverable and documented, consider zero-ETL approaches that remove copy-and-transform steps, and let AI teams combine sources themselves.

Platform engineering and provisioning

Abstract the heavy lifting for each group: simple connections to AI services, managed ML services, and managed ML infrastructure, because AI teams are "often held back" by owning infrastructure. Automate, embed AI monitoring from the start, and set guardrails so innovation never compromises security or compliance. Offer self-service catalogs of approved AI environments, so a team can start experimenting without waiting for the platform team.

Continuous integration and delivery

Two views: automating model training, evaluation, registration and deployment, including automatic retraining or rollback when performance drifts past a threshold; and using AI itself to improve development, such as code generation and anomaly detection, securely.

Security perspective

Achieves the confidentiality, integrity and availability of data and workloads. CAF-AI relies mostly on the base CAF but extends it to AI-specific attacks. Its organizing idea is that every AI system has three critical components: its inputs, its model and its outputs, each needing protection. Common stakeholders: CISO, chief compliance officer, internal audit, security architects and engineers.

What AWS recommends for AI

Inputs

Inputs can be used to steer a model over time, introduce hidden bias, or inject instructions (prompt injection). Validate inputs, keep data separate from instructions, and give language models only the permissions they need, with no access to system commands. Governance decides who may approve data sources, considering classification, regulated data, provenance and the lawful right to process it; confidential data must not go into a public AI tool.

The model

Threat-model the AI system (CAF-AI points to resources such as MITRE ATLAS and the OWASP Machine Learning Security Top 10), review commercial models' data sources and fine-tuning terms, and watch for drift. Use an author, approver and publisher process for releasing models, and make models fail closed, to a secure state, so a failure doesn't expose data.

Outputs

Long use of a system can let attackers infer its training data (data leakage), so monitor outputs for sensitive data, sanitize generative output before other systems use it, and classify generated data appropriately. For privacy, run privacy impact assessments for regulated data, check the legal basis for training on personal data, and build mechanisms for "right to erasure" requests.

Infrastructure and applications

Secure model endpoints and rate-limit access, encrypt data in transit between environments, isolate training environments, isolate user sessions, and log responses to write-once storage for compliance. In development, run prompt and security tests in the CI/CD pipeline, keep an inventory of models with named technical and business owners, back up known-good models and test recovery. Avoid training on sensitive data where possible; tokenize it instead.

Operations perspective

Makes sure AI workloads are delivered at the level the business needs, kept operational, and keep creating value reliably. Much of AI operations is covered by AI lifecycle management and MLOps (platform perspective); this perspective adds incidents and performance. Common stakeholders: infrastructure and operations leaders, ML operations engineers, site reliability engineers and IT service managers.

What AWS recommends for AI

Incident and problem management

AI systems are hard to understand fully, so they are observed through proxies and simplified statistics. CAF-AI's key line: AI systems "get validated but never verified", so they need constant observation. Watch for training-serving skew (a system performing very differently in production than in the lab), drift and rare events. Let users flag wrong results and report incidents directly, design graceful failure, and remember that the people for whom the system works badly are often missing from the data. "Expect such incidents to occur and be suspicious if none are reported."

Performance and capacity

AI workloads change shape over the life cycle: sporadic, heavy experiments early on; more predictable training and preprocessing later; then inference optimized for latency, throughput and cost. Use the cloud's elasticity for the early, uneven demand, keep clear lines between development and production, and keep model artifacts and data available for fallback.

Using the CAF in practice

A regional parcel delivery company wants AI to predict delivery delays and to answer customers' "where is my parcel?" questions automatically. Used as an assessment, the CAF turns that ambition into a gap list by perspective, in the align phase:

Business

The objectives exist (cut "where is my parcel?" calls by 40%; warn customers of delays before they ask), but there is no portfolio view: three departments each run their own chatbot pilot. Action: one prioritized portfolio, with delay prediction as the lower layer that the customer assistant builds on.

People

Drivers fear that delay prediction will be used to rate them, and managers use "AI" to mean anything from route software to chatbots. Action: ML fluency sessions with shared definitions, and an honest statement on how delay data will and won't be used.

Governance

No owner for scan and route data, no risk tiers, no responsible AI review. Action: data owners named, a governance team, and a tiered review before anything reaches customers.

Platform

Scan events sit in the depot systems and route plans in a separate planning tool. Action: a governed data product joining scans, routes and weather, and a shared platform instead of three pilot stacks.

Security

The pilots send customer addresses to a public chatbot. Action: approved models only, with inputs validated and outputs checked for personal data.

Operations

No monitoring or incident process for models. Action: delay-prediction accuracy tracked against actual delivery times, with a way for customers to report wrong estimates.

The company then launches delay prediction in one region, measured against actual delivery times, and scales it region by region before the customer assistant uses it. More deliveries mean more scan data and better predictions: the flywheel.

Exam wording that maps to the CAF: "identify capability gaps across the organization" is align; "readiness across business, people, governance, platform, security and operations" is the six perspectives; "data that improves the model, which improves outcomes, which brings more data" is the AI flywheel.

Treating the CAF as a technology checklist misses its point. Three of its six perspectives (business, people, governance) are about the organization, and CAF-AI says foundational capabilities, not tools, decide success or failure.

Key takeaways

  • The AWS CAF (2021) guides transformation through foundational capabilities; CAF-AI (2024, now marked historical but still linked by AWS) enriches many of them for AI and adds new ones.
  • The value chain runs from capabilities to four transformation domains (technology, process, organization, product) to four outcomes (reduced risk, better ESG, revenue growth, operational efficiency). Plan it backwards.
  • The journey has four iterative phases: envision (opportunities, outcomes and, for AI, data needs), align (capability gaps across perspectives), launch (pilots in production) and scale (sustained value). Don't try to do everything in one cycle.
  • Four elements drive an AI journey: business outcomes, the AI flywheel, data strategy and foundational capabilities.
  • Six perspectives hold 47 capabilities. New for AI: generative AI (business), ML fluency (people), responsible use of AI (governance), AI lifecycle management and MLOps (platform).
  • Distinctive AI guidance: build, tune or adopt per opportunity; layered portfolios; CoEs tied to business value; zig-zag AI costs; improve data before models; inputs, model and outputs as the three things to secure; AI systems are "validated but never verified".
