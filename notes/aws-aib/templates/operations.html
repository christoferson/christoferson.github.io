Monitoring plan entry

A monitoring plan lists what is watched once an AI system is live, the threshold that counts as a problem, how often it is checked, who owns it and what happens next. It is the difference between a dashboard and a control.

AI systems degrade quietly: data drifts, documents go stale, new kinds of questions arrive. A metric with no threshold, owner or response is just a chart nobody looks at. Each entry should be something a named person will act on.

Who owns itThe business owner, with the operating team that runs the system.

WhenWritten before launch, as part of production readiness; reviewed as incidents and drift teach you what matters.

AI incident runbook

The incident runbook is what to do when the AI gets it wrong at scale: detect, contain, assess who was affected, notify, fix the cause and correct affected decisions, then learn. It is written in advance, because during an incident nobody should be deciding who can switch the system off.

Two things make it work: a switch-off and fallback that have actually been tested, and logs good enough to identify every affected customer. Without logs, a company can know something went wrong but not to whom.

Who owns itThe business owner, with the on-call operating team; legal decides on notifications.

WhenBefore launch; exercised in drills and updated after every real incident.

UsePrepared and rehearsed before go-live, for each production AI system.

How to fill itExactly how to switch off or narrow the system, who may decide, and the target time.

Common mistakeA switch-off that has never been tested.

Fallback

How to fill itThe process staff use while the AI is off.

Common mistakeNo fallback, so the business stops.

Assess

How to fill itWhere logs are and who can use them to find affected people.

Common mistakeLogs not kept, or kept but inaccessible.

How to fill itLegal's role, and who else is told.

Common mistakeNotifying too late, or deciding legal duties without legal.

How to fill itA blameless review with actions and owners.

Production readiness checklist

A proof of concept answers "can this work?"; production must work every day, for every user, safely. The production readiness checklist confirms the gap has been closed: governance approval, live governed data, security, fallbacks, monitoring, change control, an operating team, trained users and cost controls.

It doesn't demand perfection before anything moves. Its job is to make gaps visible and attach them to conditions, such as "the peak test must pass before the second wave", so scaling proceeds with known, owned risks rather than surprises.

Who owns itThe review board or change advisory board, with the delivery team presenting evidence.

WhenBefore the first production release and before each major scaling wave.

Escalation criteria

Escalation criteria say exactly when an AI assistant or agent must hand over to a person: when the customer asks, when the topic is a decision reserved for people, when safeguards block an answer, when someone is vulnerable, or when an action exceeds a limit.

Writing them down lets the system enforce them consistently and lets staff know what is coming to them, with what context. They are where human oversight becomes concrete: not "a human reviews it", but which cases, how fast and with what information.

Who owns itThe business owner, with customer experience and risk.

WhenDuring design; tuned from handover data after launch.

RelatedTask 3.1: human oversight and safeguards · Incident runbook

UseFor any assistant or agent: when the AI must hand over to a person.

Escalation criteria
Hand over to a person when
  • the user asks for a person
  • the topic is a decision reserved for people (for example, loan rejections)
  • safeguards block an answer a set number of times (for example, twice) in one conversation
  • confidence is below a set threshold
  • the user shows signs of vulnerability or makes a complaint
  • identity can't be verified
  • an action exceeds a limit (for example, $40 a day)
  • there is no resolution after a set number of turns (for example, 10)
Hand over with
Summary, verified identity, documents
Target time to a person
For example, under 2 minutes during opening hours

How to fill it

Triggers

How to fill itSpecific, detectable conditions the system can enforce.

Common mistakeVague triggers like "when needed"

Reserved decisions

How to fill itTopics that always go to people because of impact or law.

Common mistakeLetting the AI decide anything it can.

Vulnerability

How to fill itWords and situations that signal extra care is needed.

Common mistakeTreating every customer the same.

Handover content

How to fill itWhat the person receives, so the customer doesn't repeat themselves.

Common mistakeHandovers that start from zero.

Target time

How to fill itHow fast a person must take over.

Common mistakeEscalation into a long queue.

Filled example

Escalation criteria: bank's website assistant
Hand over to a staff member when
  • the customer asks for a person, at any point
  • the question concerns a loan decision, a disputed payment or a refund
  • the grounding check blocks an answer twice in one conversation
  • the customer mentions bereavement, illness, financial hardship or a complaint
  • the customer's identity can't be verified
  • there is no resolution after 10 turns
Hand over with
Conversation summary, verified identity, documents already provided
Target time to a person
Under 2 minutes, 08:00 to 20:00; a callback offered outside those hours
