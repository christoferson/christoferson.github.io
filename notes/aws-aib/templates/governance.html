Governance and risk

AI policy (outline)

The AI policy is the organization's rulebook for AI: the principles it commits to, what it will never do, how risk is classified and reviewed, who decides, which tools and data are allowed, and where people must stay in control. Every other governance document hangs off it.

A policy matters most for what it makes unnecessary. When the rules are written down, a team proposing a low-risk tool doesn't need a meeting; it checks the policy. When they aren't, every decision becomes a negotiation, approvals depend on who you ask, and staff turn to unapproved tools. Keep it short, and put the detail in standards it points to.

Who owns itApproved by the steering committee or board; maintained by a named policy owner, often the chief risk officer.

WhenBefore the first customer-facing AI; reviewed at least yearly and when laws change.

RelatedTask 3.2: governance structures · Task 3.2: compliance risks · AI tool register · Risk classification

UseAs the organization's top-level statement on AI, approved by the steering committee or board.

How to fill it

Purpose and scope

How to fill itWhich AI it covers (built, bought and embedded features), which people (staff, contractors) and which entities.

Common mistakeCovering only systems built in-house.

Principles

How to fill itThe responsible AI dimensions the organization commits to, in plain language.

Common mistakePrinciples with no link to any process.

Prohibited uses

How to fill itSpecific uses that are never allowed, including legal prohibitions.

Common mistakeLeaving this empty or vague.

Risk tiers

How to fill itDefinitions of each tier and the review, approval and monitoring each needs.

Common mistakeOne process for every use.

Roles

How to fill itWho decides what: steering committee, review board, CoE, owners.

Common mistakeUnclear decision rights.

Data rules

How to fill itWhich data classifications may be used in which kinds of tools.

Common mistakeReferring to a data policy staff can't find.

Oversight and transparency

How to fill itWhere people must decide or review; when customers and staff are told AI is involved.

Common mistakePromising human review that isn't resourced.

Review

How to fill itAn owner and a review cycle of at least once a year.

Common mistakeA policy that never changes while AI does.

AI tool policy and register entry

The tool register is the answer to "which AI tools may I use, and with what data?" Each entry records a tool's status (approved, under evaluation or blocked), its scope in the Generative AI Security Scoping Matrix, the data it may handle, and the reason for the decision.

It is the most practical defense against shadow AI. Banning AI outright drives use underground; a register with a fast request process and a named alternative for every blocked tool gives staff a legitimate path, and gives security and legal a single place to see what is in use.

Who owns itThe AI governance function or center of excellence, with security and legal for each decision.

WhenContinuously: new requests, and scheduled reviews of every entry.

Governance charter (AI steering committee)

A governance charter sets up the AI steering committee: why it exists, who sits on it, what it decides, what it delegates and how often it meets. It turns "leadership oversight of AI" from a phrase into a body with a quorum and decision records.

The delegation lines matter as much as the decisions. A committee that must approve every AI use becomes a bottleneck; one that delegates medium risk to a review board and low risk to registered business owners keeps attention on what really needs it.

Who owns itThe executive sponsor, who usually chairs the committee; approved by the board or executive team.

WhenWhen the AI program is formed; revisited as the portfolio grows.

Purpose

How to fill itWhat the committee directs and decides, in one or two lines.

Common mistakeA purpose so broad it overlaps every other body.

Chair and members

How to fill itThe executive sponsor as chair; every function with a stake, including legal, risk, privacy, security and HR.

Common mistakeOnly technology leaders.

Decides and delegates

How to fill itWhat it decides itself, and what it delegates to the review board and owners.

Common mistakeApproving every AI use itself, which creates a bottleneck.

Inputs and outputs

How to fill itThe reports it receives; the decision records it produces.

Common mistakeMeetings with no recorded decisions.

How to fill itHow often it meets and how it reports to the board.

Escalation

Risk classification questionnaire

The risk classification questionnaire decides how much governance an AI use needs. A few questions (effect on people, autonomy, data sensitivity, audience, scale, reversibility, regulation) place each use in a tier, and the tier sets the review path, the documentation and the monitoring.

It is what makes oversight proportional. Without tiers, either everything gets the heavy review and useful work stalls, or nothing does and real risks slip through. Classify early, so a team knows its review path before it builds, and re-classify whenever the use changes.

Who owns itThe business owner completes it; the governance function confirms the tier.

WhenAt the idea stage, and again whenever data, audience, autonomy, country or law changes.

RelatedTask 3.2: risk classification · Release criteria · AI policy

UseAt the idea stage, and again whenever the system changes. The highest-risk answer usually sets the tier.

Risk classification questionnaire
1. Effect on people
Could output affect someone's finances, health, job, rights or safety? No, some or significant
2. Autonomy
Does the AI suggest, act with approval, or act alone?
3. Data
Public, internal, personal or special-category?
4. Audience
Internal staff, or customers and the public?
5. Scale
Decisions or interactions per day
6. Reversibility
Are errors easy to spot and undo?
7. Regulation
Named in AI-specific law (for example, EU AI Act high-risk) or in a closely regulated sector?
8. Prohibited
Does it match any prohibited use?
Tier
Prohibited, high, medium or low
Review path
The review the tier requires
Re-classify if
New data, customer-facing use, more autonomy, a new country, a new law

How to fill it

Effect on people

How to fill itConsider everyone affected by the output, not just users.

Common mistakeAnswering only for the user.

Autonomy

How to fill itWhat the system can do without a person approving.

Common mistakeIgnoring planned increases in autonomy.

Data and audience

How to fill itThe most sensitive data involved; internal or public.

Common mistakeAssuming internal tools carry no data risk.

Scale and reversibility

How to fill itVolume of decisions, and how easily errors are found and undone.

Common mistakeIgnoring scale when each error seems small.

Regulation

How to fill itCheck AI-specific law (for example, EU AI Act high-risk areas) and sector rules.

Common mistakeAssuming no AI law means no regulation.

Tier

How to fill itThe highest-risk answer usually sets the tier; record the reasoning.

Common mistakeAveraging answers to get a lower tier.

Re-classification triggers

How to fill itThe changes that would move the tier.

Common mistakeClassifying once and never again.

Filled example

Risk classification: card fraud scoring model
1. Effect on people
Significant: blocks genuine payments at the checkout
2. Autonomy
Acts: blocks the payment automatically; the customer can release it by confirming a text message
3. Data
Personal and financial data
4. Audience
Internal model, but every decision affects a customer
5. Scale
About 1.5 million card payments a day
6. Reversibility
Partly; a block can be released, but a missed purchase can't be undone
7. Regulation
Banking and anti-discrimination rules apply; the EU AI Act's credit-scoring category excludes fraud detection
8. Prohibited
No
Tier
High (the bank's own tier, stricter than the law)
Reason
Significant effect on customers at scale, with a fairness risk across regions
Review path
Full review, impact assessment, steering committee approval
Re-classify if
New data sources, closing accounts without review, expansion to new countries

What makes this example strong:

  • It considers customers affected by every automatic block, not just the fraud analysts who use the model.
  • It records why the EU AI Act category doesn't apply.
  • It still classifies as high on impact and scale: the bank's own tier is stricter than the law.

Release criteria

Release criteria are the tests a system must pass before it goes live: measurable thresholds for accuracy, fairness, privacy, safety, explainability, controllability and transparency. They turn responsible AI principles into pass-or-fail checks that everyone agreed on before building started.

Setting them up front is the point. Criteria written after the system is built tend to describe what was built; criteria written first shape what gets built, and give a clear answer when launch pressure arrives: narrow the scope, fix it or wait.

Who owns itThe business owner and the responsible AI review board; measured by the delivery team.

WhenDuring planning, before building; tested at the release decision and monitored afterwards.

Vendor evaluation questionnaire

The vendor questionnaire is due diligence for buying AI: evidence on your own data, how your data is used, what happens when the vendor's models change, certifications, IP ownership and indemnity, security, responsible AI, cost at scale and how you exit.

Demos are designed to impress; the questionnaire is designed to find what the demo didn't show. Recording an assessment beside each answer (acceptable, condition, gap or deal-breaker) makes the buy decision traceable, and the conditions become contract terms.

Who owns itProcurement with the business owner, legal, security and the AI center of excellence.

WhenBefore buying or partnering; repeated at renewal.

How to fill it

Evidence

How to fill itAsk for results on your own data, or a trial that produces them.

Common mistakeAccepting results from another customer's data.

Data use

How to fill itGet the answer in the contract, not only in marketing material.

Common mistakeRelying on a sales statement.

Models

How to fill itWhich models, how changes are notified, and retesting support.

Common mistakeIgnoring model changes and retirements.

Compliance

How to fill itCertificates with their scope, and audit reports.

Common mistakeA logo on a website as evidence.

IP

How to fill itOwnership of outputs and custom models; indemnity terms.

Common mistakeAssuming you own whatever is generated.

Cost and exit

How to fill itCost at three times today's volume; how to leave with your data.

Common mistakeComparing first-year prices only.

Assessment

How to fill itFor each area: acceptable, acceptable with conditions, or a deal-breaker.

Common mistakeCollecting answers without judging them.

Filled example

Vendor questionnaire: AI credit decisions for a bank
Evidence
Answer: "20% fewer defaults" at another bank. Assessment: not acceptable until tested on our applicants in a 3-month trial.
Data use
Answer: our data improves their shared model; opting out costs extra. Assessment: condition, opt-out required in the contract.
Models
Answer: model updated quarterly, without notice. Assessment: condition, 60 days' notice and retest results before each change.
Compliance
Answer: security certificate; no AI management certificate; limited documentation for deployers. Assessment: gap, high-risk obligations need full documentation.
Explainability
Answer: overall factors only, no per-decision explanation. Assessment: deal-breaker for declined applicants.
Exit
Answer: data export on request; 12 months' notice. Assessment: acceptable.
Decision
Do not buy; build an explainable model with a specialist partner.

What makes this example strong:

  • Each answer is judged, not just recorded.
  • The decisive issue (no per-decision explanations for a high-risk use) is clearly marked as a deal-breaker.
