Task 2.3: Position AI for competitive advantage

Using AI to compete, not just to keep up: reading the competitive landscape, spotting where AI can change how a business makes money, telling lasting advantages from improvements every competitor will soon have, and deciding how much to invest given how mature the industry is and how fast rivals are moving.

Overview

Task 2.3

Position AI for competitive advantage.

Choosing initiatives that meet the organization's own objectives is only half of an AI strategy. The other half looks outward. How are competitors and new entrants using AI? Where could AI change the rules of the industry? Which AI investments create an edge rivals can't easily copy? And how much should the organization spend given the pace of its market?

One fact shapes all of these answers. The same foundation models are available to every company, often through the same cloud services. AWS's research with 128 executives across 27 countries and 23 industries concluded that, since everyone can access the same models, advantage comes from proprietary data, including the undocumented knowledge of employees (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Announcing the new AWS Reimagine report on AI, September 2026). Access to AI is not an advantage. What an organization uniquely brings to AI is.

Examples use Harbor Mutual, a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees, 1.2 million policyholders and a 400-agent contact center, plus examples from other industries.

Assessing the competitive landscape

Skill 2.3.1

A competitive assessment answers three questions: where AI is changing what customers expect, who is using it to win business, and where the organization stands compared with them. Without one, AI plans either copy competitors' last moves or ignore threats until customers start leaving.

Who to look at

Source of pressureWhat to watchHarbor Mutual example
Direct competitorsNew AI-powered features, service speed, pricing changesA rival now pays simple car claims within hours from photos submitted in its app
New entrantsStart-ups built around AI from day one, with lower costsA digital insurer prices car policies from driving data collected by a phone app
CustomersRising expectations set by other industriesCustomers used to instant answers from retailers expect the same from their insurer
Intermediaries and channelsWho stands between you and the customer, and how they chooseComparison sites, and AI assistants that compare and buy policies on a customer's behalf
Suppliers and partnersAI changing the cost or quality of what you buyRepair networks using AI to estimate damage, which could lower claim costs
RegulatorsRules that enable or limit AI usesRules on automated decisions and on which data may be used in pricing

How to gather the evidence

  • Experience competitors as a customer: file a test claim, ask for a quote, contact their support.
  • Public signals: annual reports, investor presentations, press releases, product launches.
  • Hiring patterns: a competitor recruiting dozens of ML engineers signals serious investment.
  • Customer and partner feedback: why customers leave, what brokers and repair shops say.
  • Industry studies and analysts: adoption surveys show how mature AI use is across the industry.

Worked example: Harbor Mutual's assessment

AreaMarket positionHarbor Mutual todayImplication
Claims speedLeaders settle simple claims in hours12 days on averageFalling behind on a factor customers notice; must close the gap
Contact-center serviceAI-assisted agents and self-service are commonManual notes, no self-serviceCatch up with proven tools; little advantage to gain, but a real cost to falling behind
Fraud detectionMost insurers use some MLOne older model and 400 rules; 25 years of investigated claimsStrong data position; an opportunity to lead
PricingNew entrants use driving and property dataTraditional rating factorsA threat in the youngest, most digital customer segments

The assessment separates two kinds of response. Some areas need parity: matching the market so as not to lose customers (claims speed, contact-center service). Others offer advantage: where Harbor Mutual has something rivals don't (25 years of fraud data).

Advantages of adopting AI

AdvantageHow AI creates itExample
Lower cost to serveAutomates or speeds up routine workClaims processed with fewer manual steps
SpeedDecisions in minutes instead of daysSame-day claim decisions
Better risk decisionsMore accurate predictionsFewer fraudulent payouts; more accurate pricing
Personalization at scaleTailors offers and service to each customerRenewal offers matched to each customer's situation
New products and servicesMakes previously uneconomic offerings possiblePrevention services for homeowners (see Skill 2.3.2)
Better employee experienceRemoves tedious workAgents spend less time on notes, more with customers

Moving first or following fast

  • First mover: gains a head start in data, customer relationships and learning, but pays to learn what doesn't work and takes more technology and regulatory risk.
  • Fast follower: lets others prove the approach, then adopts quickly with lower risk, but gives up any head start and may face customers already won by the leader.

Moving first pays off where an early lead compounds, for example where more usage produces more data, which produces better results. Following fast suits capabilities that will soon be available to everyone, such as standard office AI tools.

A good competitive assessment looks beyond direct competitors (new entrants, customer expectations, intermediaries, regulators) and separates areas where AI is needed for parity from areas where it can create advantage.

Copying a competitor's AI feature without asking whether it fits your strategy and data rarely creates advantage. At best it restores parity.

Transforming business models with AI

Skill 2.3.2

Most AI work improves the existing business: the same products, delivered faster or cheaper. Business model transformation changes how the business creates and captures value: what it offers, to whom, through which channel and how it gets paid. It is rarer, riskier and potentially far more valuable.

A business model can be described by a few elements, and AI can change any of them:

ElementQuestionHow AI can change it
Value propositionWhat problem do we solve for customers?From paying after a loss to helping prevent the loss
Customer segmentsWhom do we serve?Serving customers who were too costly or risky to serve before
ChannelsHow do we reach customers?Through AI assistants and agents acting for customers
Revenue modelHow do we get paid?Usage-based, subscription or outcome-based pricing
Cost structureWhat does it cost to deliver?Much lower cost per transaction, so new price points work

Patterns of AI-enabled business model change

PatternWhat changesExample
From product to serviceSell an outcome instead of a thingA machine maker uses failure prediction to sell guaranteed uptime per hour instead of machines
From reacting to preventingUse prediction to stop problems before they happenAn insurer offers leak detection that alerts homeowners before a pipe bursts
Usage-based pricingPrice according to measured behavior or useCar insurance priced by how and how much people drive
Mass personalizationProducts tailored to each customer at no extra costA retailer generates personalized product bundles and descriptions
Selling a capability externallyAn internal AI capability becomes a product for othersA bank sells its fraud-detection service to smaller banks
Platforms and ecosystemsCoordinate partners and customers around your data and servicesAn insurer's platform links customers with approved repairers and contractors

AWS's guidance on agentic AI describes the same opportunities. Beyond immediate efficiency gains, agents can unlock longer-term growth by combining agent services into industry-specific solutions, by network effects in multi-agent ecosystems, and by market extension: new revenue from agent-enabled services that outside customers can use. AWS Marketplace is one channel for publishing agentic solutions as commercial products (AWS Prescriptive Guidance, Operationalizing agentic AI on AWS, focus area 6).

A new channel: AI agents as buyers

AI agents are starting to research and buy on people's behalf. AWS's retail strategists argue that retailers are moving from competing for human attention to competing for "algorithmic inclusion". When an agent makes the purchase, what decides visibility is structured, accurate product data, real-time availability and reliable fulfilment, not marketing. They expect the same dynamic in travel, financial services and procurement (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Experience, exploration, execution: the three channels reshaping retail, May 2026).

For Harbor Mutual, this means a customer's AI assistant may soon compare insurance quotes. If Harbor Mutual's coverage details and prices can't be read reliably by machines, it may simply not appear in the comparison.

Worked example: a prevention service

Water damage is Harbor Mutual's most expensive home-claim category. The idea is a "Home Protect" add-on: smart water sensors installed in customers' homes, with an AI model that recognizes leak patterns and alerts the homeowner, or shuts off the water automatically.

ElementTodayWith Home Protect
Value propositionPays for damage after it happensPrevents most of the damage
RevenueAnnual premiumPremium plus a monthly service fee, or a premium discount that pays for the sensors
Customer relationshipContact once a year at renewal, and at claim timeOngoing, helpful contact
DataClaims after the factLive sensor data that improves the model and pricing over time
PartnersRepair contractorsSensor manufacturer, installers, plumbers

Questions to answer before investing:

  • Value: how much would prevented losses save compared with the cost of sensors and service? Would customers pay, or stay longer?
  • Feasibility: is the leak model accurate enough, and can installation work at scale?
  • Risk: privacy of in-home data, liability if a leak is missed, regulator views on linking discounts to monitoring.
  • Fit: does the brand and the distribution network support a service business?

The sensible next step is a small paid pilot with a few thousand volunteer customers, with success criteria agreed in advance, rather than a full launch.

Business model transformation changes how value is created and captured (offering, customers, channel, revenue model). Doing the same work more cheaply is an operational improvement, not a new business model.

Launching a new AI-based business model at full scale without a pilot ignores the extra risk it carries: unproven demand, new partners, new regulatory questions and possible conflict with existing channels.

Sustainable competitive advantage and operational improvement

Skill 2.3.3

An advantage is sustainable when competitors can't easily copy it. That is the crux for AI. Anything a company can buy, a competitor can buy too. When every insurer adopts the same AI note-taking tool, it stops being an advantage and becomes table stakes: necessary to stay in the game, but not a way to win.

Operational improvement versus advantage

TypeDescriptionExampleValue
Operational improvement (parity)Doing existing work faster or cheaper with widely available toolsA purchased AI assistant for contact-center notesReal savings, but competitors will match it; needed to avoid falling behind
Sustainable advantageA capability built on something unique to the organizationA fraud model trained on 25 years of investigated claims and improved daily by investigator feedbackHard to copy and can grow over time

Both matter. Operational improvements fund the business and prevent decline. A strategy made only of operational improvements, though, leaves the organization exactly level with its rivals.

Sources of lasting advantage

SourceWhy it's hard to copyHarbor Mutual example
Proprietary dataCompetitors can't buy your history, your customers' behavior or your experts' judgments25 years of claims with investigation outcomes; adjusters' notes
Feedback loops (data flywheel)Each use generates data that improves the AI, so the lead grows with timeEvery investigated claim becomes a new training example
AI embedded in redesigned processesA redesigned process involves people, systems, rules and culture together, which takes years to copyClaims handling rebuilt around AI triage, not AI added to the old steps
Organizational capabilitySkills, experience and ways of working accumulate slowlyTeams that can take a use case from idea to production in weeks
Trust and reputationBuilt over years and lost quicklyKnown for fair, explainable decisions and responsible use of customer data
Ecosystem positionPartners and customers connected to your platform create network effectsA repair network that prefers Harbor Mutual's fast, AI-supported approvals

AWS's enterprise strategists put the data point directly: "At AWS we say that data is your differentiator." Models, open-source software and cloud services are available to everyone. When an organization adds its own data and embeds AI into its business processes, the result is differentiated (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, True data-centricity, February 2026).

The data flywheel

A flywheel is a self-reinforcing loop: once it turns, each cycle makes the next easier. For Harbor Mutual's fraud detection:

  1. Better modelThe fraud model flags suspicious claims more accurately.
  2. Better investigationsInvestigators spend their time on the right claims and confirm more fraud.
  3. More labeled dataEvery investigation outcome, fraud or not, is recorded as a training example.
  4. RetrainingThe model learns from new schemes as soon as they're confirmed.
  5. Lower losses, better pricesLower fraud losses allow more competitive premiums, attracting more customers and more claims data. The loop starts again.

A competitor buying an off-the-shelf fraud tool starts without that history and without the loop. The flywheel only turns, however, if outcomes are actually recorded. That is why data quality and the discipline of recording outcomes are strategic, not administrative.

Why the process matters more than the tool

AWS's research found that the organizations generating value from AI fix the process around AI, not just the AI. In one AWS example, AI cut the effort of rebuilding a service from 30 engineers for a year to 5 engineers in 70 days, but delivery didn't speed up because security review, deployment and interface review were still the bottleneck. The cost of doing the work fell sharply; the cost of deciding, funding, governing and approving didn't. The same research found that nearly half of the interviews describing efficiency gains showed no evidence of business impact, because no one owned turning the saved time into results (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Announcing the new AWS Reimagine report on AI, September 2026).

For Harbor Mutual: an AI that writes claim summaries in seconds doesn't speed up settlement if every claim still waits four days for a second approval. Advantage comes from redesigning the whole flow (approval limits, routing, staffing, oversight proportional to risk) and naming an owner who turns the time saved into faster settlements.

Testing whether an advantage will last

Four questions, applied to any proposed AI advantage:

  1. Valuable?Does it improve something customers or the business care about?
  2. Rare?Do few competitors have it?
  3. Hard to imitate?Does it rest on data, processes or relationships others can't buy?
  4. Organized to capture it?Do we have the owners, processes and skills to turn it into results?

A purchased AI writing tool passes the first test and fails the next two. Harbor Mutual's fraud flywheel can pass all four, provided outcome recording is enforced.

Sustainable AI advantage comes from what competitors can't buy: proprietary data, feedback loops, AI embedded in redesigned processes, organizational capability and trust. Models and tools alone are available to everyone.

"Using the most advanced model" isn't a sustainable advantage. Competitors can use the same model tomorrow. Likewise, efficiency gains that aren't tied to an owner and a business result often vanish into absorbed capacity.

Setting AI investment levels

Skill 2.3.4

How much to invest in AI, and in what kind of AI, depends on the industry and the competition, not on a fixed percentage. Investing too little in a fast-moving market risks losing customers to rivals who move first. Investing too much too early, in a market that isn't ready, burns money on capabilities customers don't yet value.

Factors that set the level

FactorPushes investment upPushes investment down or later
Industry AI maturityAI is becoming standard; laggards visibly lose customersAI use is early and unproven in this industry
Competitive pressureRivals or new entrants are winning business with AICompetitors are not moving; customers aren't asking
Strategic importanceAI affects the core of how the business competesAI touches only support functions
Data advantageThe organization holds unique data that AI can exploitNo distinctive data; generic tools will do
Organizational readinessData, skills and sponsorship are in placeFoundations are missing; invest in them first
Regulatory and risk environmentClear rules make investment saferUncertain rules on key uses; invest cautiously and keep options open
Cost of waitingLeaders' head start compounds (data, customers)Capabilities are getting cheaper and easier to adopt later

Investment postures

PostureWhen it fitsWhat investment looks like
Monitor and experimentLow industry maturity, little competitive pressure, uncertain valueSmall budget for learning: pilots, training, buying ready-made tools
Adopt (fast follower)AI becoming standard in the industry; parity neededModerate budget, mostly buying proven solutions, quick rollout
DifferentiateCompetitive pressure plus a distinctive data or process advantageSignificant budget to build on proprietary data, with platforms and skills to match
TransformAI is reshaping the industry's business modelsLarge, staged investments in new business models, alongside protecting the core

A single organization usually holds different postures in different areas, depending on where it needs parity and where it can win.

Building a portfolio

A widely used way to balance investment is to split the budget across three horizons. A commonly cited starting split from innovation management is about 70% core, 20% adjacent and 10% transformational, adjusted to the industry's pace:

  • Core: improve the existing business: efficiency, parity, quick wins.
  • Adjacent: extend into nearby areas: new customer segments, differentiating capabilities.
  • Transformational: new business models and bets on the future.

Faster-moving markets shift weight toward the later horizons.

Worked example: Harbor Mutual's two-year AI budget

Leadership has $5 million for AI over two years. AI use for claims and customer service is now mainstream in its market, competitors are moving on claims speed, and Harbor Mutual has an unusual data asset in fraud.

HorizonShareAmountInitiativesPosture
Core60%$3.0 millionAfter-call summaries, repair estimate extraction, claims triage, plus the data and governance foundationsAdopt: close the parity gap fast, largely with proven tools
Adjacent30%$1.5 millionFraud flywheel; machine-readable product and price data for comparison channels and AI agentsDifferentiate on proprietary data
Transformational10%$0.5 millionHome Protect prevention pilotTransform, as a small staged bet

Compared with the 70/20/10 starting point, Harbor Mutual moves 10% of the budget from core to adjacent, because its fraud data gives it a real chance to differentiate. Core still gets the majority, because closing the parity gap on claims speed and service can't wait. The plan also uses staged funding. The Home Protect money is released in tranches, each only after the previous stage meets its criteria, which keeps the cost of a failed bet small. AWS's research also warns that AI spending is usage-based and hard to forecast, and that successful AI creates demand faster than annual budgets can reprioritize. Budgets therefore need regular review and room to move money toward what works.

Match the investment level to industry maturity, competitive pressure, strategic importance and readiness. Buy for parity in mature areas, build where proprietary data gives an edge, and fund transformational bets in stages.

Two opposite mistakes: copying a competitor's AI spending level without regard to your own strategy and readiness, and waiting for "the technology to mature" while competitors' data and customer leads compound.

Key takeaways

  • Assess competitors, new entrants, customer expectations, intermediaries (including AI agents acting for customers), suppliers and regulators. Separate areas where AI is needed for parity from areas where it can create advantage. Move first where leads compound; follow fast where capabilities will soon be common.
  • Business model transformation changes how value is created and captured: from product to service, from paying for losses to preventing them, usage-based pricing, selling capabilities externally, platforms. Pilot it before scaling.
  • Because everyone can access the same models, lasting advantage comes from proprietary data, feedback loops (data flywheels), AI embedded in redesigned processes, organizational capability, trust and ecosystem position. Purchased tools bring parity, not advantage.
  • Value comes from fixing the process around AI and giving someone ownership of turning saved time into business results.
  • Set AI investment by industry maturity, competitive pressure, strategic importance, data advantage, readiness, regulation and the cost of waiting. Hold different postures in different areas (monitor, adopt, differentiate, transform), balance core, adjacent and transformational spending, and fund big bets in stages.
