Task 2.3: Position AI for competitive advantage

Using AI to compete, not just to keep up: reading the competitive landscape, spotting where AI can change how a business makes money, telling lasting advantages from improvements every competitor will soon have, and deciding how much to invest given how mature the industry is and how fast rivals are moving.

Overview

Task 2.3

Position AI for competitive advantage.

Choosing initiatives that meet the organization's own objectives is only half of an AI strategy. The other half looks outward. How are competitors and new entrants using AI? Where could AI change the rules of the industry? Which AI investments create an edge rivals can't easily copy? And how much should the organization spend given the pace of its market?

One fact shapes all of these answers. The same foundation models are available to every company, often through the same cloud services. AWS's research with 128 executives across 27 countries and 23 industries concluded that, since everyone can access the same models, advantage comes from proprietary data, including the undocumented knowledge of employees (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Announcing the new AWS Reimagine report on AI, September 2026). Access to AI is not an advantage. What an organization uniquely brings to AI is.

The worked examples follow one company, because competitive position only makes sense for a specific business: a mid-size maker of industrial air compressors. Factories use compressed air to power tools, machines and production lines, so when a compressor breaks down, production can stop. The company sells its machines through dealers, and earns more from spare parts and repair visits over a machine's 15-year life. It has service records for 40,000 machines going back 15 years, and 12,000 of its newer machines send sensor readings (pressure, temperature, vibration, running hours) over the internet.

Assessing the competitive landscape

Skill 2.3.1Assess competitive landscapes and identify the advantages of adopting AI

Competitive landscape

Everyone whose choices affect whether a business wins customers and makes money: not only direct rivals, but new entrants, customers, the intermediaries between the business and its customers, suppliers and regulators. AI can shift the position of any of them.

Competitive assessment

A structured look at that landscape that answers three questions: where AI is changing what customers expect, who is using it to win business, and where the organization stands compared with them. Without one, AI plans either copy competitors' last moves or ignore threats until customers start leaving.

Who to look at

Direct competitors

What to watchNew AI-powered features, service speed, pricing changes.

ExampleThe market leader now monitors its connected compressors remotely and sends a technician before a breakdown, rather than after.

New entrants

What to watchStart-ups built around AI from day one, with lower costs.

ExampleA software start-up fits low-cost sensors to compressors of any brand and sells failure alerts by subscription, getting between the manufacturer and its customers.

Customers

What to watchRising expectations set by other industries.

ExampleFactory managers used to tracking a parcel to the minute now expect to see their machines' health on their phone, and to know a repair is coming before anything fails.

Intermediaries and channels

What to watchWho stands between you and the customer, and how they choose.

ExampleDealers, online industrial marketplaces, and AI purchasing agents that compare spare parts and service contracts on a factory's behalf.

Suppliers and partners

What to watchAI changing the cost or quality of what you buy.

ExampleMotor and sensor suppliers adding their own diagnostics, which could make them the source of machine data instead of the manufacturer.

Regulators

What to watchRules that enable or limit AI uses.

ExampleIn the EU, the Data Act gives users of connected products the right to access the data their products generate and to share it with third parties, such as an independent repairer. Machine data alone may therefore not lock customers in.

How to gather the evidence

  • Experience competitors as a customer: ask for a quote, request a service visit, use their support and their customer portal.
  • Public signals: annual reports, investor presentations, press releases, product launches.
  • Hiring patterns: a competitor recruiting dozens of ML engineers signals serious investment.
  • Customer and partner feedback: why customers leave, what dealers and service technicians hear in the field.
  • Industry studies and analysts: adoption surveys show how mature AI use is across the industry.

Worked example: the compressor maker's assessment

Service response

Market positionThe leader predicts failures and repairs before breakdowns.

The company todayRepairs after breakdowns, with a technician on site within two days on average.

ImplicationFalling behind on the factor customers care about most, because every breakdown stops their production. Must close the gap.

Dealer and customer support

Market positionAI troubleshooting assistants for dealers' technicians are becoming common.

The company todayA phone line and PDF manuals.

ImplicationCatch up with proven tools; little advantage to gain, but a real cost to falling behind.

Failure prediction

Market positionMost rivals are at an early stage.

The company todayNo model yet, but 15 years of service records on 40,000 machines and live sensor data from 12,000.

ImplicationA strong data position; an opportunity to lead.

How customers pay

Market positionNew entrants sell compressed air by the cubic meter instead of selling machines.

The company todayOne-off machine sales plus parts and repairs.

ImplicationA threat to the business model itself, especially with large customers who prefer operating costs to capital purchases.

The assessment separates two kinds of response. Some areas need parity: matching the market so as not to lose customers (service response, dealer support). Others offer advantage: where the company has something rivals don't (15 years of service records).

Advantages of adopting AI

Lower cost to serve

How AI creates itAutomates or speeds up routine work.

ExampleA telecom company resolves routine broadband faults through an AI self-service assistant, with no technician visit.

Speed

How AI creates itDecisions in minutes instead of days.

ExampleA lender gives small-business loan decisions the same day instead of in two weeks.

Better risk decisions

How AI creates itMore accurate predictions.

ExampleA card issuer loses less to fraud while blocking fewer genuine payments.

Personalization at scale

How AI creates itTailors offers and service to each customer.

ExampleAn online retailer shows each visitor products matched to their past purchases.

New products and services

How AI creates itMakes previously uneconomic offerings possible.

ExampleThe compressor maker guaranteeing uptime, which only works if failures can be predicted (Skill 2.3.2).

Better employee experience

How AI creates itRemoves tedious work.

ExampleService technicians dictate visit reports instead of typing them after each job.

Moving first or following fast

  • First mover: gains a head start in data, customer relationships and learning, but pays to learn what doesn't work and takes more technology and regulatory risk.
  • Fast follower: lets others prove the approach, then adopts quickly with lower risk, but gives up any head start and may face customers already won by the leader.

Moving first pays off where an early lead compounds, for example where more usage produces more data, which produces better results; that is the compressor maker's failure prediction. Following fast suits capabilities that will soon be available to everyone, such as its dealer troubleshooting assistant or standard office AI tools.

A good competitive assessment looks beyond direct competitors (new entrants, customer expectations, intermediaries, regulators) and separates areas where AI is needed for parity from areas where it can create advantage.

Copying a competitor's AI feature without asking whether it fits your strategy and data rarely creates advantage. At best it restores parity.

Transforming business models with AI

Skill 2.3.2Identify opportunities to transform business models with AI

Business model

How a business creates and captures value: what it offers, to whom, through which channels, how it gets paid, and what it costs to deliver. Two companies can sell similar products with very different business models; one sells a printer cheaply and makes its money on ink, another sells printing by the page.

Business model transformation

Changing one or more of those elements, not just doing the same work better. Most AI work improves the existing business: the same products, delivered faster or cheaper. Transformation is rarer, riskier and potentially far more valuable, because AI can make offers possible that were too costly, too risky or impossible to deliver before.

The elements AI can change

Value proposition

QuestionWhat problem do we solve for customers?

How AI can change itFrom fixing a problem after it happens to preventing it.

Customer segments

QuestionWhom do we serve?

How AI can change itServing customers who were too costly or risky to serve before.

Channels

QuestionHow do we reach customers?

How AI can change itThrough AI assistants and agents acting for customers.

Revenue model

QuestionHow do we get paid?

How AI can change itUsage-based, subscription or outcome-based pricing.

Cost structure

QuestionWhat does it cost to deliver?

How AI can change itMuch lower cost per transaction, so new price points work.

Patterns of AI-enabled business model change

From product to service

What changesSell an outcome instead of a thing.

ExampleAircraft engine makers have long sold engine availability by the flying hour rather than engines alone; failure prediction makes the same model viable for compressors, pumps and lifts.

From reacting to preventing

What changesUse prediction to stop problems before they happen.

ExampleA home insurer offers water sensors that alert homeowners to a leak before it becomes a flood.

Usage-based pricing

What changesPrice according to measured behavior or use.

ExampleCar insurance priced by how and how much people drive.

Mass personalization

What changesProducts tailored to each customer at no extra cost.

ExampleA retailer generates personalized product bundles and descriptions.

Selling a capability externally

What changesAn internal AI capability becomes a product for others.

ExampleA bank sells its fraud-detection service to smaller banks.

Platforms and ecosystems

What changesCoordinate partners and customers around your data and services.

ExampleA farm equipment maker's platform connects farmers' machine and field data with agronomists, seed suppliers and contractors.

AWS's guidance on agentic AI describes the same opportunities. Beyond immediate efficiency gains, agents can unlock longer-term growth by combining agent services into industry-specific solutions, by network effects in multi-agent ecosystems, and by market extension: new revenue from agent-enabled services that outside customers can use. AWS Marketplace is one channel for publishing agentic solutions as commercial products (AWS Prescriptive Guidance, Operationalizing agentic AI on AWS, focus area 6).

A new channel: AI agents as buyers

AI agents are starting to research and buy on people's behalf. AWS's retail strategists argue that retailers are moving from competing for human attention to competing for "algorithmic inclusion". When an agent makes the purchase, what decides visibility is structured, accurate product data, real-time availability and reliable fulfilment, not marketing. They expect the same dynamic in travel, financial services and procurement (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Experience, exploration, execution: the three channels reshaping retail, May 2026).

For the compressor maker, a factory's purchasing agent may soon compare spare parts and service contracts automatically. If its parts catalogue, stock levels and prices can't be read reliably by machines, it may simply not appear in the comparison, however good its products are.

Worked example: selling compressed air instead of compressors

The idea: instead of selling a compressor for a one-off price, the company installs and owns the machine at the customer's factory, guarantees a supply of compressed air, and charges per cubic meter delivered. A failure-prediction model, fed by the machine's sensors, schedules maintenance before breakdowns, so the company can afford to promise uptime.

ElementTodayAir as a service
Value propositionA machine, repaired after it breaksGuaranteed compressed air, with breakdowns prevented
RevenueOne-off sale, then parts and repairsMonthly fee per cubic meter delivered
Customer relationshipContact at purchase and at breakdownsContinuous monitoring and planned visits
DataService records after the factLive sensor data that improves the model and pricing over time
PartnersDealers sell machinesDealers become service partners; a finance partner funds machines the company now owns

Questions to answer before investing:

  • Value: over a machine's life, does fee revenue beat a sale plus parts? Can the company fund owning thousands of machines while the fees come in gradually?
  • Feasibility: does the failure model predict far enough ahead to schedule visits, and is internet connectivity reliable at customer sites?
  • Risk: penalties if supply fails, and customers' rights over their machines' data.
  • Fit: dealers earn from sales today; will they support a model that replaces sales with fees (channel conflict)?

The sensible next step is a paid pilot at 50 customer sites, with success criteria agreed in advance, rather than a full launch.

Business model transformation changes how value is created and captured (offering, customers, channel, revenue model). Doing the same work more cheaply is an operational improvement, not a new business model.

Launching a new AI-based business model at full scale without a pilot ignores the extra risk it carries: unproven demand, new partners, new regulatory questions and possible conflict with existing channels.

Sustainable competitive advantage and operational improvement

Skill 2.3.3Describe how AI creates sustainable competitive advantages and operational improvements

Operational improvement

Doing existing work faster, cheaper or better, usually with widely available tools. The savings are real, but competitors can buy the same tools and match them, so the benefit is parity: needed to avoid falling behind, not a way to pull ahead.

ExampleA purchased AI assistant that drafts contact-center notes.

Sustainable competitive advantage

An advantage competitors can't easily copy. For AI this is the crux: anything a company can buy, a competitor can buy too. When every company in an industry adopts the same AI note-taking tool, it stops being an advantage and becomes table stakes, necessary to stay in the game but not a way to win. Lasting AI advantage rests on something unique to the organization, and often grows over time.

ExampleThe compressor maker's failure-prediction model, trained on 15 years of service records and improved by every technician visit.

Both matter. Operational improvements fund the business and prevent decline. A strategy made only of operational improvements, though, leaves the organization exactly level with its rivals.

Sources of lasting advantage

Proprietary data

Why it's hard to copyCompetitors can't buy your history, your customers' behavior or your experts' judgments.

Example15 years of service records showing which readings came before which failures, and technicians' notes on what they found.

Feedback loops (data flywheel)

Why it's hard to copyEach use generates data that improves the AI, so the lead grows with time.

ExampleEvery repair visit confirms or corrects a prediction and becomes a new training example.

AI embedded in redesigned processes

Why it's hard to copyA redesigned process involves people, systems, rules and culture together, which takes years to copy.

ExampleService rebuilt around predicted failures: parts stocked at regional depots ahead of need, technicians' routes planned from the predictions.

Organizational capability

Why it's hard to copySkills, experience and ways of working accumulate slowly.

ExampleTeams that can take a use case from idea to production in weeks.

Trust and reputation

Why it's hard to copyBuilt over years and lost quickly.

ExampleKnown for honest uptime figures and for respecting what customers' machine data may be used for.

Ecosystem position

Why it's hard to copyPartners and customers connected to your platform create network effects.

ExampleDealers and parts suppliers working from the company's service platform, so leaving it means rebuilding their own ways of working.

AWS's enterprise strategists put the data point directly: "At AWS we say that data is your differentiator." Models, open-source software and cloud services are available to everyone. When an organization adds its own data and embeds AI into its business processes, the result is differentiated (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, True data-centricity, February 2026).

The data flywheel

A flywheel is a self-reinforcing loop: once it turns, each cycle makes the next easier. For the compressor maker's failure prediction:

  1. Better predictionsThe model warns of failures further ahead and with fewer false alarms.
  2. Fewer breakdownsTechnicians fix machines before they stop, so customers' production keeps running.
  3. More connected customersBetter uptime wins more service contracts and more machine sales, so more machines send data.
  4. More labeled dataEvery visit records what the technician actually found, confirming or correcting a prediction.
  5. RetrainingThe model learns from the new examples, and the loop starts again.

A competitor buying an off-the-shelf monitoring tool starts without that history and without the loop. The flywheel only turns, however, if technicians record the real cause they found, not just "repaired". That is why data quality and the discipline of recording outcomes are strategic, not administrative. The regulator point from the assessment matters here too: if customers can share their machine data with others, the lasting edge is less the raw data than the 15 years of labeled outcomes and the redesigned service process built around them.

Why the process matters more than the tool

AWS's research found that the organizations generating value from AI fix the process around AI, not just the AI. In one AWS example, AI cut the effort of rebuilding a service from 30 engineers for a year to 5 engineers in 70 days, but delivery didn't speed up because security review, deployment and interface review were still the bottleneck. The cost of doing the work fell sharply; the cost of deciding, funding, governing and approving didn't. The same research found that nearly half of the interviews describing efficiency gains showed no evidence of business impact, because no one owned turning the saved time into results (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Announcing the new AWS Reimagine report on AI, September 2026).

For the compressor maker: a model that predicts a bearing failure ten days ahead is worthless if the spare part still takes two weeks to arrive from the central warehouse. Advantage comes from redesigning the whole flow (parts stocking, technician scheduling, what dealers are paid for) and naming an owner who turns predictions into avoided breakdowns.

Testing whether an advantage will last

Four questions, applied to any proposed AI advantage:

  1. Valuable?Does it improve something customers or the business care about?
  2. Rare?Do few competitors have it?
  3. Hard to imitate?Does it rest on data, processes or relationships others can't buy?
  4. Organized to capture it?Do we have the owners, processes and skills to turn it into results?

A purchased AI writing tool passes the first test and fails the next two. The compressor maker's failure-prediction flywheel can pass all four, provided technicians record outcomes and the service process is redesigned around it.

Sustainable AI advantage comes from what competitors can't buy: proprietary data, feedback loops, AI embedded in redesigned processes, organizational capability and trust. Models and tools alone are available to everyone.

"Using the most advanced model" isn't a sustainable advantage. Competitors can use the same model tomorrow. Likewise, efficiency gains that aren't tied to an owner and a business result often vanish into absorbed capacity.

Setting AI investment levels

Skill 2.3.4Determine appropriate AI investment levels based on industry maturity and competitive dynamics

Industry AI maturity

How far AI use has spread and proven itself in an industry. In an immature industry, a few companies are experimenting and the value is still uncertain; in a mature one, certain AI uses are standard and customers expect them. Banking fraud detection is mature; AI in many construction trades is not.

Competitive dynamics

How fast and in what direction rivals are moving: whether competitors or new entrants are winning business with AI, and whether an early lead compounds or can easily be caught up.

How much to invest in AI, and in what kind of AI, depends on these, not on a fixed percentage. Investing too little in a fast-moving market risks losing customers to rivals who move first. Investing too much too early, in a market that isn't ready, burns money on capabilities customers don't yet value.

Factors that set the level

Industry AI maturity

Pushes investment upAI is becoming standard; laggards visibly lose customers.

Pushes it down or laterAI use is early and unproven in this industry.

Competitive pressure

Pushes investment upRivals or new entrants are winning business with AI.

Pushes it down or laterCompetitors are not moving; customers aren't asking.

Strategic importance

Pushes investment upAI affects the core of how the business competes.

Pushes it down or laterAI touches only support functions.

Data advantage

Pushes investment upThe organization holds unique data that AI can exploit.

Pushes it down or laterNo distinctive data; generic tools will do.

Organizational readiness

Pushes investment upData, skills and sponsorship are in place.

Pushes it down or laterFoundations are missing; invest in them first.

Regulatory and risk environment

Pushes investment upClear rules make investment safer.

Pushes it down or laterUncertain rules on key uses; invest cautiously and keep options open.

Cost of waiting

Pushes investment upLeaders' head start compounds (data, customers).

Pushes it down or laterCapabilities are getting cheaper and easier to adopt later.

Investment postures

Monitor and experiment

When it fitsLow industry maturity, little competitive pressure, uncertain value.

What investment looks likeSmall budget for learning: pilots, training, buying ready-made tools.

Adopt (fast follower)

When it fitsAI becoming standard in the industry; parity needed.

What investment looks likeModerate budget, mostly buying proven solutions, quick rollout.

Differentiate

When it fitsCompetitive pressure plus a distinctive data or process advantage.

What investment looks likeSignificant budget to build on proprietary data, with platforms and skills to match.

Transform

When it fitsAI is reshaping the industry's business models.

What investment looks likeLarge, staged investments in new business models, alongside protecting the core.

A single organization usually holds different postures in different areas, depending on where it needs parity and where it can win.

Building a portfolio

A widely used way to balance investment is to split the budget across three horizons. A commonly cited starting split from innovation management is about 70% core, 20% adjacent and 10% transformational, adjusted to the industry's pace:

  • Core: improve the existing business: efficiency, parity, quick wins.
  • Adjacent: extend into nearby areas: new customer segments, differentiating capabilities.
  • Transformational: new business models and bets on the future.

Faster-moving markets shift weight toward the later horizons.

Worked example: the compressor maker's two-year AI budget

Leadership has $5 million for AI over two years. Predictive service is becoming standard among the market leaders, a new entrant is selling air by the cubic meter, and the company holds an unusual data asset in its service records.

Core: 60%, $3.0 million
Initiatives
Dealer troubleshooting assistant, dictated service reports, extraction of parts orders from emails, plus the data and governance foundations
Posture
Adopt: close the parity gap fast, largely with proven tools
Adjacent: 30%, $1.5 million
Initiatives
Failure-prediction flywheel; a machine-readable parts catalogue for marketplaces and purchasing agents
Posture
Differentiate on proprietary data
Transformational: 10%, $0.5 million
Initiatives
Air-as-a-service pilot at 50 customer sites
Posture
Transform, as a small staged bet

Compared with the 70/20/10 starting point, the company moves 10% of the budget from core to adjacent, because its service records give it a real chance to differentiate. Core still gets the majority, because closing the parity gap on service can't wait. The plan also uses staged funding. The air-as-a-service money is released in tranches, each only after the previous stage meets its criteria, which keeps the cost of a failed bet small. AWS's research also warns that AI spending is usage-based and hard to forecast, and that successful AI creates demand faster than annual budgets can reprioritize. Budgets therefore need regular review and room to move money toward what works.

Match the investment level to industry maturity, competitive pressure, strategic importance and readiness. Buy for parity in mature areas, build where proprietary data gives an edge, and fund transformational bets in stages.

Two opposite mistakes: copying a competitor's AI spending level without regard to your own strategy and readiness, and waiting for "the technology to mature" while competitors' data and customer leads compound.

Key takeaways

  • Assess competitors, new entrants, customer expectations, intermediaries (including AI agents acting for customers), suppliers and regulators. Separate areas where AI is needed for parity from areas where it can create advantage. Move first where leads compound; follow fast where capabilities will soon be common.
  • Business model transformation changes how value is created and captured: from product to service, from paying for losses to preventing them, usage-based pricing, selling capabilities externally, platforms. Pilot it before scaling.
  • Because everyone can access the same models, lasting advantage comes from proprietary data, feedback loops (data flywheels), AI embedded in redesigned processes, organizational capability, trust and ecosystem position. Purchased tools bring parity, not advantage.
  • Value comes from fixing the process around AI and giving someone ownership of turning saved time into business results.
  • Set AI investment by industry maturity, competitive pressure, strategic importance, data advantage, readiness, regulation and the cost of waiting. Hold different postures in different areas (monitor, adopt, differentiate, transform), balance core, adjacent and transformational spending, and fund big bets in stages.
