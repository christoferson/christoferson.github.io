Task 2.2: Measure and demonstrate AI business value

Proving that AI pays off: choosing indicators that reflect business results, measuring the starting point before anything changes, separating the AI's effect from everything else, calculating return on investment honestly, spotting early signs of success or failure, and keeping AI costs planned, visible and under control.

Overview

Task 2.2

Measure and demonstrate AI business value.

AI initiatives compete for budget with every other investment, and leaders need evidence, not impressions, to decide whether to scale them. Measuring AI value is harder than it sounds. Benefits are often spread across many people (90 seconds saved per call), some are hard to put a price on (customer satisfaction), costs keep running after launch, and other changes happen at the same time. This task covers the chain of evidence: what to measure, what it was before, what changed because of AI, what it was worth, what it cost, and how to keep costs in check.

The running example is the use case Harbor Mutual prioritized first: AI-drafted after-call summaries for its 400 contact-center agents, who handle about 24,000 calls a day. Harbor Mutual is a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees and 1.2 million policyholders. Examples from other industries are included too.

Defining KPIs for AI initiatives

Skill 2.2.1

A key performance indicator (KPI) is a measure that shows whether an initiative is achieving its purpose. For AI, the most common mistake is measuring the technology instead of the business. "The model is 94% accurate" says nothing about whether claims are settled faster or customers are happier. A model can be highly accurate and still deliver no value if nobody uses it or the process around it doesn't change.

Linking measures from model to business

Good AI measurement connects four levels, so you can trace a business result back to its cause:

LevelQuestionAfter-call summary example
Business outcomeDid the business result improve?Cost per call down 15%; customer satisfaction maintained
OperationalDid the process change?After-call work time per call, calls handled per agent-hour
AdoptionAre people using it as intended?Share of calls where the AI summary is used
Model qualityIs the AI output good?Share of summaries accepted without edits; factual errors found in audits

If the business outcome doesn't move, the lower levels show why. Perhaps quality is fine but agents don't use the summaries, or they use them but the time saved isn't being redeployed.

Tangible and intangible benefits

TypeWhat it isExample KPIsHow to measure
Tangible: cost reductionMoney no longer spentCost per call, cost per claim processed, overtime hours, outsourcing spendFinance and operations data
Tangible: revenue growthMore money coming inRetention rate at renewal, cross-sell conversion, new policies soldSales and policy data
Tangible: loss or risk reductionLosses avoidedFraud losses, compliance penalties, payment errorsClaims and audit data
Intangible: customer satisfactionHow customers feel about the serviceCustomer satisfaction score (CSAT), Net Promoter Score (NPS), complaintsPost-call surveys, complaint logs
Intangible: employee productivity and experienceHow well and how happily people workTime spent on high-value work, employee satisfaction, staff turnoverTime studies, staff surveys, HR data
Intangible: quality and speedConsistency and responsivenessNote completeness, claim cycle time, first-contact resolutionQuality audits, system timestamps

"Intangible" doesn't mean unmeasurable. It means the benefit is hard to put a reliable price on. Measure it through a proxy, a number that moves with it (survey scores, complaint counts, turnover), and report it alongside the financial figures rather than forcing it into a dollar value. Intangible benefits often become tangible later. Lower agent turnover, for example, cuts hiring and training costs.

What makes a good KPI

Each KPI should be specific, measurable from existing data, owned by a named person, compared against a baseline, and tied to a target and a date. A KPI definition for the after-call summary initiative:

KPI definition 
KPI:          Average after-call work time per call
Definition:   Minutes from call end to "wrap-up complete" in the
              contact-center system, all inbound service calls
Owner:        Head of Contact Center Operations
Baseline:     6.2 minutes (4-week average, measured before pilot)
Target:       4.5 minutes within 6 months of full rollout
Data source:  Contact-center system timestamps, daily
Related:      CSAT (must stay at or above 82%), note quality audit
              score (must stay at or above 90%)

The "Related" line matters. Every efficiency KPI needs a guardrail metric that would reveal a hidden cost. Agents could cut wrap-up time by writing poor notes; the quality audit and CSAT would catch that.

Examples in other industries

Business and use caseTangible KPIIntangible KPI
Retailer: demand forecastingStock-outs avoided, write-offs of unsold stockCustomer satisfaction with availability
Bank: AI coding assistant for developersFeatures delivered per quarter, contractor spendDeveloper satisfaction, time spent on design rather than routine code
Hospital: summarizing patient historiesClinician minutes per appointment spent on recordsClinician burnout scores, patient experience

The best KPIs measure business outcomes (cost, revenue, risk, satisfaction), with adoption and model quality as supporting measures, and include guardrail metrics so that gains in one area don't hide losses in another.

Vanity metrics such as "number of prompts sent" or "employees with access" show activity, not value. Model accuracy alone isn't a business KPI either.

Establishing baselines before implementing AI

Skill 2.2.2

A baseline is the measured value of each KPI before the AI is introduced. Without it, there is no way to show what changed. Once the AI is live, the old process can't be measured anymore, and people's memories of "how long it used to take" are unreliable and usually exaggerated. The baseline has to be captured before the project starts changing the work.

How to capture a reliable baseline

  1. Fix the definitionsUse the same KPI definition, data source and calculation before and after. Changing the definition midway makes the comparison meaningless.
  2. Measure for long enoughSeveral weeks at least, covering normal variation in workload. One unusual week can distort everything.
  3. Account for seasonalityHarbor Mutual's call volumes jump after storms and at renewal season. Compare like with like, or use the same period last year.
  4. Record the contextStaffing levels, call mix, other changes planned during the same period.
  5. Measure costs tooThe current cost of the process, so savings can be calculated.

Isolating the AI's effect

Measuring before and after isn't always enough, because other things change at the same time: a new phone system, a training program, a quiet month. There are three ways to compare, from weakest to strongest:

MethodHow it worksWeakness or strength
Before and afterCompare the KPI before launch with the KPI after launchSimple, but any other change during the period gets credited to the AI
Pilot group versus comparison groupOne group uses the AI; a similar group doesn't; compare the change in both over the same periodRemoves the effect of changes that hit both groups; the groups must be genuinely similar
Randomized test (A/B test)Cases or people are assigned to AI or no AI at randomStrongest evidence, though not always practical or fair to staff

Worked example: what the pilot really saved

Harbor Mutual pilots the after-call summaries with 40 agents for eight weeks. A comparison group of 40 similar agents (same shifts, similar experience) carries on as before. During the same period, a new phone system is installed for everyone.

GroupBaselineEnd of pilotChange
Pilot group (AI summaries)6.2 minutes4.6 minutes−1.6 minutes
Comparison group (no AI)6.1 minutes5.8 minutes−0.3 minutes

A simple before-and-after would have claimed 1.6 minutes per call. The comparison group shows 0.3 minutes came from the new phone system, which affected everyone. The AI's real effect is the difference between the two changes: 1.6 − 0.3 = about 1.3 minutes (78 seconds) per call, and that is the number the business case should use.

Common pitfalls

  • Selection bias: putting the best agents in the pilot makes the AI look better than it is. Choose a representative pilot group.
  • Novelty effect: people often work differently simply because they are being observed or have a new tool. Results in the first two weeks can fade, so measure for long enough.
  • Moving the goalposts: redefining the KPI after seeing results.
  • Ignoring quality: time fell, but did note quality or customer satisfaction fall too? Check the guardrail metrics against their baselines as well.

"Measure before you change anything" is the core principle. When a scenario says an organization can't prove an AI benefit, the missing piece is often a baseline or a comparison group.

Crediting the AI with every improvement since launch overstates its value and damages trust when finance checks the numbers.

Calculating AI return on investment

Skill 2.2.3

Return on investment (ROI) compares what an initiative returns with what it costs:

Formulas 
ROI = (total benefits − total costs) ÷ total costs × 100%

Payback period = time until cumulative benefits equal cumulative costs

The arithmetic is simple. The skill lies in counting benefits honestly and costs completely, over a realistic period, usually three years for AI.

Benefit categories

CategoryHow to value itCaution
Time savingsHours saved × loaded hourly costSaved time is only cash if it reduces spending (fewer hires, less overtime or outsourcing) or is used for work that creates value. Otherwise it is capacity, not savings.
Cost reductionSpending that stops: licences, outsourcing, rework, error correctionCount only costs that actually disappear
Revenue growthExtra revenue attributable to the AI, ideally from a comparison groupUse profit margin, not total revenue, when comparing with costs
Productivity gainsMore output with the same people: claims closed per adjuster, calls per agent-hourConvert to value through avoided hiring or extra output sold
Risk and loss reductionLosses avoided: fraud caught, penalties avoidedBase it on measured detection rates, not on vendor claims

Cost categories

AI business cases most often fail by leaving out costs. A comprehensive view includes:

  • Build or acquisition: development, partner fees, licences, setup.
  • Usage: tokens, model hosting, storage, data processing, which grow with volume.
  • Data: preparing, cleaning, labeling and integrating data.
  • People: the team that runs, monitors and improves the solution.
  • Change management: training, process redesign, communications.
  • Governance and risk: security reviews, audits, monitoring, human review of outputs.
  • Ongoing change: model upgrades, prompt retesting, retraining.

Worked example: three-year ROI for after-call summaries

Start with the measured effect from the pilot: 1.3 minutes saved per call.

  1. Time saved24,000 calls a day × 1.3 minutes = 31,200 minutes, or 520 agent-hours a day. Over 220 working days, that is about 114,000 hours a year.
  2. Gross value of the time114,000 hours × $35 loaded cost is about $4.0 million a year. This is capacity, not cash.
  3. Realized cash benefitCall volume is forecast to grow 10%, which would have needed 40 more agents. The freed capacity absorbs the growth instead, avoiding 40 hires at $60,000 each: $2.4 million a year. The rest of the freed time goes into quality and coaching, reported as an intangible benefit.
  4. Ramp-upYear 1 delivers half the benefit while rollout and adoption build: $1.2 million. Years 2 and 3 deliver $2.4 million each.
ItemYear 1Year 2Year 3
Build (partner project)$300,000––
Model usage (tokens)$85,000$85,000$85,000
Run team (2 people)$280,000$280,000$280,000
Training and change management$50,000––
Monitoring, audits and governance$40,000$40,000$40,000
Total cost$755,000$405,000$405,000
Benefit (avoided hiring)$1,200,000$2,400,000$2,400,000
  • Three-year totals: costs $1,565,000; benefits $6,000,000.
  • ROI: ($6,000,000 − $1,565,000) ÷ $1,565,000 = about 283%.
  • Payback: within the first year, since year 1 benefits ($1.2 million) already exceed year 1 costs ($755,000).

Testing the assumptions

A credible business case shows what happens if the assumptions are wrong. If the benefit turns out to be half the estimate ($3.0 million over three years), ROI is ($3,000,000 − $1,565,000) ÷ $1,565,000, about 92%: still clearly positive. Leaders can then see that the decision doesn't depend on an optimistic guess. Note also what was not counted. The $4.0 million gross value of time was not used; only the $2.4 million that turns into avoided cost was. Being conservative here is what makes finance teams trust the number.

The same framework applies elsewhere. For Harbor Mutual's fraud model, the benefit is the additional fraud caught (measured against the old rules on the same claims) multiplied by the average payout, minus the extra investigation cost of any additional false alarms.

Strong ROI answers use measured effects against a baseline, complete costs including running, people, governance and change management, a multi-year view, and a check of what happens if the assumptions are wrong.

Treating all time saved as cash savings is the most common ROI error. Saved hours are only money if they reduce spending or produce valuable extra output.

Leading indicators of AI success

Skill 2.2.4

Business outcomes such as cost per call or annual fraud losses are lagging indicators: they confirm success, but only months later. Leading indicators move early and predict whether those outcomes will arrive. They let leaders fix problems, or stop an initiative, before the money is spent.

Leading indicatorWhat it predictsAfter-call summary example
Active usage rateWhether benefits will materialize at allShare of pilot agents using summaries on most calls
Repeat use over timeWhether usage lasts after the novelty wears offUsage in week 6 compared with week 2
Acceptance or override rateWhether output quality is good enough to save timeSummaries accepted without edits
User feedbackTrust and willingness to rely on itThumbs-up rate, comments in team meetings
Integration into the workflowWhether the process has really changedSummary appears automatically in the wrap-up screen, not in a separate tool
Time to first valueMomentum and stakeholder confidenceDays from pilot start to the first measurable time saving
Unit cost trendWhether the economics will hold at scaleCost per summary stable or falling as volume grows
Readiness milestonesWhether the project can deliverData access approved, security review passed, executive sponsor active

Reading the indicators during a pilot

IndicatorWeek 2Week 4Week 8
Agents using summaries on most calls55%78%90%
Summaries accepted without edits70%84%92%
After-call work time5.6 minutes5.0 minutes4.6 minutes

All three are rising together, and the improvement in acceptance after week 2 matches a prompt change the team made. That is a healthy pattern that supports a decision to scale. An unhealthy pattern would be usage of 80% in week 2 falling to 40% by week 8, high override rates, or agents keeping their own notes in parallel. Those are warning signs to investigate before any rollout.

Leading indicators for the initiative as a whole are just as telling: an active executive sponsor, a named business owner, data access secured early, and end users involved in design. Their absence is one of the most reliable predictors of a pilot that never reaches production.

Adoption, repeat use and acceptance rates are the classic leading indicators. Revenue, cost savings and ROI are lagging indicators.

High sign-up numbers in the first week aren't a leading indicator of success. Sustained, repeat use in the real workflow is.

Cost planning and control

Skill 2.2.5

AI costs behave differently from traditional software. Generative AI costs grow with every request and with the length of each request. A popular tool can cost far more than planned simply because people use it, and an agent stuck in a loop can spend money all night. Cost control means planning costs before building, seeing them clearly while running, and optimizing them continuously.

What drives AI costs

Cost driverWhy it grows
Model choiceLarger, more capable models cost more per token
Request volumeMore users and more use cases mean more requests
Tokens per requestLong instructions, conversation history, retrieved documents and long answers
Agent stepsEach step can be another model call; loops multiply cost
Hosting and capacityReserved capacity costs money whether it is used or not
Data and storageVector stores, document processing and logs grow over time
CustomizationTraining jobs and monthly storage for custom models

Plan: estimate before building

AWS Pricing Calculator is a free planning tool for creating cost estimates before using AWS services. It shows the calculations behind each estimate, groups estimates to match the architecture, and exports them as CSV or PDF to share with stakeholders (AWS Pricing Calculator User Guide). A planning estimate should state its assumptions (volume, tokens per request, model) so they can be checked against reality later. It should also include a unit cost, the cost per business transaction, which is easier for business owners to judge than a monthly total. For the after-call summaries: $324 a day ÷ 24,000 summaries = about $0.0135 per summary.

See: track costs by initiative

  • Cost allocation tags label AWS resources with business information so costs can be grouped by application, team or cost center.
  • Amazon Bedrock application inference profiles let each application or team call models through its own profile. Tags attached to the profile track that application's model costs, and usage metrics can be collected per profile (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Inference profiles). Without this, every team's model usage appears as one combined Bedrock line on the bill.
  • AWS Cost Explorer shows and analyzes costs and usage, grouped and filtered by service or tag. It covers up to the last 13 months and forecasts the next 18 months, and supports natural-language questions about costs (AWS Cost Management User Guide, Cost Explorer).

Tags for Harbor Mutual's agent-assist profile might be:

Cost allocation tags 
Application:  agent-assist
CostCenter:   contact-center
Owner:        head-of-cc-operations
Environment:  production

With these, Cost Explorer can show the contact center's AI cost each month next to the KPIs it is meant to improve. Showing each department its costs (showback) or charging them to its budget (chargeback) makes teams accountable for how they use AI.

Control: alerts and limits

  • AWS Budgets tracks costs and usage against set amounts, with alerts on actual or forecasted spend sent by email or Amazon SNS. Budget actions can respond automatically, for example by applying a policy that stops new resources being created (AWS Cost Management User Guide, Budgets). AWS notes there can be a delay between incurring a charge and the alert.
  • Workflow limits: for agents, set stopping conditions such as maximum steps, time and spend per task, so a workflow can't run away (AWS Well-Architected Generative AI Lens, GENCOST05-BP01).
  • Usage quotas: per-user or per-team limits on requests for internal tools.

Optimize: reduce cost without losing value

The AWS Well-Architected Generative AI Lens groups cost practices into three principles: optimize model and inference selection, control the parameters that drive consumption, and design workflow boundaries (AWS Well-Architected Generative AI Lens, Cost optimization).

Worked example: cutting the summary cost in half

Using the same hypothetical prices as before ($3 per million input tokens, $15 per million output tokens, and an assumed cache-read price of $0.30 per million), the current cost is $216 a day for input plus $108 for output, $324 in total:

  1. Trim the instructionsInput falls from 3,000 to 2,400 tokens per request, so input cost drops from $216 to $172.80 a day.
  2. Cache the fixed instructions1,500 of those 2,400 tokens are identical in every request. Cached: 36 million tokens at $0.30 per million = $10.80. Uncached: 21.6 million tokens at $3 = $64.80. Input is now $75.60 a day (ignoring the smaller cost of writing to the cache).
  3. Shorten the summariesOutput falls from 300 to 200 tokens per summary, so output cost drops from $108 to $72 a day.
  4. Result$147.60 a day instead of $324, a 54% reduction. The unit cost falls from about $0.0135 to $0.0062 per summary, and the monthly cost from about $7,100 to $3,250.

Each change is tested against the quality test set first. A cheaper summary that agents have to rewrite saves nothing.

Basic AI cost control has three parts: plan (Pricing Calculator estimates with stated assumptions and unit costs), see (tags, application inference profiles, Cost Explorer), and control and optimize (Budgets alerts, workflow limits, right-sized models, the right pricing model, shorter prompts and responses, caching).

Choosing the most capable model for every task "to be safe" is a common and expensive mistake. Optimizing cost without checking quality is the opposite mistake.

Key takeaways

  • Measure business outcomes first, supported by operational, adoption and model quality measures. Include tangible KPIs (cost, revenue, losses) and intangible ones (satisfaction, productivity) measured through proxies. Add guardrail metrics, and give each KPI an owner, baseline, target and date.
  • Capture baselines before anything changes, over a long enough period, with fixed definitions and attention to seasonality. Use a comparison group or randomized test to separate the AI's effect from other changes.
  • ROI = (benefits − costs) ÷ costs. Value time savings only where they become cash or valuable output; include all costs (usage, people, data, change management, governance, upgrades); use a multi-year view and show what happens if assumptions are wrong.
  • Leading indicators (active and repeat usage, acceptance rates, feedback, workflow integration, unit cost trend, readiness milestones) predict the lagging business outcomes and support early scale, pause or stop decisions.
  • Control AI costs by planning with AWS Pricing Calculator and unit costs, tracking with tags, Bedrock application inference profiles and Cost Explorer, alerting with AWS Budgets, and optimizing models, pricing models, prompt and response length, caching and agent limits, always checking quality.
