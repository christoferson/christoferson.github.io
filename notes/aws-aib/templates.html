Templates

Fill-in templates for the documents an AI business strategist produces, from the first use case brief to the production readiness review. Each shows what to capture, with placeholders in {braces} and short notes on the fields that matter most. They also make a good revision tool: if you can fill one in for a scenario, you understand that part of the exam.

Strategy and value

Use case brief

UseAt the envision stage, to propose a use case on one page so it can be compared with others.

use-case-brief 
Use case:
  {short name}
Business owner:
  {name, role}
  accountable for the outcome
Objective served:
  {which stated business objective}
Problem today:
  {what is slow, costly or risky, with
  numbers}
Proposed solution:
  {what the AI will do: rules, ML,
  generative AI or agent; what stays with
  people}
Users:
  {who uses it; internal or
  customer-facing}
Value hypothesis:
  {e.g. save 90 seconds on 24,000 calls a
  day}
Measures:
  {primary KPI, guardrail metrics}
Data needed:
  {sources; available? permitted for this
  use?}
Risk tier (draft):
  {low / medium / high, and why}
Sourcing (draft):
  {build / buy / partner}
Not AI?
  {could rules, a process change or a
  dashboard solve it instead?}
Next step:
  {e.g. 6-week proof of concept}

WatchA brief without a business owner, a baseline number or a "could this be solved without AI?" answer isn't ready to compare.

How to fill it

Use case

How to fill itA short name for the task the AI will do, in business language: "after-call summaries", not "LLM pilot"

Common mistakeNaming a technology instead of a task.

Business owner

How to fill itOne named business leader who will answer for the result and has authority over the process that changes.

Common mistake"The AI team", or two co-owners.

Objective served

How to fill itQuote the stated business objective it moves. If it doesn't move one, say so; that is useful information.

Common mistakeVague goals such as "innovation" or "being AI-first"

Problem today

How to fill itWhat is slow, costly or risky now, with measured numbers and their source. If you have no numbers, getting them is the first step.

Common mistakeEstimates from memory ("it takes ages").

Proposed solution

How to fill itWhat the AI will do, the solution type (rules, ML, generative AI, agent), and what stays with people.

Common mistakeChoosing the most advanced type by default.

Users

How to fill itWho uses it, how many, and whether it is internal or customer-facing. The audience strongly affects the risk tier.

Common mistakeForgetting the people affected by its output, not only those who use it.

Value hypothesis

How to fill itA testable statement: effect per case × volume × value, with the assumption labeled as an estimate to be tested.

Common mistakeCounting all time saved as cash.

Measures

How to fill itOne primary business KPI, one or two guardrail metrics, and a leading indicator for the pilot.

Common mistakeModel accuracy as the only measure.

Data needed

How to fill itEach source, whether it exists digitally, its quality, who owns it, and whether its use for this purpose is permitted.

Common mistakeAssuming data collected for one purpose can be used for another.

Risk tier (draft)

How to fill itA first view using impact on people, autonomy, data sensitivity, audience, scale, reversibility and regulation. Governance confirms it.

Common mistakeClassifying by technology rather than use.

Sourcing (draft)

How to fill itBuild, buy or partner, with one line of reasoning (differentiation, timeline, skills, data, compliance).

Common mistakeDeciding before checking whether a product already does this.

Not AI?

How to fill itAn honest answer: could a rule, a process change or a dashboard solve most of the problem?

Common mistakeLeaving it blank.

Next step

How to fill itThe smallest step that tests the riskiest assumption, with a duration and a decision at the end.

Common mistake"Build it", with no end point.

Filled example

use-case-brief: after-call summaries (Harbor Mutual) 
Use case:
  After-call summaries for contact-center
  agents

Business owner:
  Head of Contact Center Operations

Objective served:
  Reduce contact-center cost per call by
  15% without lowering customer
  satisfaction

Problem today:
  After-call work averages 6.2 minutes per
  call (4-week measurement from the
  contact-center system). 24,000 calls a
  day. Note quality varies; 18% of notes
  fail the monthly quality audit.

Proposed solution:
  Generative AI drafts a structured
  summary from the call transcript. The
  agent reviews and edits every draft
  before saving. Nothing is saved without
  the agent.

Users:
  400 agents (internal). Customers are
  affected indirectly through the notes
  used on their next call.

Value hypothesis (estimate to be tested):
  Save about 90 seconds per call x 24,000
  calls a day = 600 agent-hours a day.
  Cash benefit only if freed time avoids
  planned hiring (40 agents for 10% volume
  growth).

Measures:
  Primary:
    after-call work time per call
  Guardrails:
    CSAT stays at or above 82%; note audit
    pass rate at or above 90%
  Leading:
    share of drafts accepted without edits

Data needed:
  Call transcripts (available; owner: Head
  of Contact Center). Privacy officer to
  confirm use for this purpose is covered
  by the customer notice.

Risk tier (draft):
  Medium. Internal, human review of every
  draft, reversible; but it processes
  personal data at scale.

Sourcing (draft):
  Build on Amazon Bedrock with a partner,
  with handover to two internal engineers.
  No product fits our note format and
  systems, and we need the skills for
  later use cases.

Not AI?
  Partly. A shorter note template would
  save about 20 seconds; we will adopt it
  anyway. It doesn't remove the typing.

Next step:
  4-week baseline, then an 8-week pilot
  with 40 agents and a comparison group of
  40. Decision on rollout at the end of
  the pilot.

Notice what makes this brief strong:

  • Every number has a source.
  • The value is stated as a hypothesis, separating capacity from cash.
  • The "Not AI?" answer is honest and leads to an extra quick fix.
  • The next step ends in a decision.

Use case scoring matrix

UseTo prioritize several candidates consistently. Weights are agreed by the steering committee before scoring.

scoring-matrix 
Criteria and weights (must total 100%)
  Business value       {35}%
    savings, revenue, risk, experience
  Feasibility          {25}%
    data, complexity, skills, integration
  Sustainability       {15}%
    running cost, maintenance, lasting
    value
  Strategic alignment  {25}%
    fit with objectives and risk appetite

Score each 1 (low) to 5 (high), with a
one-line reason.

Candidate: {name}
  Value {4}   Feasibility {5}
  Sustainability {4}   Alignment {4}
  Weighted {4.25}   Group {quick win}

Candidate: {name}
  Value { }   Feasibility { }
  Sustainability { }   Alignment { }
  Weighted {    }   Group {    }

Weighted = Σ (score × weight)
Groups: quick win / strategic bet /
        nice to have / avoid

WatchScores support judgment; a mid-scoring idea with one fixable blocker may still be the right next step.

How to fill it

Criteria and weights

How to fill itAgree the four criteria and their weights with the steering committee before anyone scores, so weights reflect strategy, not favorite projects.

Common mistakeChanging weights after seeing the scores.

Score definitions

How to fill itWrite what 1, 3 and 5 mean for each criterion (for example, feasibility 5 = data available and joined, no new integration).

Common mistakeScores that mean different things to different people.

Scores

How to fill itScore as a group with business, technical and risk views, and write one line of reasoning per score.

Common mistakeOne person scoring alone, or scores with no reasons.

Weighted score

How to fill itMultiply each score by its weight and add them up.

Common mistakeSimple averages that ignore the agreed weights.

Group

How to fill itPlace each candidate as a quick win, strategic bet, nice to have or avoid, based on value and feasibility.

Common mistakeRanking only by the total and ignoring the mix of risk and timing.

Judgment check

How to fill itNote any fixable blocker that explains a low score, and whether a smaller version would score better.

Common mistakeTreating the score as the decision.

  later

What makes this example strong:

  • Weights were fixed before scoring.
  • Every score has a reason.
  • The mid-scoring chatbot gets a plan (agent-facing first) instead of a simple yes or no.

ROI worksheet

UseTo build the business case, then to check it against measured results after launch.

roi-worksheet 
Measured effect:
  {e.g. 1.3 min saved per call, pilot vs
  comparison group}
Volume:
  {e.g. 24,000 calls/day × 220 days}

Benefits per year
Time savings realized (cash only)
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }
Cost reduction
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }
Revenue (at margin)
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }
Losses avoided
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }

Costs per year
Build, partner or licences
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }
Usage (tokens, hosting)
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }
Data preparation
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }
Run team
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }
Training and change management
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }
Governance and monitoring
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }
Model upgrades and retesting
  Y1 {   }  Y2 {   }  Y3 {   }

Results
Total benefits (3 years):
  {   }
Total costs (3 years):
  {   }
ROI = (benefits − costs) ÷ costs
  {   }
Payback (cumulative benefits pass costs):
  {   }
ROI if benefits are 50% lower:
  {   }
Intangible benefits (reported, not
priced):
  {   }

WatchCount saved time as cash only when it reduces spending or produces valuable output; otherwise it is capacity.

How to fill it

Measured effect

How to fill itUse the effect measured against a comparison group in the pilot, not the vendor's claim or a before-and-after figure.

Common mistakeUsing the most optimistic number available.

Volume

How to fill itReal volumes from systems, with working days and expected growth.

Common mistakePeak volumes presented as normal.

Time savings realized

How to fill itOnly the part of saved time that turns into cash: avoided hiring, less overtime or outsourcing, or extra output that is sold.

Common mistakeCounting all saved hours at full salary.

Other benefits

How to fill itCost reduction, revenue at profit margin, and losses avoided, each with its source.

Common mistakeUsing revenue instead of margin.

Costs

How to fill itEvery category, every year: build, usage, data, run team, training, governance, upgrades.

Common mistakeLeaving out running and people costs after year 1.

Ramp-up

How to fill itReduce year 1 benefits for the time adoption takes.

Common mistakeAssuming full benefit from day one.

Sensitivity

How to fill itShow ROI if benefits are much lower, for example 50%.

Common mistakePresenting a single number as certain.

Intangibles

How to fill itList them separately, with how they are measured.

Common mistakeForcing them into dollar values.

Filled example

roi-worksheet: after-call summaries 
Measured effect:
  1.3 min saved per call (pilot vs
  comparison group)
Volume:
  24,000 calls/day × 220 days
Time freed:
  about 114,000 agent-hours a year (gross
  value about $4.0M: capacity, not cash)

Benefits per year (cash only)
Avoided hiring of 40 agents for growth
  Y1 $1,200,000 (50% ramp-up)
  Y2 $2,400,000
  Y3 $2,400,000

Costs per year
Build (partner project)
  Y1 $300,000   Y2 -          Y3 -
Usage (tokens)
  Y1 $85,000    Y2 $85,000    Y3 $85,000
Run team (2 people)
  Y1 $280,000   Y2 $280,000   Y3 $280,000
Training and change management
  Y1 $50,000    Y2 -          Y3 -
Governance and monitoring
  Y1 $40,000    Y2 $40,000    Y3 $40,000

Results
Total benefits (3 years):
  $6,000,000
Total costs (3 years):
  $1,565,000
ROI:
  (6,000,000 − 1,565,000) ÷ 1,565,000
  = about 283%
Payback:
  within year 1
ROI if benefits are 50% lower:
  about 92%
Intangible benefits:
  more time with complex callers; note
  quality (audit pass rate)

What makes this example strong:

  • It uses the measured 1.3 minutes, not the 1.6 a before-and-after comparison suggested.
  • Only avoided hiring is counted as cash; the $4.0 million gross time value is shown but not used.
  • All cost categories run for all three years, and the 50% test shows the decision doesn't depend on optimism.

KPI definition

UseFor every KPI in a business case, so before and after are measured the same way.

kpi-definition 
KPI:
  {name}
Definition:
  {exact calculation and scope}
Type:
  {tangible / intangible (proxy)}
  {leading / lagging}
Owner:
  {name}
Baseline:
  {value, period measured, seasonality
  noted}
Target:
  {value} by {date}
Data source:
  {system}, {frequency}
Guardrails:
  {metrics that must not get worse, e.g.
  CSAT}
Attribution:
  {comparison group / A-B test /
  before-after}

How to fill it

KPI and definition

How to fill itThe exact calculation and scope, precise enough that two people would get the same number.

Common mistakeDefinitions like "handling efficiency"

Type

How to fill itTangible or intangible (with its proxy), leading or lagging.

Common mistakeOnly lagging measures, so problems show up too late.

Owner

How to fill itOne named person who reports it and acts on it.

Common mistakeA team or a system as the owner.

Baseline

How to fill itThe measured value before any change, the period it covers, and any seasonality.

Common mistakeA baseline captured after the pilot started.

Target and date

How to fill itA realistic target with a date, based on the pilot.

Common mistakeA target with no date, or copied from a vendor claim.

Data source

How to fill itThe system and how often it is refreshed.

Common mistakeManual spreadsheets nobody maintains.

Guardrails

How to fill itMetrics that must not get worse while this one improves.

Common mistakeNo guardrails, so gains hide losses.

Attribution

How to fill itHow the AI's effect will be separated from other changes.

Common mistakeCrediting the AI with every improvement.

Filled example

kpi-definition: after-call work time 
KPI:
  Average after-call work time per call
Definition:
  Minutes from call end to "wrap-up
  complete" in the contact-center system,
  all inbound service calls, excluding
  calls transferred out
Type:
  Tangible; lagging (operational)
Owner:
  Head of Contact Center Operations
Baseline:
  6.2 minutes (4 weeks, February; no storm
  events)
Target:
  4.9 minutes by end of Q3 (all agents)
Data source:
  Contact-center system timestamps, daily
Guardrails:
  CSAT at or above 82%; note audit pass
  rate at or above 90%
Attribution:
  Pilot group vs comparison group of 40
  agents

  [ ] Staff willing to experiment
  [ ] Failures discussed openly
  [ ] Concerns about jobs addressed

  Score {1-5}   Evidence {   }

  Score {1-5}   Evidence {   }
  [ ] AI policy, inventory, risk-tiered
      review

  Score {1-5}   Evidence {   }

      practitioners

Test: could we deliver our top 3 use cases

  Blockers: {   }

  operational / systematic /

How to fill it

Scope

How to fill itWhole enterprise or one division, and the purpose: to decide investments, not to grade people.

Common mistakeRunning it as an audit, which makes answers defensive.

Evidence

How to fill itInterviews at several levels, surveys, reviews of data, systems and policies.

Common mistakeSelf-assessment by one executive.

Scores

How to fill itUse written definitions for each level; record the evidence next to each score.

Common mistakeScores without evidence.

Checklist items

How to fill itTick only what is in place and working, not what is planned.

Common mistakeTicking items that exist on paper only.

Use case test

How to fill itAsk whether the top three use cases could be delivered today, and record each blocker.

Common mistakeLeaving the scores abstract.

Maturity stage

How to fill itOverall stage, plus any function that is clearly ahead or behind.

Common mistakeOne stage for an organization that varies widely.

Governance and risk

AI policy (outline)

UseAs the organization's top-level statement on AI, approved by the steering committee or board.

ai-policy-outline 
1. Purpose and scope
   {which AI, which people, which
   entities}
2. Principles
   {fairness, explainability, privacy and
   security, safety, controllability,
   veracity and robustness, governance,
   transparency}
3. Prohibited uses
   {e.g. emotion recognition of staff;
   fully automated claim denials}
4. Risk appetite and tiers
   {definition of low / medium / high and
   the review each needs}
5. Roles
   {steering committee, review board, CoE,
   business owners, data owners}
6. Approved tools
   {link to the AI tool register}
7. Data rules
   {what data may be used in which tools}
8. Human oversight
   {where people must decide or review}
9. Transparency
   {disclosure to customers and staff}
10. Monitoring and incidents
   {requirements and reporting}
11. Training
   {AI literacy requirements by role}
12. Review
   {policy owner; review at least yearly}

How to fill it

Purpose and scope

How to fill itWhich AI it covers (built, bought and embedded features), which people (staff, contractors) and which entities.

Common mistakeCovering only systems built in-house.

Principles

How to fill itThe responsible AI dimensions the organization commits to, in plain language.

Common mistakePrinciples with no link to any process.

Prohibited uses

How to fill itSpecific uses that are never allowed, including legal prohibitions.

Common mistakeLeaving this empty or vague.

Risk tiers

How to fill itDefinitions of each tier and the review, approval and monitoring each needs.

Common mistakeOne process for every use.

Roles

How to fill itWho decides what: steering committee, review board, CoE, owners.

Common mistakeUnclear decision rights.

Data rules

How to fill itWhich data classifications may be used in which kinds of tools.

Common mistakeReferring to a data policy staff can't find.

Oversight and transparency

How to fill itWhere people must decide or review; when customers and staff are told AI is involved.

Common mistakePromising human review that isn't resourced.

Review

How to fill itAn owner and a review cycle of at least once a year.

Common mistakeA policy that never changes while AI does.

Filled example

   Low: owner self-assessment, registered
   Medium: review board approval

   steering committee approval

UseTo reduce shadow AI. Publish the register where every employee can find it.

ai-tool-register-entry 
Tool:
  {name and version}

  {approved / under evaluation / blocked}

  {1 consumer / 2 enterprise / 3
  pre-trained / 4 fine-tuned / 5
  self-trained}
Business owner:
  {name}
Allowed data:
  {public / internal / confidential /
  personal}
Allowed uses:
  {   }
Not allowed:
  {   }
Decision basis:
  {terms on data use, opt-out, data
  location, security review, cost, value}
Alternative:
  {approved tool to use instead, if
  blocked}

  {if under evaluation, who and until
  when}

  {date}

WatchEvery "blocked" entry needs an approved alternative, or use simply moves out of sight.

How to fill it

Tool and status

How to fill itThe exact product and version; one status only.

Common mistakeListing a vendor without naming the product.

Scoping Matrix

How to fill itConsumer app, enterprise app, pre-trained model, fine-tuned or self-trained; decides the depth of review.

Common mistakeTreating a consumer app like an enterprise contract.

Allowed data and uses

How to fill itData classifications permitted and specific allowed uses.

Common mistakeApproving a tool without stating what data may go into it.

Decision basis

How to fill itThe facts behind the decision: data-use terms, opt-out, location, security, cost, value.

Common mistakeDecisions with no stated reason.

Alternative

How to fill itFor blocked tools, the approved tool to use instead.

Common mistakeBlocking with no alternative.

Pilot group

How to fill itFor tools under evaluation, who may use them and until when.

Common mistakeOpen-ended evaluations.

Next review

How to fill itA date; tools and their terms change.

Common mistakeEntries never revisited.

Governance charter (AI steering committee)

UseTo set up the body that owns AI strategy, policy and high-risk approvals.

governance-charter 
Purpose:
  {direct AI strategy; approve policy and
  high-risk uses; resolve escalated
  trade-offs}
Chair:
  {executive sponsor}
Members:
  {business unit leaders, CIO/CTO, chief
  risk officer, general counsel, privacy
  officer, CISO, HR, customer experience}
Decides:
  {AI strategy and investment priorities;
  AI policy; high-risk approvals; risk
  appetite}
Delegates:
  {medium risk to the review board; low
  risk to business owners with
  registration}
Inputs:
  {portfolio dashboard, risk reports,
  incidents}
Outputs:
  {decision records with conditions and
  owners}
Cadence:
  {monthly}
Reports to:
  {board risk committee}, {quarterly}
Quorum and escalation:
  {   }

How to fill it

Purpose

How to fill itWhat the committee directs and decides, in one or two lines.

Common mistakeA purpose so broad it overlaps every other body.

Chair and members

How to fill itThe executive sponsor as chair; every function with a stake, including legal, risk, privacy, security and HR.

Common mistakeOnly technology leaders.

Decides and delegates

How to fill itWhat it decides itself, and what it delegates to the review board and owners.

Common mistakeApproving every AI use itself, which creates a bottleneck.

Inputs and outputs

How to fill itThe reports it receives; the decision records it produces.

Common mistakeMeetings with no recorded decisions.

Cadence and reporting

How to fill itHow often it meets and how it reports to the board.

Risk classification questionnaire

UseAt the idea stage, and again whenever the system changes. The highest-risk answer usually sets the tier.

risk-classification 
1. Effect on people Could output affect
   someone's finances, health, job, rights
   or safety?
   {no / some / significant}
2. Autonomy Does the AI suggest, act with
   approval, or act alone?
   {suggest / approve / act}
3. Data Public, internal, personal or
   special-category?
   {   }
4. Audience Internal staff, or customers
   and public?
   {   }
5. Scale Decisions or interactions per
   day?
   {   }
6. Reversibility Are errors easy to spot
   and undo?
   {yes / no}
7. Regulation Named in AI-specific law
   (e.g. EU AI Act high-risk) or a closely
   regulated sector?
   {   }
8. Prohibited Does it match any prohibited
   use?
   {yes / no}

Tier:
  {prohibited / high / medium / low}
Review path:
  {   }
Re-classify if:
  {new data, customer-facing, more
  autonomy, new country, new law}

How to fill it

Effect on people

How to fill itConsider everyone affected by the output, not just users.

Common mistakeAnswering only for the user.

Autonomy

How to fill itWhat the system can do without a person approving.

Common mistakeIgnoring planned increases in autonomy.

Data and audience

How to fill itThe most sensitive data involved; internal or public.

Common mistakeAssuming internal tools carry no data risk.

Scale and reversibility

How to fill itVolume of decisions, and how easily errors are found and undone.

Common mistakeIgnoring scale when each error seems small.

Regulation

How to fill itCheck AI-specific law (for example, EU AI Act high-risk areas) and sector rules.

Common mistakeAssuming no AI law means no regulation.

Tier

How to fill itThe highest-risk answer usually sets the tier; record the reasoning.

Common mistakeAveraging answers to get a lower tier.

Re-classification triggers

How to fill itThe changes that would move the tier.

Common mistakeClassifying once and never again.

Filled example

risk-classification: fraud scoring model 
1. Effect on people significant: delays
   payment of honest customers' claims
2. Autonomy suggest; investigators decide
3. Data personal and financial data
4. Audience internal, but affects
   customers
5. Scale about 10,000 claims a month
6. Reversibility partly; delays can't be
   undone
7. Regulation insurance and
   anti-discrimination rules; the EU AI
   Act credit-scoring category excludes
   fraud detection
8. Prohibited no

Tier:
  HIGH
Reason:
  significant effect on customers at
  scale, with fairness risk across regions
Review path:
  full review, impact assessment, steering
  committee approval
Re-classify if:
  new data sources, any automatic denial,
  expansion to new countries

What makes this example strong:

  • It considers customers affected by the output, not just the investigators who use it.
  • It records why the EU AI Act category doesn't apply.
  • It still classifies as high on impact and scale.

UseAgreed before building; the system is released only when they are met.

release-criteria

  {name}
Tier and approver:
  {   }

Veracity
  {correct answers on test set}
  ≥ {99%}
Robustness
  {accuracy on unusual inputs}
  ≥ {   }

  {gap between groups or languages}
  < {2 points}

  {data disclosures in attack tests}
  = {0}
Safety
  {harmful outputs in red-team tests}
  = {0}

  {answers with sources}
  = {100%}
Controllability
  {time to switch off}
  < {15 min}
Transparency
  {AI disclosure shown}

How to fill it

System, tier, approver

How to fill itWho signs off, matching the tier.

Common mistakeBuilders approving their own release.

Dimensions

How to fill itAt least one criterion per relevant responsible AI dimension.

Common mistakeTesting accuracy only.

Metric

How to fill itA measurable test with a defined test set (size, source, languages, groups).

Common mistakeMetrics with no test set behind them.

Threshold

How to fill itSet before building, based on the risk and today's human performance.

Common mistakeSetting thresholds after seeing results.

If not met

How to fill itThe pre-agreed response: narrow scope, delay or add review.

Common mistakeLowering the threshold to pass.

Vendor evaluation questionnaire

UseBefore buying an AI product or engaging an AI partner.

vendor-questionnaire 
Evidence
  Tested on our data? Results we can
  verify?
Data use
  Is our data used to train or improve
  models? Can we opt out? Shared with
  third parties? Where is it stored and
  processed?
Models
  Which models does it use? What happens
  when they change or are retired?
Compliance
  Certifications and independent audit
  reports (e.g. ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC
  27001)? Support for our duties as
  deployer?
Intellectual property
  Who owns outputs and custom models? Is
  there IP indemnity?
Security
  Access controls, encryption, logging,
  incident notification?
Responsible AI
  Fairness testing? Explanations?
  Guardrails?
Cost
  Pricing model? Cost at 3× today's
  volume?
Exit
  Export of data, prompts and
  configuration? Notice periods and
  transition support?

How to fill it

Evidence

How to fill itAsk for results on your own data, or a trial that produces them.

Common mistakeAccepting results from another customer's data.

Data use

How to fill itGet the answer in the contract, not only in marketing material.

Common mistakeRelying on a sales statement.

Models

How to fill itWhich models, how changes are notified, and retesting support.

Common mistakeIgnoring model changes and retirements.

Compliance

How to fill itCertificates with their scope, and audit reports.

Common mistakeA logo on a website as evidence.

IP

How to fill itOwnership of outputs and custom models; indemnity terms.

Common mistakeAssuming you own whatever is generated.

Cost and exit

How to fill itCost at three times today's volume; how to leave with your data.

Common mistakeComparing first-year prices only.

Assessment

How to fill itFor each area: acceptable, acceptable with conditions, or a deal-breaker.

Common mistakeCollecting answers without judging them.

Filled example

Evidence
  Answer: "20% fewer defaults" from
  another bank
  Assessment: NOT ACCEPTABLE until tested
  on our applicants in a 3-month trial
Data use
  Answer: our data improves their shared
  model; opt-out costs extra
  Assessment: CONDITION, opt-out required
  in contract
Models

  notice
  Assessment: CONDITION, 60 days' notice
  and retest results before each change
Compliance

  management certificate; limited
  documentation for deployers
  Assessment: GAP, high-risk obligations
  need full documentation
Explainability

  per-decision explanation
  Assessment: DEAL-BREAKER for declines
Exit
  Answer: data export on request; 12-month
  notice
  Assessment: acceptable

Decision:
  do not buy; build an explainable model

What makes this example strong:

  • Each answer is judged, not just recorded.
  • The decisive issue (no per-decision explanations for a high-risk use) is clearly marked as a deal-breaker.

How to fill it

Metric

How to fill itOne specific, measurable signal.

AI incident runbook

UsePrepared and rehearsed before go-live, for each production AI system.

incident-runbook 
System:
  {name}
Business owner:
  {   }
On call:
  {   }

1. Detect
   Sources: {monitoring alerts, staff,
   customers}
2. Contain
   Switch-off: {how; who decides; < 15
   min}
   Fallback: {manual process / previous
   version}
3. Assess Who was affected, how, since
   when?
   Logs: {where; who can access}
4. Notify Legal decides on regulator and
   customer notice
   Inform: {review board, sponsor,
   communications}
5. Remediate Fix the root cause; correct
   affected decisions
6. Learn Blameless review within {5} days
   Which control should have caught it?
   Actions and owners: {   }

How to fill it

System and contacts

How to fill itOwner and on-call contact who can be reached at any time.

Common mistakeContacts who are on holiday or have left.

Contain

How to fill itExactly how to switch off or narrow the system, who may decide, and the target time.

Common mistakeA switch-off that has never been tested.

Fallback

How to fill itThe process staff use while the AI is off.

Common mistakeNo fallback, so the business stops.

Assess

How to fill itWhere logs are and who can use them to find affected people.

Common mistakeLogs not kept, or kept but inaccessible.

Notify

How to fill itLegal's role, and who else is told.

Common mistakeNotifying too late, or deciding legal duties without legal.

Learn

How to fill itA blameless review with actions and owners.

Common mistakeBlaming individuals; no change to controls.

Escalation criteria

UseFor any assistant or agent: when the AI must hand over to a person.

escalation-criteria 
Hand over to a person when:
  - the user asks for a person
  - the topic is {decisions reserved for
    people, e.g. denials}
  - safeguards block an answer {2} times
    in one conversation
  - confidence is below {threshold}
  - the user shows signs of vulnerability
    or makes a complaint
  - identity can't be verified
  - an action exceeds {limit, e.g. $40 a
    day}
  - there is no resolution after {10}
    turns
Hand over with:
  {summary, verified identity, documents}
Target time to a person:
  {   }

How to fill it

Triggers

How to fill itSpecific, detectable conditions the system can enforce.

Common mistakeVague triggers like "when needed"

Reserved decisions

How to fill itTopics that always go to people because of impact or law.

Common mistakeLetting the AI decide anything it can.

Vulnerability

How to fill itWords and situations that signal extra care is needed.

Common mistakeTreating every customer the same.

Handover content

How to fill itWhat the person receives, so the customer doesn't repeat themselves.

Common mistakeHandovers that start from zero.

Target time

How to fill itHow fast a person must take over.

Common mistakeEscalation into a long queue.

Filled example

escalation-criteria: customer assistant 
Hand over to a human agent when:
  - the customer asks for a person, at any
    point
  - the question concerns a coverage
    decision or a claim denial
  - the grounding check blocks an answer
    twice in one conversation
  - the customer mentions bereavement,
    illness, financial hardship or a
    complaint
  - the customer's identity can't be
    verified
  - there is no resolution after 10 turns
Hand over with:
  conversation summary, verified identity,
  documents already provided
Target time to human:
  under 2 minutes, 08:00 to 20:00;
  callback offered outside those hours

Change communication message

UseTo announce an AI change to affected staff, delivered by their own managers, well before go-live.

change-message 
What is changing:
  {tool, who, from when}
What stays human:
  {decisions and reviews people keep}
Why:
  {the problem and realistic expected
  outcome}
Your job:
  {honest statement of effects on roles
  and the commitments the company makes}
Monitoring:
  {what is measured; what isn't used to
  rate people}
Support:
  {training dates, champion, help channel}
Your voice:
  {how to give feedback; Q&A sessions}
What happens next:
  {when results will be shared; decision
  points}

WatchOnly make commitments leadership can keep; a broken promise undermines every later change.

How to fill it

What is changing

How to fill itTool, who is affected and from when, in plain words.

Common mistakeTechnical descriptions.

What stays human

How to fill itThe decisions and reviews people keep.

Common mistakeLeaving people to assume the worst.

Why

How to fill itThe problem and a realistic expected outcome.

Common mistakeOver-promising results.

Your job

How to fill itAn honest statement on roles and the commitments made.

Common mistakeAvoiding the question, or promising "nothing will change"

Monitoring

How to fill itWhat is measured, and what isn't used to rate people.

Common mistakeSilence, which feeds fear of surveillance.

Support and voice

How to fill itTraining, champions, feedback channels.

Common mistakeOne-way announcements.

What happens next

How to fill itWhen results are shared and decisions made.

Common mistakeNo follow-up.

Filled example

change-message: after-call summary pilot 
What is changing:
  From 3 March, 40 agents in two teams
  will pilot a tool that drafts after-call
  notes.
What stays human:
  You review and edit every draft. Nothing
  is saved without you.
Why:
  Wrap-up takes about 6 minutes per call.
  We expect to save around a minute, time
  we want you to spend on customers who
  need more help.
Your job:
  No agent will lose their job because of

  expect to hire fewer new agents than we
  otherwise would.
Monitoring:
  We measure how the tool performs. We
  won't rate individual agents on how
  often they edit drafts.
Support:
  Two-hour training before the pilot; your
  team champion; help channel
  #agent-assist.

  Q&A every Thursday; feedback button on

How to fill it

Purpose

How to fill itOne specific purpose; anything else needs a new agreement.

Common mistakeBroad purposes such as "analytics"

Data in scope

How to fill itExact fields, minimized, anonymized or aggregated where possible.

Common mistakeSharing whole tables "in case"

How to fill itHow sharing happens; prefer methods where raw data isn't exchanged.

Common mistakeEmailing extracts.

Uses

How to fill itPermitted and prohibited, including whether data may train models.

Common mistakeSilence on model training.

Ownership

How to fill itWho owns results, insights and any models.

Common mistakeDisputes discovered later.

Compliance and audit

How to fill itLawful basis, residency, logs and audit rights.

Common mistakeNo way to check the partner.

Termination

How to fill itWhat happens to data and results at the end.

Common mistakeData kept forever.
