Templates

Fill-in templates for the documents an AI business strategist produces, from the first use case brief to the production readiness review. Each shows the fields to capture, with a hint in italics for what goes in each, a field-by-field guide to filling it in, and a completed example. They also make a good revision tool: if you can fill one in for a scenario, you understand that part of the exam.

Use case brief

UseAt the envision stage, to propose a use case on one page so it can be compared with others.

Use case brief
Use case
Short name for the task, in business language
Business owner
Name and role of the person accountable for the outcome
Objective served
Which stated business objective it moves
Problem today
What is slow, costly or risky, with measured numbers
Proposed solution
What the AI will do (rules, ML, generative AI or agent) and what stays with people
Users
Who uses it, how many; internal or customer-facing
Value hypothesis
For example: save 90 seconds on 24,000 calls a day
Measures
Primary KPI, guardrail metrics, leading indicator
Data needed
Sources; available? permitted for this use?
Risk tier (draft)
Low, medium or high, and why
Sourcing (draft)
Build, buy or partner
Not AI?
Could rules, a process change or a dashboard solve it instead?
Next step
For example: a 6-week proof of concept

WatchA brief without a business owner, a baseline number or a "could this be solved without AI?" answer isn't ready to compare.

How to fill it

Use case

How to fill itA short name for the task the AI will do, in business language: "after-call summaries", not "LLM pilot"

Common mistakeNaming a technology instead of a task.

Business owner

How to fill itOne named business leader who will answer for the result and has authority over the process that changes.

Common mistake"The AI team", or two co-owners.

Objective served

How to fill itQuote the stated business objective it moves. If it doesn't move one, say so; that is useful information.

Common mistakeVague goals such as "innovation" or "being AI-first"

Problem today

How to fill itWhat is slow, costly or risky now, with measured numbers and their source. If you have no numbers, getting them is the first step.

Common mistakeEstimates from memory ("it takes ages").

Proposed solution

How to fill itWhat the AI will do, the solution type (rules, ML, generative AI, agent), and what stays with people.

Common mistakeChoosing the most advanced type by default.

Users

How to fill itWho uses it, how many, and whether it is internal or customer-facing. The audience strongly affects the risk tier.

Common mistakeForgetting the people affected by its output, not only those who use it.

Value hypothesis

How to fill itA testable statement: effect per case × volume × value, with the assumption labeled as an estimate to be tested.

Common mistakeCounting all time saved as cash.

Measures

How to fill itOne primary business KPI, one or two guardrail metrics, and a leading indicator for the pilot.

Common mistakeModel accuracy as the only measure.

Data needed

How to fill itEach source, whether it exists digitally, its quality, who owns it, and whether its use for this purpose is permitted.

Common mistakeAssuming data collected for one purpose can be used for another.

Risk tier (draft)

How to fill itA first view using impact on people, autonomy, data sensitivity, audience, scale, reversibility and regulation. Governance confirms it.

Common mistakeClassifying by technology rather than use.

Sourcing (draft)

How to fill itBuild, buy or partner, with one line of reasoning (differentiation, timeline, skills, data, compliance).

Common mistakeDeciding before checking whether a product already does this.

Not AI?

How to fill itAn honest answer: could a rule, a process change or a dashboard solve most of the problem?

Common mistakeLeaving it blank.

Next step

How to fill itThe smallest step that tests the riskiest assumption, with a duration and a decision at the end.

Common mistake"Build it", with no end point.

Filled example

Use case brief: after-call summaries
Use case
After-call summaries for contact-center agents
Business owner
Head of Contact Center Operations
Objective served
Reduce contact-center cost per call by 15% without lowering customer satisfaction
Problem today
After-call work averages 6.2 minutes per call (4-week measurement from the contact-center system), on 24,000 calls a day. Note quality varies; 18% of notes fail the monthly quality audit.
Proposed solution
Generative AI drafts a structured summary from the call transcript. The agent reviews and edits every draft before saving; nothing is saved without the agent.
Users
400 agents (internal). Customers are affected indirectly through the notes used on their next call.
Value hypothesis (estimate to be tested)
Save about 90 seconds per call × 24,000 calls a day = 600 agent-hours a day. Cash benefit only if freed time avoids planned hiring (40 agents for 10% volume growth).
Measures
Primary: after-call work time per call. Guardrails: customer satisfaction stays at or above 82%; note audit pass rate at or above 90%. Leading: share of drafts accepted without edits.
Data needed
Call transcripts (available; owner: Head of Contact Center). The privacy officer confirms this use is covered by the customer notice.
Risk tier (draft)
Medium. Internal, human review of every draft, reversible; but it processes personal data at scale.
Sourcing (draft)
Build on Amazon Bedrock with a partner, with handover to two internal engineers. No product fits our note format and systems, and we need the skills for later use cases.
Not AI?
Partly. A shorter note template would save about 20 seconds; we will adopt it anyway. It doesn't remove the typing.
Next step
4-week baseline, then an 8-week pilot with 40 agents and a comparison group of 40. Decision on rollout at the end of the pilot.

Notice what makes this brief strong:

  • Every number has a source.
  • The value is stated as a hypothesis, separating capacity from cash.
  • The "Not AI?" answer is honest and leads to an extra quick fix.
  • The next step ends in a decision.

Sustainability

Strategic alignment
Weight, for example 25%: fit with objectives and risk appetite

Candidate
Name
Scores (1 to 5)

Weighted score

Group

WatchScores support judgment; a mid-scoring idea with one fixable blocker may still be the right next step.

How to fill it

Criteria and weights

How to fill itAgree the four criteria and their weights with the steering committee before anyone scores, so weights reflect strategy, not favorite projects.

Common mistakeChanging weights after seeing the scores.

Score definitions

How to fill itWrite what 1, 3 and 5 mean for each criterion (for example, feasibility 5 = data available and joined, no new integration).

Common mistakeScores that mean different things to different people.

Scores

How to fill itScore as a group with business, technical and risk views, and write one line of reasoning per score.

Common mistakeOne person scoring alone, or scores with no reasons.

Weighted score

How to fill itMultiply each score by its weight and add them up.

Common mistakeSimple averages that ignore the agreed weights.

Group

How to fill itPlace each candidate as a quick win, strategic bet, nice to have or avoid, based on value and feasibility.

Common mistakeRanking only by the total and ignoring the mix of risk and timing.

Judgment check

How to fill itNote any fixable blocker that explains a low score, and whether a smaller version would score better.

Common mistakeTreating the score as the decision.

WatchCount saved time as cash only when it reduces spending or produces valuable output; otherwise it is capacity.

How to fill it

Measured effect

How to fill itUse the effect measured against a comparison group in the pilot, not the vendor's claim or a before-and-after figure.

Common mistakeUsing the most optimistic number available.

Volume

How to fill itReal volumes from systems, with working days and expected growth.

Common mistakePeak volumes presented as normal.

Time savings realized

How to fill itOnly the part of saved time that turns into cash: avoided hiring, less overtime or outsourcing, or extra output that is sold.

Common mistakeCounting all saved hours at full salary.

Other benefits

How to fill itCost reduction, revenue at profit margin, and losses avoided, each with its source.

Common mistakeUsing revenue instead of margin.

Costs

How to fill itEvery category, every year: build, usage, data, run team, training, governance, upgrades.

Common mistakeLeaving out running and people costs after year 1.

Ramp-up

How to fill itReduce year 1 benefits for the time adoption takes.

Common mistakeAssuming full benefit from day one.

Sensitivity

How to fill itShow ROI if benefits are much lower, for example 50%.

Common mistakePresenting a single number as certain.

Intangibles

How to fill itList them separately, with how they are measured.

Common mistakeForcing them into dollar values.

UseFor every KPI in a business case, so before and after are measured the same way.

KPI definition
KPI
Name
Definition
Exact calculation and scope
Type
Tangible or intangible (proxy); leading or lagging
Owner
Name
Baseline
Value, period measured, seasonality noted
Target
Value, and the date by when
Data source
System and frequency
Guardrails
Metrics that must not get worse, for example customer satisfaction
Attribution
Comparison group, A/B test or before-and-after

How to fill it

KPI and definition

How to fill itThe exact calculation and scope, precise enough that two people would get the same number.

Common mistakeDefinitions like "handling efficiency"

Type

How to fill itTangible or intangible (with its proxy), leading or lagging.

Common mistakeOnly lagging measures, so problems show up too late.

Owner

How to fill itOne named person who reports it and acts on it.

Common mistakeA team or a system as the owner.

Baseline

How to fill itThe measured value before any change, the period it covers, and any seasonality.

Common mistakeA baseline captured after the pilot started.

Target and date

How to fill itA realistic target with a date, based on the pilot.

Common mistakeA target with no date, or copied from a vendor claim.

Data source

How to fill itThe system and how often it is refreshed.

Common mistakeManual spreadsheets nobody maintains.

Guardrails

How to fill itMetrics that must not get worse while this one improves.

Common mistakeNo guardrails, so gains hide losses.

Attribution

How to fill itHow the AI's effect will be separated from other changes.

Common mistakeCrediting the AI with every improvement.

Readiness assessment checklist

UseBefore setting AI ambitions, and yearly after that. Score each dimension 1 to 5 with evidence.

Readiness assessment checklist
Leadership alignment
  • Score 1 to 5, with evidence
  • ☐ AI objectives in the business strategy
  • ☐ Named executive sponsor with budget
  • ☐ Leaders agree on priorities
Data quality and access
  • Score 1 to 5, with evidence
  • ☐ Owners named for key data
  • ☐ Key data sets can be joined and accessed in weeks, not months
  • ☐ Permission to use data for AI checked
Cultural preparedness
  • Score 1 to 5, with evidence
  • ☐ Staff willing to experiment
  • ☐ Failures discussed openly
  • ☐ Concerns about jobs addressed
Technical infrastructure
  • Score 1 to 5, with evidence
  • ☐ Platform to build, deploy, integrate and monitor AI
  • ☐ Core systems reachable through APIs
Governance frameworks
  • Score 1 to 5, with evidence
  • ☐ AI policy, inventory and risk-tiered review
Skills and talent
  • Score 1 to 5, with evidence
  • ☐ AI literacy program; core practitioners
  • ☐ Not dependent on one person or vendor
Test against use cases
Could we deliver our top three use cases today? List the blockers.
Maturity stage
Awareness, experimentation, operational, systematic or transformational

How to fill it

Scope

How to fill itWhole enterprise or one division, and the purpose: to decide investments, not to grade people.

Common mistakeRunning it as an audit, which makes answers defensive.

Evidence

How to fill itInterviews at several levels, surveys, reviews of data, systems and policies.

Common mistakeSelf-assessment by one executive.

Scores

How to fill itUse written definitions for each level; record the evidence next to each score.

Common mistakeScores without evidence.

Checklist items

How to fill itTick only what is in place and working, not what is planned.

Common mistakeTicking items that exist on paper only.

Use case test

How to fill itAsk whether the top three use cases could be delivered today, and record each blocker.

Common mistakeLeaving the scores abstract.

Maturity stage

How to fill itOverall stage, plus any function that is clearly ahead or behind.

Common mistakeOne stage for an organization that varies widely.

  • The use case test turns scores into concrete blockers.

Governance and risk

AI policy (outline)

UseAs the organization's top-level statement on AI, approved by the steering committee or board.

AI policy outline
1. Purpose and scope
Which AI, which people, which entities
2. Principles
Fairness, explainability, privacy and security, safety, controllability, veracity and robustness, governance, transparency
3. Prohibited uses
For example: emotion recognition of staff; loan rejections decided solely by AI
4. Risk appetite and tiers
Definition of low, medium and high, and the review each needs
5. Roles
Steering committee, review board, CoE, business owners, data owners
6. Approved tools
Link to the AI tool register
7. Data rules
What data may be used in which tools
8. Human oversight
Where people must decide or review
9. Transparency
Disclosure to customers and staff
10. Monitoring and incidents
Requirements and reporting
11. Training
AI literacy requirements by role
12. Review
Policy owner; review at least yearly

How to fill it

Purpose and scope

How to fill itWhich AI it covers (built, bought and embedded features), which people (staff, contractors) and which entities.

Common mistakeCovering only systems built in-house.

Principles

How to fill itThe responsible AI dimensions the organization commits to, in plain language.

Common mistakePrinciples with no link to any process.

Prohibited uses

How to fill itSpecific uses that are never allowed, including legal prohibitions.

Common mistakeLeaving this empty or vague.

Risk tiers

How to fill itDefinitions of each tier and the review, approval and monitoring each needs.

Common mistakeOne process for every use.

Roles

How to fill itWho decides what: steering committee, review board, CoE, owners.

Common mistakeUnclear decision rights.

Data rules

How to fill itWhich data classifications may be used in which kinds of tools.

Common mistakeReferring to a data policy staff can't find.

Oversight and transparency

How to fill itWhere people must decide or review; when customers and staff are told AI is involved.

Common mistakePromising human review that isn't resourced.

Review

How to fill itAn owner and a review cycle of at least once a year.

Common mistakeA policy that never changes while AI does.

How to fill it

Purpose

How to fill itWhat the committee directs and decides, in one or two lines.

Common mistakeA purpose so broad it overlaps every other body.

Chair and members

How to fill itThe executive sponsor as chair; every function with a stake, including legal, risk, privacy, security and HR.

Common mistakeOnly technology leaders.

Decides and delegates

How to fill itWhat it decides itself, and what it delegates to the review board and owners.

Common mistakeApproving every AI use itself, which creates a bottleneck.

Inputs and outputs

How to fill itThe reports it receives; the decision records it produces.

Common mistakeMeetings with no recorded decisions.

Cadence and reporting

How to fill itHow often it meets and how it reports to the board.

Common mistakeNo line to the board.

Escalation

How to fill itHow disputed trade-offs reach it, and how fast.

Common mistakeNo defined route, so disputes stall projects.

Risk classification questionnaire

UseAt the idea stage, and again whenever the system changes. The highest-risk answer usually sets the tier.

Risk classification questionnaire
1. Effect on people
Could output affect someone's finances, health, job, rights or safety? No, some or significant
2. Autonomy
Does the AI suggest, act with approval, or act alone?
3. Data
Public, internal, personal or special-category?
4. Audience
Internal staff, or customers and the public?
5. Scale
Decisions or interactions per day
6. Reversibility
Are errors easy to spot and undo?
7. Regulation
Named in AI-specific law (for example, EU AI Act high-risk) or in a closely regulated sector?
8. Prohibited
Does it match any prohibited use?
Tier
Prohibited, high, medium or low
Review path
The review the tier requires
Re-classify if
New data, customer-facing use, more autonomy, a new country, a new law

How to fill it

Effect on people

How to fill itConsider everyone affected by the output, not just users.

Common mistakeAnswering only for the user.

Autonomy

How to fill itWhat the system can do without a person approving.

Common mistakeIgnoring planned increases in autonomy.

Data and audience

How to fill itThe most sensitive data involved; internal or public.

Common mistakeAssuming internal tools carry no data risk.

Scale and reversibility

How to fill itVolume of decisions, and how easily errors are found and undone.

Common mistakeIgnoring scale when each error seems small.

Regulation

How to fill itCheck AI-specific law (for example, EU AI Act high-risk areas) and sector rules.

Common mistakeAssuming no AI law means no regulation.

Tier

How to fill itThe highest-risk answer usually sets the tier; record the reasoning.

Common mistakeAveraging answers to get a lower tier.

Re-classification triggers

How to fill itThe changes that would move the tier.

Common mistakeClassifying once and never again.

Filled example

Risk classification: card fraud scoring model
1. Effect on people
Significant: blocks genuine payments at the checkout
2. Autonomy

4. Audience

6. Reversibility

8. Prohibited

  • It records why the EU AI Act category doesn't apply.
  • It still classifies as high on impact and scale: the bank's own tier is stricter than the law.

Escalation criteria

UseFor any assistant or agent: when the AI must hand over to a person.

Escalation criteria
Hand over to a person when
  • the user asks for a person
  • the topic is a decision reserved for people (for example, loan rejections)
  • safeguards block an answer a set number of times (for example, twice) in one conversation
  • confidence is below a set threshold
  • the user shows signs of vulnerability or makes a complaint
  • identity can't be verified
  • an action exceeds a limit (for example, $40 a day)
  • there is no resolution after a set number of turns (for example, 10)
Hand over with
Summary, verified identity, documents
Target time to a person
For example, under 2 minutes during opening hours

How to fill it

Triggers

How to fill itSpecific, detectable conditions the system can enforce.

Common mistakeVague triggers like "when needed"

Reserved decisions

How to fill itTopics that always go to people because of impact or law.

Common mistakeLetting the AI decide anything it can.

Vulnerability

How to fill itWords and situations that signal extra care is needed.

Common mistakeTreating every customer the same.

Handover content

How to fill itWhat the person receives, so the customer doesn't repeat themselves.

Common mistakeHandovers that start from zero.

Target time

How to fill itHow fast a person must take over.

Common mistakeEscalation into a long queue.

Filled example

Escalation criteria: bank's website assistant
Hand over to a staff member when
  • the customer asks for a person, at any point
  • the question concerns a loan decision, a disputed payment or a refund
  • the grounding check blocks an answer twice in one conversation
  • the customer mentions bereavement, illness, financial hardship or a complaint
  • the customer's identity can't be verified
  • there is no resolution after 10 turns
Hand over with
Conversation summary, verified identity, documents already provided
Target time to a person
Under 2 minutes, 08:00 to 20:00; a callback offered outside those hours

Filled example

Data sharing agreement: retailer and manufacturer campaign measurement
1. Parties and purpose
The retailer and a consumer goods manufacturer; measuring whether the manufacturer's online ads led to purchases in the retailer's stores
