Domain 1: AI literacy at Harbor Mutual

Domain 1 is about understanding what AI is, choosing the right kind of solution, and keeping it trustworthy. At Harbor Mutual this shows up in the fraud model (predictions and thresholds), claims documents (data types), data quality, the split between rules and AI in the claims process, an overnight claims agent, monitoring for drift, shadow AI, and the prompts, token costs, retrieval and fine-tuning behind its generative AI assistants.

The fraud model in the five terms

Harbor Mutual's fraud model shows each of the Task 1.1 terms in one system:

Algorithm and training

The data science team chose a standard learning method and trained it on five years of claims, about 600,000, each labeled "fraud confirmed" or "legitimate" from past investigations. The features include the claim amount, the days since the policy started, the number of past claims and the time of day the claim was reported.

Model, inference and prediction

Training produced the fraud model. Every new claim is scored as it is registered (real-time inference), and each prediction is a probability: "this claim is 82% likely to be fraudulent".

Threshold

Claims above the threshold go to an investigator; the rest continue normally. Where to set the threshold is a business decision, worked through below.

Choosing the fraud threshold

Harbor Mutual receives about 10,000 claims a month, and about 2% (200) are fraudulent. An investigation costs about $150; an average fraudulent claim would pay out about $8,000. The data science team offers two thresholds for the same model:

MeasureThreshold 70%Threshold 50%
Claims flagged300600
Frauds caught (recall)160 of 200 (80%)185 of 200 (92.5%)
Honest customers flagged140 (precision 53%)415 (precision 31%)
Investigation cost300 × $150 = $45,000600 × $150 = $90,000
Fraud paid out (misses)40 × $8,000 = $320,00015 × $8,000 = $120,000
Total cost of the two errors$365,000$210,000

The lower threshold saves $155,000 a month, but delays 415 honest customers instead of 140, and investigators need double the capacity. Insurance regulators may also set limits on how long claims can be delayed. The Head of Claims, as business owner, decides: the 50% threshold, with two extra investigators funded from the savings and a target to clear flagged claims within two days.

One missing field: the branch that never recorded outcomes

One branch never recorded whether its investigated claims were fraud, so its roughly 3,000 claims a year all appear as "legitimate" in the training data:

  1. Wrong labelsFraudulent claims from that branch are labeled legitimate.
  2. Wrong lessonClaims resembling that branch's profile get low fraud scores, wherever they come from.
  3. Invisible in testingThe test data has the same gap, so the model looks accurate.
  4. The fixRecover the outcomes from investigators' files, correct the labels, retrain, and make outcome recording mandatory in every branch.

The decision for leadership isn't a technical one: it is to make outcome recording part of every investigator's process, with the Head of Claims as data owner.

Rules or AI in the claims process

One claim passes through several steps, and each needs a different kind of solution:

Check that the policy was active on the date of loss: rules

A yes/no fact in the policy system. It must be exact and auditable, and there is nothing to learn.

Pay small windscreen (glass) claims automatically: rules

Clear limits set by the business, easy to explain to regulators.

Read the customer's description and the repair estimate: AI

Unstructured, varied documents that rules can't parse: document extraction and generative AI.

Score the claim for fraud: AI

Hundreds of interacting signals, and fraud patterns keep changing: a machine learning model.

Decide whether to deny a claim: a person, supported by AI

High impact on the customer, and regulators expect an accountable human decision.

The fraud rules also show when rules stop scaling. Harbor Mutual started with about 20 fraud rules; over eight years they grew to about 400, written by different people. Some contradict each other, nobody knows which still work, and fraudsters learned to stay just under each limit. That is the business case for replacing most of them with a fraud model, keeping a few hard rules as limits around it.

An agent for claims reported at night

About 15% of claims are reported outside office hours and wait until morning to be registered. A claims-intake agent can handle them immediately. A customer messages at 11 p.m.: "Someone hit my parked car this evening, the rear bumper is smashed."

  1. GoalRegister the claim and arrange repair.
  2. Tool callLook up the policy by the customer's phone number (lookup_policy).Auto policy AP-77812: active, collision cover, $500 deductible.
  3. Tool callAsk the customer for photos of the damage (request_photos).Three photos received.
  4. Tool callOpen a collision claim on the policy (create_claim).Claim CL-55120 opened.
  5. Tool callFind approved repair shops near the customer (find_repair_shops).Three approved shops nearby.
  6. Reply"Your claim CL-55120 is open. Your $500 deductible applies. Here are three approved shops near you."

The design decisions are business decisions:

  • Tools: the agent can look up policies, request photos, open claims and find repair shops, but has no payment tool.
  • Autonomy: it acts within limits; it can book a rental car up to $40 a day, and anything above goes to a person.
  • Handover: if the policy isn't active or the customer is injured, it hands over to a person.
  • Coordination: for complex home claims, a supervisor agent coordinates an intake agent, a coverage agent and a fraud agent, and passes a summary to an adjuster, who alone decides payment.

Monitoring the fraud model

Fraudsters adapt, so the fraud model is a textbook case of concept drift: signals that predicted fraud last year stop working. Harbor Mutual sets up AWS's open-source endpoint monitoring solution:

  1. CaptureEach scored claim and its score are saved to Amazon S3.
  2. Check inputs dailyAn Evidently AI drift check compares claim amounts, causes and timing with the training baseline.
  3. Check outcomes monthlyInvestigation outcomes arrive weeks or months after a claim, so accuracy and fairness by region are calculated as they come in.
  4. Alert and reviewThe Head of Claims sees trends in a Quick Sight dashboard; alerts go to the fraud data science team through Amazon SNS.

Six months in, input checks show claim amounts from a newly entered coastal region far above the baseline. The model is retrained with the new region's data, and the region gets extra human review until enough outcomes are recorded.

Shadow AI and the tool register

About a third of Harbor Mutual's staff use free public chatbots, some pasting in customer emails. The response combines a register, approved alternatives and the Scoping Matrix to decide how deep each review goes:

Free public chatbot used by agents to draft replies: Scope 1, blocked

Consumer terms, no agreement; customer data must not go in. The approved company assistant is named as the alternative.

AI email drafting added to the CRM: Scope 2, under evaluation

Bought under an existing enterprise contract; legal must confirm whether the AI feature is covered and whether Harbor Mutual's data is used for training.

Policy assistant on Amazon Bedrock: Scope 3, approved

Built in-house on an existing foundation model; approved for customer and claims data after confirming it only retrieves documents the signed-in agent may see.

Claim-summary model fine-tuned on past adjuster summaries: Scope 4, approved with limits

A customized model containing Harbor Mutual data; approved for claims staff only, after personal data was removed from the training summaries.

Prompting the claim summary

Adjusters spend about 45 minutes reading each claim file (pain point 2). A summary prompt only helps if it is written for the adjuster's actual decision:

Claim summary prompt 
Role and task
You are a claims assistant at a home and auto insurer. Summarize the claim below for an adjuster who has two minutes to decide whether to send an inspector.

<claim>
Water came through the kitchen ceiling on 3 March after heavy rain...
</claim>

Output format
Five bullets, under 80 words:
- Cause of loss
- Damage described
- Estimated cost, if stated
- Anything unusual or inconsistent
- Recommended next step

Constraints
Use only facts in the claim notes. If a bullet can't be answered, write "Not stated". Do not say whether the claim is covered.

The last line matters most at an insurer: a coverage decision belongs to a licensed adjuster, so the summary must not hint at one.

Sorting the 3,000 daily emails

A few-shot prompt teaches the model Harbor Mutual's five email categories: BILLING, NEW_CLAIM, CLAIM_STATUS, POLICY_CHANGE and COMPLAINT. One example carries a regulatory rule: "It's been three weeks and nobody has called me back about my claim" is a COMPLAINT, not a CLAIM_STATUS question, because insurance regulators set handling deadlines for complaints. The classifier runs at a low temperature so the same email always gets the same category.

What the contact-center assistant costs in tokens

The assistant drafts after-call notes for 24,000 calls a day (pain point 4). With 3,000 input tokens per call (instructions plus the transcript) and a 300-token note, at hypothetical prices of $3 and $15 per million tokens:

  1. Input72 million tokens a day: $216.
  2. Output7.2 million tokens a day: $108.
  3. Month$324 a day over 22 working days: about $7,100 a month, against 2,480 agent-hours a day spent writing notes today.

The same 6,000 tokens of instructions and handbook text start every request, so they are the part worth caching. Quality-review summaries of each day's calls don't need an instant answer, so they run overnight as batch inference at half the price.

The policy assistant: RAG on policy wording

Agents spend about 800 hours a day searching documents (pain point 5). The policy assistant retrieves the relevant clauses from Harbor Mutual's policy wordings with Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases. An agent asks: "Does the standard home policy cover a burst pipe while the house is empty for two months?" The wording says "unoccupied for more than 60 consecutive days", so only a search by meaning finds it. The model receives:

Augmented prompt (simplified) 
Answer the agent's question using only the policy extracts below. Cite the clause for every statement. If the extracts don't answer it, suggest escalating to underwriting.

<extract clause="4.2">
Escape of water: we cover sudden and accidental escape of water from fixed pipes, tanks or heating systems...
</extract>
<extract clause="7.1">
Unoccupied property: if the home is unoccupied for more than 60 consecutive days, cover for escape of water, theft and vandalism is suspended unless the water supply is turned off...
</extract>

Question: Does the standard home policy cover a burst pipe while the house is empty for two months?

The answer: "Probably not, unless the water supply was turned off. Burst pipes are normally covered (clause 4.2), but that cover is suspended after 60 days unoccupied (clause 7.1)." Two controls matter most here: last year's policy wordings are removed from the index so the old clause can't be quoted, and a contextual grounding check blocks any answer not supported by the extracts.

House-format summaries: fine-tuning plus RAG

Prompting gets claim summaries close, but adjusters want one fixed house format every time. Harbor Mutual fine-tunes a model on 5,000 past claim notes paired with the summaries senior adjusters wrote, after removing personal data from them. The format is a behavior, so fine-tuning fits; the current policy wording changes, so it still comes from RAG at question time.

Domain 2: AI strategy at Harbor Mutual

Domain 2 is about choosing the right AI initiatives, sourcing them, proving their value and changing over safely. At Harbor Mutual this shows up in how the claims objective turns into use cases, the sourcing decision for each initiative, the scored portfolio, the approval rule that needed a process fix, not AI, how the value of the after-call summaries and the fraud model is measured, and where AI gives Harbor Mutual parity or advantage.

From the claims objective to use cases

Harbor Mutual's first objective is to cut the average claim cycle time from 12 days to 7. Mapping where claims wait finds two delays AI can remove, and one it shouldn't touch:

3 days waiting for repair estimates to be retyped: extraction

Pain point 1. Repair shops send quotes as PDFs; extraction reads the fields into the claims system the day the estimate arrives.

2 days waiting for adjusters to read claim files: summarization

Pain point 2. A five-bullet summary replaces 45 minutes of reading per file.

4 days waiting for a second approval: not AI

Pain point 3, worked through below.

Sourcing each initiative

Meeting notes and email drafting for office staff: buy

A common need, needed quickly, with nothing to gain from building it. The choice rests on data-use terms that meet company policy.

Plain-language questions about claims data for managers: buy (Amazon Quick)

"How many claims did each branch close last month?" is a reporting question; managed business intelligence answers it without ML skills.

Fraud detection: build (on SageMaker AI)

Fraud decides losses, and the model's strength comes from Harbor Mutual's own 25 years of investigated claims, which no vendor has. Regulators expect the insurer to explain why a claim was delayed, which is easier with a model it controls.

Contact-center policy assistant: partner, on Amazon Bedrock, with handover

Strategic and close to customers, and it must answer from Harbor Mutual's own policy wordings, but the team has no generative AI experience. A partner builds it with two Harbor Mutual engineers, who then take it over.

Then the stage gates:

After-call summaries: scale

In a 40-agent pilot, against a comparison group, summaries saved 1.3 minutes (78 seconds) per call, with 92% accepted unedited. Rolled out to all 400 agents.

Coverage chatbot: pause

Wrong coverage answers in 4% of test cases against a 1% limit. Paused until it answers only from retrieved policy text with grounding checks.

Marketing images: terminate

Little use, unresolved image-rights questions, and the design tool already licensed added a similar feature.

The approval rule: a process fix, not AI

The claims director asked for "an AI solution to clear the claims backlog". About 4 of the 12 days are spent waiting for a second manager to approve any payment above $2,000, a limit set 15 years ago that 60% of claims now exceed. Raising the limit to $7,500, with random spot checks below it, cuts about 3 days at almost no cost. A model that prioritized the approval queue would have sped up a step that shouldn't exist.

Going live with extraction

Clerks keep the ability to type estimates manually for three months, so claims keep moving if extraction has problems. The load test uses storm peaks, when estimates arrive at up to 5,000 a day instead of the usual 2,000 a week.

Proving the value of after-call summaries

Agents spend about 2,480 hours a day writing notes (pain point 4): 24,000 calls × 6.2 minutes. How Harbor Mutual measures what the summaries change:

Baseline: avoid storm weeks

The 6.2-minute baseline is a four-week average taken outside storm weeks and renewal season, when call volumes and call types change sharply.

Pilot: separate the AI from the new phone system

Pilot agents improved by 1.6 minutes; the comparison group, which got only the new phone system, by 0.3 minutes. The AI's effect is 1.3 minutes (78 seconds) per call.

ROI: count only avoided hiring

The freed time is worth about $4.0 million a year, but only the 40 agents not hired as call volume grows 10% ($2.4 million a year) counts as cash. Over three years that gives an ROI of about 283% against $1,565,000 of total cost.

Valuing the fraud model

The fraud model's benefit is measured by scoring the same month's claims with both the old rules and the model, not from a vendor's claims. At the 50% threshold chosen by the Head of Claims:

  1. Old rulesFlag 500 claims a month and catch 120 of the 200 fraudulent ones.
  2. ModelFlags 600 claims and catches 185.
  3. Extra fraud caught65 claims × $8,000 average payout = $520,000 a month, assuming caught fraud isn't paid.
  4. Extra investigation cost100 more flagged claims × $150 = $15,000 a month.
  5. Net benefitAbout $505,000 a month, roughly $6 million a year, before the model's running and monitoring costs.

Fraud losses are a lagging indicator: investigations take weeks or months to close. The leading indicator the claims team watches is the share of flagged claims that investigators confirm as suspicious within the first week.

Seeing AI costs by department

The agent-assist and claim-summary applications each call Amazon Bedrock through their own application inference profile, tagged CostCenter: contact-center and CostCenter: claims. Each month the Head of Contact Center and the Head of Claims see their own AI cost in Cost Explorer next to the KPI it is meant to improve.

Harbor Mutual's competitive position

The assessment separates where Harbor Mutual must catch up from where it can lead:

Claims speed: parity

Leading insurers settle simple car claims within hours from photos in their apps; Harbor Mutual takes 12 days on average. Customers notice, so the gap must close.

Contact-center service: parity

AI-assisted agents and self-service are common; Harbor Mutual has manual notes and no self-service. Proven tools close the gap, but bring no edge.

Fraud detection: advantage

Most insurers use some machine learning, but Harbor Mutual has 25 years of investigated claims, data no rival can buy.

Pricing: threat

Digital insurers price car policies from driving data collected by phone apps, winning the youngest, most digital customers. Comparison sites, and soon customers' AI assistants, choose between insurers; if Harbor Mutual's cover and prices can't be read by machines, it won't be compared at all.

The fraud flywheel and what stops it

A better fraud model sends investigators to the right claims, they confirm more fraud, every outcome becomes a training example, and the retrained model improves again. Lower fraud losses allow lower premiums, which win more customers and more claims data. The loop only turns if outcomes are recorded, which is why the branch that never recorded them (Domain 1) is a strategic problem. And claim summaries that take seconds don't speed up settlement while every claim waits four days for a second approval: the advantage needs the process redesign too.

Home Protect: a prevention business model

Water damage is Harbor Mutual's most expensive home-claim category. Home Protect would install smart water sensors in customers' homes, with an AI model that recognizes leak patterns and alerts the homeowner or shuts off the water. It changes the business model, not just the process:

Domain 3: Responsible AI and governance at Harbor Mutual

Domain 3 is about applying responsible AI principles, governing AI and managing its risks. Insurance decisions affect people's finances and are closely regulated, so at Harbor Mutual this shows up in fairness checks on the fraud model, explanations investigators can act on, how much freedom the customer assistant is given, who is accountable, what the EU AI Act means for an auto and home insurer, and how production risks are caught.

Fairness in the fraud model

The fraud model flags 4% of claims in Region A but 9% in Region B. The comparison that matters is how many of those flags are wrong:

MeasureRegion ARegion B
Claims flagged4%9%
Flagged claims confirmed as fraud55%22%
Honest customers wrongly flagged1.8% of claims7.0% of claims

Honest customers in Region B are nearly four times as likely to have their payment delayed by an investigation. The model leans on postal code and on the older housing stock common there. Removing postal code costs 1.5 points of fraud detection (about $480,000 a year); replacing it with repair-shop history and claim timing recovers 1 point and cuts wrongful flags in Region B to 2.3%. The fraud steering group accepts the remaining $160,000 a year, records why, and reviews flag rates by region every quarter.

The investigator can judge the reasoning, and the customer can be told what is being reviewed. Insurance regulators expect exactly this when a claim is delayed.

The customer assistant: narrowed, guarded and escalated

Marketing wanted the website assistant live before renewal season, but testing showed wrong coverage answers in 3% of cases against a 1% limit. It launched for claim status and document upload only, where accuracy is 99.6%; coverage questions go to agents. Its controls are insurance-specific:

  • Denied topics: legal advice, investment advice, and any promise of payment or a coverage decision.
  • Sensitive information: a custom pattern masks Harbor Mutual policy numbers in logs.
  • Automated Reasoning checks: rules for deductibles and eligibility conditions.
  • Escalation: to an agent whenever the question concerns a coverage decision or a claim denial, or the customer mentions bereavement, illness or a complaint.

Oversight across Harbor Mutual's AI

Email routing: human on the loop

Weekly sampling; a misrouted email is easy to redirect.

After-call summaries: human in the loop

The agent reviews each summary before saving; quality audits and acceptance-rate tracking.

Fraud scoring: human in the loop for any adverse action

A flag only sends a claim to an investigator; explanations and fairness monitoring by region support the decision.

Claim denial: a human decision

AI only summarizes the file; the adjuster is accountable, and a sample of denials is audited.

Who is accountable for the fraud model

The Head of Claims is the accountable business owner: they answer for the fraud model's production approval, its threshold and any incident. The data science lead builds it, a fraud operations manager sets the threshold day to day, and the chief risk officer is accountable for monitoring fairness by region, as the second line of defense that checks the first.

What the EU AI Act means for an auto and home insurer

Harbor Mutual operates in EU member states, so the detail of the Act matters:

Home and auto pricing: not automatically high-risk

The Act's high-risk list for insurance covers risk assessment and pricing for life and health insurance only. Insurance regulators and anti-discrimination law still apply.

Fraud model: not high-risk under the Act, high in Harbor's own tiers

AI used to detect financial fraud is excluded from the credit-scoring category. Harbor Mutual still classifies it as high, because it delays honest customers' claims at scale and has a known fairness issue.

Customer assistant: transparency duty

Customers must know they are dealing with AI. Classified medium, rising to high if it ever answers coverage questions without review.

HR screening tool: high-risk

Any AI tool that scores job applicants carries the full high-risk obligations.

Inferring customers' emotions from voice to adjust claim handling: rejected by policy

Emotion recognition is banned in the workplace under the Act and raises serious rights concerns for customers too, so Harbor Mutual's AI policy rules it out.

Securing the agent-assist system

The policy assistant retrieves only documents tagged for contact-center staff; underwriting guidelines marked confidential are excluded by document permissions. It looks up only the customer the agent has open, using the agent's own identity, and data is encrypted with Harbor Mutual's own KMS key.

An incident: the wrong deductible

On Monday Harbor Mutual updates its home policy deductibles. On Wednesday the weekly sample finds the customer assistant still quoting the old deductible. Deductible questions go to agents within the hour. Logs show 340 conversations mentioned deductibles and 61 quoted the old amount, because the old policy document was never removed from the knowledge base. The 61 customers get a written correction, any claim already settled is checked, and old documents must now be retired the same day.

A feedback loop in fraud investigations

Only investigated claims get a "fraud" or "legitimate" label. If the fraud model flags Region B slightly too often, investigators find more Region B fraud, the labels make Region B look worse, and retraining widens the gap. Harbor Mutual breaks the loop by investigating a random sample of unflagged claims in every region and reviewing regional flag rates before each retraining.

Domain 4: Readiness and scaling at Harbor Mutual

Domain 4 is about assessing readiness, leading the change and scaling AI across the organization. At Harbor Mutual this shows up in a frank readiness assessment, gaps that are mostly not technical, a pathway from experimentation to repeatable delivery, the data foundations the fraud upgrade needs, how people and roles change, and how proven AI is scaled.

Readiness scores

Leadership alignment: 3 of 5

A CEO sponsor and three stated objectives, but business unit heads compete for AI budget and disagree on priorities.

Data quality and access: 2 of 5

Claims, policy and billing data use different customer IDs; one branch never recorded investigation outcomes; access takes 6 to 8 weeks (pain points 9 and 10).

Cultural preparedness: 2 of 5

Contact-center staff worry about job cuts and a failed 2022 chatbot is still cited, yet a third of staff already use public AI tools.

Technical infrastructure: 3 of 5

Most systems run on AWS; the core policy system is old and hard to integrate; no shared AI platform.

Governance frameworks: 1 of 5

No AI policy, inventory or review process; shadow AI widespread (pain point 12).

Skills and talent: 2 of 5

Four data scientists, all on the fraud model; no generative AI experience.

Tested against the top use cases: after-call summaries could start now, the fraud upgrade is blocked by data, and anything customer-facing is blocked by governance. Governance scores lowest, is cheapest to fix and blocks the most, so it comes first. On a maturity scale Harbor Mutual is at stage 2, experimentation, while its fraud team is at stage 3.

Catching fraud rings across insurers

Some fraud rings file claims with several insurers. In an AWS Clean Rooms collaboration, Harbor Mutual and other insurers each contribute claims data; an agreed analysis flags overlapping phone numbers, addresses and repair shops, and returns only the matches each insurer is entitled to see. No insurer hands over its customer files.

Leading the change

Harbor Mutual's leaders pull in different directions: the Head of Claims wants faster settlement, the Head of Customer Service wants lower costs, and the Chief Risk Officer worries about regulators. The CEO, as sponsor, gives them joint objectives and seats on one steering committee. About 25 champions, one per contact-center team plus volunteers in claims and underwriting, get four hours a week released from call targets.

The 2022 customer chatbot, withdrawn after two months of confusing answers, is still cited as proof "AI doesn't work here". The CEO addresses it in a town hall: it was launched to customers without testing on real questions, and agents weren't involved. This time the first tool is for agents, agents help design it, it cites its sources, and nothing reaches customers without an agent's review.

Bereavement claims, vulnerable customers and denials stay with people. To protect how juniors build judgment, new adjusters handle a share of simpler claims fully by hand in their first year, review AI explanations as a learning tool, and rotate through investigations.

Scaling the policy assistant

  1. EnvisionAgents spend about 2 minutes per call searching policy documents (part of pain point 5); the target is 30 seconds.
  2. ExperimentA proof of concept on 300 real questions answers 91% correctly with citations.
  3. LaunchA 40-agent production pilot: search time falls to 40 seconds, and 97% of sampled answers are correct.
  4. ScaleAll 400 agents in four waves, after a peak test at post-storm call volume.

The patterns then spread: summarization moves from after-call notes to claim files for adjusters; document Q&A to underwriting guidelines for brokers; extraction from repair estimates to medical reports for injury claims; classification from customer emails to broker requests.

Keeping the value

After-call summaries saved 1.3 minutes per call at rollout. A year later monthly tracking shows 1.0 minute, because a new call type isn't summarized well; the prompt and test set are updated and the saving returns. When Harbor Mutual acquires a smaller insurer in another EU country, the assistant is re-evaluated on 300 local questions in the local language (93% against the 97% release criterion) and improved before its 120 agents get it, with local insurance rules checked and the old search tool kept as the fallback.
