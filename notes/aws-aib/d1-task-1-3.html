Task 1.3: Apply GenAI concepts and techniques

The three levers a business controls when it uses generative AI: how it writes prompts, how much information it fits into the model's context window (and pays for in tokens), and whether it adapts the model with its own data through retrieval augmented generation (RAG) or fine-tuning.

Overview

Task 1.3

Apply GenAI concepts and techniques.

A foundation model is general-purpose. Whether it gives useful answers for a particular business depends on what it is asked, what information it is given, and whether it has been adapted to the business. Questions in this task describe a disappointing or costly generative AI result and ask which technique fixes it. Examples use Harbor Mutual, a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees, 1.2 million policyholders and a 400-agent contact center.

Prompt engineering principles

Skill 1.3.1

Prompt engineering is the practice of improving the text given to a large language model so that it produces the response you need. The quality of the prompt directly affects the quality of the response (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Prompt engineering concepts). It is the cheapest and fastest way to improve a generative AI result, so it is always the first thing to try.

The parts of a good prompt

PartWhat it doesHarbor Mutual example
RoleSets the perspective and expertise the model should write from"You are a claims assistant at a home and auto insurer."
Instruction (task)Says exactly what to do"Summarize this claim file for the adjuster."
ContextBackground that helps the model interpret the input"The adjuster has 2 minutes and decides whether to send an inspector."
Input dataThe content the model works on, clearly separated from the instructionsThe claim notes, wrapped in tags such as <claim> … </claim>
ExamplesSample inputs with the desired outputsTwo past summaries the adjusters rated as excellent
Output formatThe shape, length and tone of the answer"Five bullets, under 80 words, plain language."
ConstraintsWhat the model must not do, and what to do when unsure"Use only facts in the claim notes. If something is missing, write 'Not stated'."

A vague prompt leaves the model to guess the audience, length and focus:

Weak prompt 
Summarize this claim.

Water came through the kitchen ceiling on 3 March after heavy rain...

The same request with role, task, context, input, format and constraints:

Improved prompt 
Role
You are a claims assistant at Harbor Mutual, a home and auto insurer.

Task and context
Summarize the claim below for an adjuster who has two minutes to decide
whether to send an inspector.

Input, separated from the instructions
<claim>
Water came through the kitchen ceiling on 3 March after heavy rain...
</claim>

Output format
Return exactly five bullets, under 80 words in total:
- Cause of loss
- Damage described
- Estimated cost, if stated
- Anything unusual or inconsistent
- Recommended next step

Constraints
Use only facts from the claim. If a bullet can't be answered, write "Not stated".
Do not guess whether the claim is covered.

The improved version tells the model who it is writing for, what the reader will do with the answer, and how to behave when information is missing. The "Not stated" instruction matters most: it gives the model an acceptable answer other than inventing a detail.

Prompting techniques

TechniqueHow it worksUse it when
Zero-shotGive the instruction with no examplesThe task is common and clear (summarize, translate, classify into obvious groups)
Few-shot (in-context learning)Include a few example inputs with the desired outputsYou need a specific format, labels or style the model wouldn't guess
Chain of thoughtAsk the model to reason step by step before answeringThe task involves several steps of logic, such as checking a claim against several policy conditions
Role (persona)Tell the model who it is and who the audience isTone and level of detail matter
Structured inputSeparate instructions from data with tags or headingsThe input is long, or comes from customers and might contain text that looks like instructions
Prompt templatesA reusable prompt with placeholders filled in for each requestThe same task runs thousands of times and must be consistent and reviewed

A few-shot prompt that teaches the model Harbor Mutual's own email categories, which it could not guess:

Few-shot prompt 
Classify each customer email into one category:
BILLING, NEW_CLAIM, CLAIM_STATUS, POLICY_CHANGE or COMPLAINT.
Answer with the category only.

Email: I was charged twice for my auto policy this month.
Category: BILLING

Email: A tree fell on my garage last night. What do I do?
Category: NEW_CLAIM

Email: It's been three weeks and nobody has called me back about my claim.
Category: COMPLAINT

Email: Can I add my daughter to my car insurance? She just got her licence.
Category:

The third example teaches a business rule: an unhappy status question counts as a complaint, not as a routine status request. Examples communicate rules like this better than long explanations.

Inference parameters

Besides the prompt text, each request carries inference parameters that shape the response:

ParameterEffectBusiness guidance
TemperatureHigher values make output more varied and creative; lower values make it more focused and repeatableLow for factual answers, classification and anything customer-facing; higher for brainstorming marketing ideas
Top PLimits the model to the most likely word choices, which also controls varietyUsually adjust temperature or top P, not both
Maximum output tokensCaps the length of the responseControls cost and keeps answers short; set too low, answers get cut off mid-sentence
Stop sequencesText that tells the model to stop generatingKeeps output to the expected structure

Treating prompts as business assets

For a solution used across a business, prompts are managed like any other important asset:

  1. Define what good looks likeAgree with the users (here, adjusters) on what makes a summary useful, accurate and complete.
  2. Build a test setCollect 50 to 100 real examples with known good answers, including difficult cases.
  3. Write and compare versionsChange one thing at a time and score each version against the test set.
  4. Review and approveCompliance and the business owner sign off on prompts that reach customers.
  5. Version and store centrallyKeep approved prompts in a shared library (on AWS, Amazon Bedrock Prompt management), not in individual spreadsheets.
  6. Retest when anything changesA new model version can respond differently to the same prompt.

When output is the wrong format, tone or length, or the model ignores what matters, the first answer is better prompting: clearer instructions, examples, an explicit output format and lower temperature. It costs nothing to try and needs no training data.

Prompting can't give a model knowledge it doesn't have. If the model doesn't know Harbor Mutual's current deductibles, no wording will fix that; the information has to be provided, which is what RAG does (Skill 1.3.3).

Tokens and context window constraints

Skill 1.3.2

A token is the unit of text a model reads and writes: a whole word, part of a word (such as "-ed"), a punctuation mark or a common phrase (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Key terminology). As a rough guide, an English word is between one and two tokens on average; the exact count depends on the model and the language. Tokens matter to a business for two reasons: they set the limit on how much a model can handle at once, and they are how most generative AI is priced.

The context window

The context window is the maximum number of tokens a model can consider in a single request, counting both what goes in and what comes out. Models don't remember earlier requests on their own; to continue a conversation, the application resends the earlier messages each time, and they take up space in the window (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Prompt engineering concepts). A single request from Harbor Mutual's contact-center assistant might be made up of:

What's in the windowExample size
Instructions (system prompt)1,500 tokens
Conversation so far4,000 tokens after a 20-minute call, and growing
Policy documents retrieved for this question6,000 tokens
The agent's latest question100 tokens
Space reserved for the answer1,000 tokens

Signs that token limits are affecting a system

SymptomLikely causeBusiness fix
Answers stop mid-sentenceMaximum output tokens set too lowRaise the output limit, or ask for a shorter answer format
Assistant "forgets" what the customer said early in a long conversationOlder messages were dropped to fit the windowSummarize older parts of the conversation and keep the summary
A long document is rejected or only partly consideredThe document is larger than the windowSplit it into sections and send only the relevant parts (RAG), or summarize in stages
Details buried in a very long input are missedToo much loosely relevant text competes for the model's attentionSend less, more relevant content; put key instructions clearly at the start or end
Responses get slower and costs rise over timeEvery request carries more tokens (longer histories, more documents)Trim context, cache repeated content, choose a smaller model where quality allows

Tokens and cost

With on-demand pricing, as on Amazon Bedrock, you pay per input token and per output token, and output tokens usually cost several times more than input tokens. Costs therefore scale with how much text each request carries and how many requests are made. An illustrative calculation with hypothetical prices of $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens:

  1. Volume400 agents each request 60 call summaries a day: 24,000 requests a day.
  2. Tokens per request3,000 input tokens (instructions plus call transcript) and 300 output tokens (the summary).
  3. Daily input cost24,000 × 3,000 = 72 million tokens, which is $216.
  4. Daily output cost24,000 × 300 = 7.2 million tokens, which is $108.
  5. Monthly cost$324 a day over 22 working days is about $7,100 a month. Doubling the transcript length nearly doubles the input cost.

Ways to keep token use under control:

  • Send only what's needed: retrieve the relevant sections of a document instead of the whole thing, and summarize long histories.
  • Limit output length: ask for concise formats and set a sensible maximum.
  • Cache repeated content: Amazon Bedrock prompt caching lets a model reuse a long, unchanging prompt prefix (such as standard instructions or a document many questions refer to). Cached tokens are billed at a lower rate and respond faster (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Prompt caching).
  • Right-size the model: a smaller, cheaper model may do simple classification as well as a large one.

"The assistant loses track in long conversations", "can't handle the full contract" or "costs grew as usage grew" all point to token and context window limits. The fix is usually to send less but more relevant content, not simply to buy a model with a bigger window.

A larger context window isn't free. More tokens per request means higher cost and slower responses, and stuffing in loosely related material can make answers worse.

Adapting models with RAG and fine-tuning

Skill 1.3.3

A foundation model knows a lot about the world in general but nothing about Harbor Mutual's products, procedures or customers, and nothing that happened after its training data was collected. When it lacks information, it may produce a confident but wrong answer (a hallucination). Two techniques adapt a model to a business, and they solve different problems:

  • RAG gives the model the right knowledge at the moment of the question.
  • Fine-tuning changes the model's behavior: its style, format or skill at a specific task.

Retrieval augmented generation (RAG)

AWS describes RAG in three steps: retrieve information from a data source, add it to the prompt as context, and get a better response from the model using that context (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Key terminology). The model itself is not changed. Harbor Mutual's policy assistant for contact-center agents works like this:

  1. Prepare the documents (once, then whenever they change)Policy wordings, underwriting guidelines and procedures are split into sections. Each section is converted into an embedding, a numeric representation of its meaning, and stored in a searchable index (a vector store).
  2. An agent asks a question"Does the standard home policy cover a burst pipe while the house is empty for two months?"
  3. RetrieveThe question is matched by meaning against the index, and the most relevant sections are found, such as the vacancy clause and the water damage clause.
  4. AugmentThose sections are added to the prompt with an instruction to answer only from them.
  5. Generate with citationsThe model answers and cites the clauses it used, so the agent can check the source.

On AWS, Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases provides this as a managed service. The managed option connects to sources such as Amazon S3, SharePoint, Confluence and Google Drive. It includes citations in responses and can filter results by document-level permissions, so users only get answers from documents they're allowed to see (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Knowledge Bases).

  • Strengths: knowledge stays current by updating documents, with no retraining; answers can be traced to sources; access permissions can be respected; and it reduces hallucinations because the model has the facts in front of it.
  • Limits: answers are only as good as the documents (outdated or contradictory documents produce outdated or contradictory answers); poor retrieval brings back the wrong sections; and each request carries extra tokens and a retrieval step.

Fine-tuning and other model customization

Fine-tuning trains an existing foundation model further on labeled examples (inputs paired with the desired outputs), which adjusts the model's parameters so it performs better on the tasks those examples represent. The result is a new, custom model (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Customize your model). Amazon Bedrock offers three customization methods:

MethodWhat you provideWhat it achievesHarbor Mutual example
Supervised fine-tuningLabeled examples of inputs and ideal outputsConsistent style, format, terminology or skill on a specific task5,000 claim notes paired with the structured adjuster summaries the company wants every time
Reinforcement fine-tuningPrompts plus reward functions that score response qualityBetter alignment with what the business considers a good answer, learned from feedbackScoring draft customer replies on accuracy, tone and compliance wording
DistillationExample prompts for your use caseA smaller, faster, cheaper "student" model that approaches the accuracy of a larger "teacher" model for your taskMoving high-volume email classification from a large model to a small one to cut cost

Customization has ongoing costs: training is charged by the number of tokens processed (training data size times the number of passes over it), and each custom model has a monthly storage charge (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Customize your model). It also needs good labeled data, retesting, and repeating when the business wants to move to a newer base model.

Training a model from scratch is a different order of effort: very large data, specialist teams and high compute cost. It is rarely the right choice for a business that is adopting AI rather than building AI products.

A sensible order for deciding:

  1. Start with prompt engineeringMeasure the result against a test set before spending more.
  2. Missing or outdated knowledge? Add RAGIf answers are wrong because the model doesn't know company facts, or the facts change often, or answers must cite sources.
  3. Wrong behavior despite good prompts? Consider fine-tuningIf the style, format or specialized judgment stays inconsistent and good labeled examples exist.
  4. Too expensive at scale? Consider distillation or a smaller modelOnce the task is stable and well defined.

The techniques combine. Harbor Mutual's claims assistant could use a fine-tuned model that writes summaries in the house format and RAG that supplies the current policy wording, with a prompt template tying them together.

Match the wording: "doesn't know our latest products, prices or policies", "information changes weekly" or "must show where the answer came from" means RAG. "Must always write in our format and terminology" or "needs specialized task performance" means fine-tuning.

Fine-tuning is a poor way to keep facts current. Every policy change would need retraining, and the model still can't cite a source. Use RAG for changing knowledge.

Key takeaways

  • A strong prompt has a role, a clear task, context, input separated from instructions, examples where useful, an output format and constraints (including what to do when information is missing).
  • Zero-shot suits common tasks; few-shot teaches specific formats and business rules; chain of thought helps multi-step reasoning; low temperature gives consistent, factual answers.
  • Treat prompts as managed assets: test sets, versioning, approval and retesting when the model changes.
  • Tokens set both the limit (context window = input plus output) and the price (per input and output token). Long histories, large documents and long answers raise cost and latency and can lower quality.
  • Fix token problems by sending less but more relevant content, summarizing, limiting output, caching repeated content and right-sizing the model.
  • RAG supplies current company knowledge at question time with citations and no change to the model; fine-tuning changes the model's behavior using labeled examples; distillation produces a cheaper model for a stable task. Start with prompting, add RAG for knowledge, fine-tune for behavior.
