Task 3.2: Establish AI governance structures and ensure regulatory compliance

Who decides, who is accountable and under which rules: setting up governance bodies with the right people in them, identifying the laws and regulations that apply to AI-enabled processes, protecting AI systems and their data with the right access controls, and classifying AI uses by risk so governance effort goes where it matters most.

Overview

Task 3.2

Establish AI governance structures and ensure regulatory compliance.

AI governance is the set of structures, roles, policies and processes that decide how an organization uses AI, and that hold people accountable for the results. Without it, decisions about AI are made by whoever happens to be building it, risks are discovered by customers or regulators, and nobody can say who approved what.

Good governance is not mainly about saying no. It makes AI faster to adopt safely: teams know which rules apply, which approvals they need and how long they take. AWS's research found that the organizations generating value from AI make the degree of oversight proportional to the consequence of being wrong, rather than removing oversight or applying the same heavy process to everything (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Announcing the new AWS Reimagine report on AI, September 2026).

Examples use Harbor Mutual, a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees, 1.2 million policyholders and a 400-agent contact center, operating in several countries including EU member states. Examples from other industries are included too.

AI governance structures and accountability

Skill 3.2.1

AI decisions touch many disciplines at once. A single customer-facing assistant raises business, legal, privacy, security, risk, technology, customer experience and workforce questions. No one function can judge all of them, which is why AI governance must be cross-functional. AWS's enterprise strategists recommend a diverse group overseeing AI initiatives, operating both as an AI steering group and at board level (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, Overseeing AI risk in a rapidly changing landscape, July 2024).

Typical governance layers

BodyRoleTypical membersMeets
Board or board risk committeeSets risk appetite for AI, oversees strategy and major risks, holds executives accountableBoard directorsQuarterly
AI steering committee (or AI council)Approves AI strategy and policy, prioritizes investment, approves high-risk uses, resolves escalated trade-offsExecutive sponsor (chair), business unit leaders, CIO or CTO, chief risk officer, general counsel, privacy officer, CISO, HRMonthly
Responsible AI review boardReviews individual use cases against policy, sets release criteria, approves medium and high-risk launchesRisk, legal, privacy, security, data science, the relevant business owner, customer experienceEvery two weeks or as needed
AI center of excellence (CoE)Provides standards, shared platforms, guidance, training and expert help to delivery teamsArchitects, data scientists, ML engineers, product managersOngoing
Delivery teams and business ownersBuild and run AI systems, and own their outcomes, monitoring and incidentsProduct owner, builders, operationsOngoing

Why each function needs a seat:

  • Legal and compliance: know which laws apply.
  • Privacy: judges whether data may be used for a purpose.
  • Security: understands AI-specific attacks.
  • Risk: sets thresholds and tracks exposure.
  • The business: owns the outcome and the value.
  • Technology: knows what is feasible.
  • HR: brings in workforce effects.
  • Customer experience: represents the people affected.

Leaving out a function means its risks are found late. A board without HR, for example, may approve a hiring tool without anyone asking about employment law.

Clear accountability

Every AI system needs one named accountable owner on the business side: the person who answers for its outcomes. That should not be "the AI team". Accountability is often set out in a RACI matrix (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed):

ActivityResponsible (does it)Accountable (answers for it)ConsultedInformed
Approve the fraud model for productionData science leadHead of Claims (business owner)Legal, risk, privacySteering committee
Set the fraud score thresholdFraud operations managerHead of ClaimsData science, customer experienceInvestigators
Monitor fairness by regionModel risk analystChief risk officerData scienceHead of Claims, review board
Respond to an AI incidentSystem operations teamHead of ClaimsSecurity, legal, communicationsSteering committee, regulator if required

There is exactly one "Accountable" per activity. Two accountable people in practice means none.

Three lines of defense

Regulated industries such as insurance and banking often organize risk oversight in three lines, which works well for AI:

  • First line: the business and delivery teams that build and use AI own and manage its risks day to day.
  • Second line: risk, compliance and privacy functions set policies, challenge the first line and monitor independently.
  • Third line: internal audit independently checks that the whole system works.

The separation ensures that the people building a model aren't the only ones checking it.

What the governance structure produces

  • An AI policy: principles, prohibited uses, risk appetite, roles.
  • An AI inventory: a register of every AI system and tool in use, with owner, risk tier and status, including approved third-party tools.
  • Standards and procedures: how to assess risk, set release criteria, document and monitor.
  • Decision records: what was approved, by whom, with what conditions.
  • Training: AI literacy for everyone who builds, buys, uses or oversees AI. The EU AI Act made AI literacy one of its first provisions to apply, in February 2025; check the current wording, as the 2026 amendments revised parts of the Act.

Good governance answers include cross-functional representation (business, legal, privacy, security, risk, technology, HR), a named accountable business owner for each AI system, executive sponsorship and board oversight, and oversight proportional to risk.

Leaving AI governance entirely to the IT or data science team is a classic wrong answer: they can't judge the legal, ethical and business risks alone. So is a committee that must approve every AI use, however small. It becomes a bottleneck that drives shadow AI.

Regulatory compliance risks

Skill 3.2.2

AI doesn't sit outside existing law. A process that uses AI is still subject to data protection, anti-discrimination, consumer protection and sector rules. AI-specific laws, such as the EU AI Act, add obligations on top. Compliance risk arises when an organization assumes "the AI did it" changes its responsibilities. It doesn't.

Areas of law that commonly apply

AreaWhat it requiresHarbor Mutual example
AI-specific regulationObligations based on the AI system's risk levelEU AI Act duties for the customer assistant (transparency) and any HR screening tool (high-risk)
Data protection and privacyA lawful basis for processing; use data only for its stated purpose; collect no more than needed; protect it; honor individuals' rights; impact assessments for high-risk processingUsing call recordings to train a model may need a new purpose assessment and notice to customers
Automated decision rulesSome data protection laws, such as the EU's GDPR, give people rights regarding decisions based solely on automated processing with significant effectsA human must make or review claim denials
Anti-discriminationNo unfair treatment of protected groups, whether intended or notThe postal code effect in the fraud model
Sector regulationInsurance regulators' rules on fair pricing, claims handling and treating customers fairlyPricing models filed with or explainable to regulators

Intellectual propertyRespect copyright and licences in training data and outputsRights to images used in AI-generated marketing

Employment lawFairness in hiring and monitoring of workers; consultation obligations in some countriesAn AI tool that scores job applicants or monitors agents' calls

The consumer protection point has been tested in practice. In 2024, a Canadian tribunal held Air Canada responsible for incorrect refund information its website chatbot gave a customer, rejecting the argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own statements.

The EU AI Act

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the first comprehensive legal framework on AI. It sorts AI systems into four tiers by risk (European Commission, AI Act):

TierMeaningExamplesConsequence
Unacceptable riskA clear threat to people's safety, livelihoods or rightsSocial scoring, harmful manipulation, emotion recognition at work or in schools, untargeted scraping of facial imagesBanned
High riskCan pose serious risks to health, safety or fundamental rightsCV sorting for recruitment, credit scoring, exam scoring, critical infrastructure; risk assessment and pricing for life and health insuranceStrict obligations before and after going to market
Transparency riskPeople need to know AI is involvedChatbots; AI-generated content, deep fakesDisclosure and labeling duties
Minimal or no riskMost AI in useSpam filters, AI in video gamesNo specific rules

High-risk systems must have:

  • risk assessment and mitigation;
  • high-quality data sets that reduce discriminatory outcomes;
  • activity logging for traceability;
  • detailed documentation;
  • clear information for deployers;
  • human oversight;
  • robustness, cybersecurity and accuracy.

After launch, providers monitor the market and both providers and deployers report serious incidents. General-purpose AI model providers have their own obligations, for example on transparency and copyright.

Key dates, as amended by the 2026 "digital omnibus" changes:

  • February 2025: prohibitions and AI literacy provisions apply.
  • August 2025: obligations for general-purpose AI models apply.
  • August 2026: transparency rules and general applicability.
  • December 2027: high-risk rules for the Annex III areas, such as employment and credit scoring.
  • August 2028: high-risk rules for AI in regulated products.

The detail matters. The Act's high-risk list for insurance covers risk assessment and pricing for life and health insurance. Harbor Mutual's home and auto pricing isn't automatically high-risk under the Act, though insurance regulators and anti-discrimination law still apply. Its credit-scoring category also excludes AI used to detect financial fraud. An AI tool Harbor Mutual uses to screen job applicants, however, is high-risk.

Outside the EU, approaches differ. In the United States, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (January 2023) is a widely used voluntary framework, with a Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024). Other countries and states are adopting their own AI laws, so a multinational needs a process for tracking regulatory change, not a one-time review.

Addressing compliance risk

  1. Inventory AI usesEvery system and tool, where it operates, whose data it uses and what decisions it affects.
  2. Map applicable rulesFor each use, by jurisdiction: AI-specific law, data protection, sector rules, anti-discrimination, consumer protection.
  3. Assess gapsCompare current practice with requirements: documentation, human oversight, disclosures, impact assessments.
  4. Implement controlsFor example, AI disclosure in the chatbot, human review of denials, a data protection impact assessment for call-recording analysis.
  5. Document and keep evidenceRecords that show regulators what was done and why.
  6. Monitor regulatory changeLegal owns a watch list; changes trigger reassessment of affected systems.

Providers help but don't take over responsibility. The AWS Well-Architected Responsible AI Lens states that it is not a compliance checklist, that every organization must decide how to interpret the regulations and standards it is subject to (naming the EU AI Act, NIST AI 600 and ISO/IEC 42001), and that legal counsel should be consulted.

Compliance answers start from an inventory of AI uses mapped to applicable laws by jurisdiction, then add controls, documentation and ongoing regulatory monitoring. The organization using the AI remains responsible for compliance; a vendor or cloud provider doesn't take that over.

Assuming a use is unregulated because no AI-specific law names it is wrong. Data protection, anti-discrimination, consumer protection and sector rules apply to AI-enabled processes regardless.

Access controls and data security for AI

Skill 3.2.3

AI systems concentrate valuable data: training sets, document collections, prompts containing customer details, and models that have learned from sensitive information. They also bring new ways in for attackers, such as instructions hidden in content, attempts to extract data through clever questions, and agents with broad permissions. Security for AI combines standard controls with AI-specific ones.

The shared responsibility model

On AWS, security is shared. AWS protects the global infrastructure that runs the AWS Cloud. The customer is responsible for its content, and for the security configuration and management of the AWS services it uses (Amazon Bedrock User Guide, Data protection). For AI, the split looks like this:

AWS is responsible forThe customer is responsible for
Physical data centers, hardware, networks and the managed service itselfWho can access the AI application and the models (identity and permissions)
Isolating model providers from customer data in Amazon BedrockWhich data is put into prompts, knowledge bases and training sets, and who can see the answers
Encryption capabilities and default protectionsChoosing encryption keys, network setup, logging and monitoring
Security of AWS-built featuresGuardrails configuration, application security, agent permissions, and use that is lawful and responsible

What Amazon Bedrock commits to on data, relevant to many compliance discussions (Amazon Bedrock FAQs and User Guide):

  • Inputs to and outputs from Bedrock are not used to train Amazon or third-party models, and are not shared with model providers.
  • Model providers have no access to the accounts where models run, so they can't see customer prompts, completions or logs.
  • Data is encrypted in transit and at rest, optionally with the customer's own keys, and stored in the AWS Region where Bedrock is used.
  • Fine-tuning trains a private copy of the base model.
  • AWS PrivateLink allows private connections without exposing traffic to the internet.

The key principle for generative AI comes from AWS's security guidance: foundation models have no concept of access control over data placed in the prompt. The safest approach is never to give the model information the user isn't allowed to see. Access control has to happen before data reaches the model, not by instructing the model to keep secrets (AWS Security Blog, Securing generative AI: Applying relevant security controls, March 2024).

Worked example: securing the agent-assist system

  1. Sign-inAgents sign in through the company identity provider with multi-factor authentication.
  2. RetrievalThe assistant retrieves only documents tagged for contact-center staff. Underwriting guidelines marked confidential are excluded by document permissions.
  3. Customer dataThe assistant can look up only the customer the agent has open on screen, passed as the agent's own identity, not a broad database role.
  4. PromptsCard numbers are masked before text reaches the model; guardrails mask any that slip through.
  5. Network and keysTraffic to Amazon Bedrock uses a VPC endpoint; data is encrypted with Harbor Mutual's own KMS key.
  6. LoggingInvocation logs are kept for the retention period legal sets, readable only by the security and audit teams.
  7. Change controlOnly the platform team can change prompts, guardrails or model choice, through a reviewed change process.

Strong security answers apply least privilege to people, applications and agents, enforce data access before content reaches the model, encrypt with managed keys, use private connectivity, mask sensitive data, and log activity, all under the shared responsibility model.

Relying on the system prompt ("don't reveal confidential information") as an access control is wrong. Instructions can be overridden; only data the user may see should ever reach the model.

Risk classification frameworks

Skill 3.2.4

An organization may have hundreds of AI uses, from spelling suggestions to claim decisions. Applying the same heavy review to all of them wastes effort and slows useful work; applying light review to all of them leaves serious risks unmanaged. A risk classification framework sorts AI uses into tiers, so the depth of governance matches the potential for harm.

Frameworks to know

FrameworkTypeWhat it contributes
EU AI Act risk tiersLaw (EU)Legally binding tiers (unacceptable, high, transparency, minimal) with obligations attached
NIST AI Risk Management FrameworkVoluntary framework (US)Four functions for managing AI risk across the life cycle: Govern (culture, policies, accountability), Map (understand context and identify risks), Measure (analyze and track risks), Manage (prioritize and act on them)
ISO/IEC 42001 and ISO/IEC 23894International standardsA certifiable AI management system, and guidance on AI risk management
Internal risk tiersThe organization's ownPractical tiers that combine legal tiers with company-specific factors and decide review depth

The AWS Generative AI Security Scoping Matrix is a different tool: it classifies uses by how much of the solution the organization owns, to decide security controls. A risk tier classifies uses by potential harm. Both are used together.

Classification across the life cycle

A tier isn't set once. Classification happens at the idea stage, so the right review path is known from the start. It is revisited at each stage gate, and whenever something changes the risk:

  • new data;
  • a move from internal to customer-facing use;
  • more autonomy;
  • expansion to new countries;
  • a new law.

For example, if the customer assistant is later allowed to confirm coverage on its own, it moves from medium to high and needs the full review before that change goes live.

Risk classification lets governance be proportional: classify early using impact on people, autonomy, data sensitivity, audience, scale, reversibility and regulatory exposure; attach review, documentation and monitoring requirements to each tier; and re-classify whenever risk changes.

Classifying by technology ("all generative AI is high risk") instead of by use and impact is wrong. A generative AI spelling helper is low risk; a simple ML model that decides loan approvals is high risk.

Key takeaways

  • AI governance needs cross-functional structures: board oversight, an AI steering committee, a responsible AI review board, a center of excellence and accountable delivery teams. Represent business, legal, privacy, security, risk, technology and HR.
  • Give each AI system one named, accountable business owner (RACI). Use the three lines of defense. Produce a policy, an AI inventory, standards, decision records and AI literacy training.
  • Existing laws (data protection, automated decision rights, anti-discrimination, sector rules, consumer protection, IP, data residency, employment) apply to AI. The EU AI Act adds tiers: unacceptable (banned), high (strict obligations), transparency (disclosure), minimal. High-risk rules for Annex III areas apply from December 2027.
  • Address compliance by inventorying AI uses, mapping laws by jurisdiction, closing gaps, documenting and tracking regulatory change. Responsibility stays with the deploying organization.
  • Under shared responsibility, AWS secures the infrastructure and the customer secures access, data and configuration. Bedrock doesn't use inputs or outputs to train models or share them with providers. Apply least privilege, approved-model restrictions, permission-aware retrieval, identity passing for agents, KMS encryption, PrivateLink, masking, logging and AI-specific attack protection. Enforce data access before data reaches the model.
  • Classify AI uses by impact, autonomy, data, audience, scale, reversibility and regulation into tiers with proportional review, documentation and monitoring. Use the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and ISO standards, and re-classify when risk changes.
