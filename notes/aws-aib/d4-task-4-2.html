Task 4.2: Establish data and infrastructure foundations for AI

The foundations every AI initiative stands on: judging whether data is good enough, findable and joinable, and what silos cost; setting a data strategy with clear owners and safe ways to share data inside and outside the company; and knowing what technology and infrastructure AI needs to run reliably at scale.

Overview

Task 4.2

Establish data and infrastructure foundations for AI.

AI projects most often stall not on the model but on the data: it can't be found, can't be accessed, can't be joined, isn't trusted, or isn't allowed to be used. AWS's enterprise strategists put the stakes plainly: "At AWS we say that data is your differentiator." Models and cloud services are available to everyone; an organization's own data is what makes its AI distinctive. They urge leaders to keep data rigorous, healthy and meaningful, to break it out of silos so it flows between departments, and to secure it (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, True data-centricity, February 2026).

Infrastructure matters too, but for most organizations the choice is less about building infrastructure and more about choosing managed services and connecting them to existing systems securely.

The running example is a national retailer with 300 stores, a website and a loyalty app, operating in several countries including the EU. Retail shows data foundations clearly: the same customer appears in several systems under different IDs, and its AI ambitions (personalized offers, demand forecasts for every product in every store, a customer service assistant) all depend on data that today sits in separate places.

Evaluating data readiness

Skill 4.2.1Evaluate data readiness: data quality, accessibility and the effects of data silos on AI initiatives

Data readiness

Whether the data a specific use case needs is good enough, available enough and permitted enough to use. It is always judged against a use case. The same sales data may be ready for a weekly dashboard but not for a demand forecast that needs three years of consistent daily sales for every product in every store.

Three questions of data readiness

Is it good enough? (quality)

What to checkAccuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, uniqueness and representativeness for this use case.

Example of a problemSales from one region's stores missing for the four months its tills were being replaced.

Can we get to it? (accessibility)

What to checkCan teams find it, understand it, get permission and use it in a usable format, in reasonable time?

Example of a problemAccess requests take eight weeks; nobody knows which table holds "net sales after returns".

Are we allowed to use it? (permission)

What to checkLawful basis and consent for this purpose; contractual restrictions; data residency.

Example of a problemCall recordings were collected "for quality and training of staff", which may not cover training AI models.

For generative AI, readiness also covers unstructured content:

  • Is there an inventory of documents?
  • Are outdated versions removed?
  • Is each document tagged with who may see it?
  • Is there an owner who keeps it current?

Data silos and what they cost

A data silo is data held by one part of the organization in a way others can't readily find, access or combine. Silos form for understandable reasons: systems bought at different times, mergers, departments with separate budgets, and caution about sharing sensitive data. They can be:

  • Technical: separate systems that don't connect.
  • Organizational: a department that controls "its" data.
  • Legal or contractual: restrictions on combining data.

Incomplete picture, weaker models

ExampleA recommendation model can't see that the customer browsing on the website is the same person who buys in stores with a loyalty card.

Inconsistent definitions

Example"Active customer" means three different things in the online, loyalty and marketing systems, so models and reports disagree.

Duplicated effort and cost

ExampleThree teams each build their own copy of customer data for their own AI project.

Slow delivery

ExampleMost of a project's time goes into negotiating access and matching records.

Hidden bias

ExampleA model trained only on one region's data, because that's the only data available, performs worse elsewhere.

Poor customer experience

ExampleThe customer service assistant can't see an order placed through a marketplace partner.

Worked example: the single customer view

The retailer's website, store tills (through the loyalty card) and app each assign their own customer ID. Its personalization and retention use cases both need one view of each customer:

  1. Measure the problemA sample of 10,000 customers shows 18% can't be matched across systems with certainty, mostly because of name spellings, old addresses and different email addresses.
  2. Agree definitionsThe data owners for online, stores and loyalty agree one definition of "customer" and of the key fields.
  3. Match recordsMatching rules link the IDs; uncertain matches go to a review queue.
  4. Publish a governed data setOne "customer and purchases" data set, documented, with an owner, a quality score and controlled access.
  5. Reuse itRecommendations, retention prediction and the customer service assistant all use the same data set instead of building their own.

A data readiness scorecard

For each priority use case, a short scorecard turns readiness into a go, fix-first or stop decision:

Data readiness scorecard: store demand forecast
Data needed
Daily sales by store and product (3 years), the promotions calendar, weather, local events
Quality: amber
One region's sales missing for 4 months during till replacement; returns recorded inconsistently before 2024
Accessibility: red
The promotions calendar lives in marketing spreadsheets, not linked to product codes; access to sales data takes 6 weeks
Permission: green
No personal data needed; the weather data is licensed for commercial use
Representative: amber
12 new stores have under a year of history
Decision
Fix first: link promotions to product codes and fill the missing region before model work starts

Data readiness is judged per use case on quality, accessibility and permission. Silos weaken models, create inconsistent definitions, duplicate effort and slow delivery, so breaking them down is a strategic investment, not housekeeping.

Starting model development before checking whether the data can be accessed, joined and lawfully used for this purpose is a classic cause of stalled AI projects.

Data strategy, ownership and sharing frameworks

Skill 4.2.2Describe why data strategies, data ownership and data sharing frameworks are the foundation for AI

Fixing data one project at a time is slow and repeats work. A data strategy is the organization's plan for managing data as a business asset, so that each new AI initiative starts from usable, trusted, accessible data rather than from scratch.

Elements of a data strategy

Business alignment

What it definesWhich business objectives the data must serve.

ExampleMore sales per customer, less waste of unsold stock, lower cost to serve.

Priority data domains

What it definesThe data that matters most, improved first.

ExampleCustomer, product, sales, stock, suppliers.

Architecture

What it definesWhere data lives and how it moves.

ExampleA central data lake on Amazon S3 with a shared catalog; product manuals and policies in a governed repository.

Ownership and governance

What it definesWho is accountable for each data set, and the rules for using it.

ExampleNamed owners per domain; classification and access policies.

Quality management

What it definesHow quality is measured, reported and improved.

ExampleQuality scores published for each governed data set.

Sharing frameworks

What it definesHow data is shared internally and externally, safely.

ExampleA catalog with request and approval; agreements with suppliers and partners.

Skills and culture

What it definesData literacy and treating data as part of everyone's job.

ExampleStore staff trained on why recording returns correctly matters.

Data ownership roles

Data without an owner degrades. Nobody fixes errors, approves access or keeps definitions consistent. Clear roles prevent that:

Data owner

ResponsibilityA business leader accountable for a data domain: its definitions, quality, permitted uses and access decisions.

ExampleThe chief merchandising officer owns product and sales data.

Data steward

ResponsibilityDay-to-day quality, definitions and documentation on the owner's behalf.

ExampleA merchandising data analyst who maintains the glossary and fixes quality issues.

Data custodian

ResponsibilityTechnical storage, security, backup and access implementation.

ExampleThe data platform team.

Data consumer

ResponsibilityUses data within the agreed rules and reports problems.

ExampleThe demand forecasting team.

The owner is a business role, not IT. The people who create and use the data understand what it means and what good quality looks like.

Data as a product

Many organizations now manage key data sets as data products: data sets packaged for reuse, with an owner, documentation, a quality commitment and a clear way to request access, much like an internal product with customers. This approach is often part of a "data mesh", where domain teams own and publish their own data products under shared governance standards. A data product description:

Data product: daily sales by store and product
Owner
Chief merchandising officer
Steward
Merchandising data analyst
Contents
All store and online sales since 2021, net of returns, linked to product and store, with promotion flags
Refresh
Daily, by 06:00
Quality
Completeness 99.2%; promotion flag 97% filled
Classification
Internal; contains loyalty IDs, which are personal data
Permitted uses
Forecasting, pricing, range planning; not individual marketing without consent
Access
Request through the data catalog; the owner approves; loyalty IDs masked for analytics users

Sharing frameworks inside the organization

Sharing must be easy enough that teams don't make their own copies, and controlled enough that sensitive data is protected. AWS services that support this:

  • Amazon DataZone lets organizations catalog, discover, share and govern data across AWS, on-premises and third-party sources. Data is published to a catalog with business terms and a business glossary; users find it and request access; owners approve through a governed workflow; and usage can be audited (Amazon DataZone User Guide). It is reached through the Amazon SageMaker or DataZone consoles.
  • AWS Lake Formation centrally governs, secures and shares data for analytics and ML. It provides fine-grained permissions at the database, table, column, row and cell level, tag-based access control to manage permissions at scale, sharing across AWS accounts, and audit logging through AWS CloudTrail (AWS Lake Formation Developer Guide).

Together these let the forecasting team use the sales data product with loyalty IDs masked, while the loyalty team sees them in full, without anyone copying data into a separate spreadsheet.

Sharing with partners

Some of the most valuable AI uses combine data across organizations, which raises privacy, competition and contractual questions. A data sharing agreement sets out:

  • the purpose;
  • exactly which data;
  • permitted and prohibited uses;
  • security requirements;
  • retention and deletion;
  • who owns any results;
  • audit rights;
  • what happens when the agreement ends.

Privacy-enhancing techniques reduce what has to be shared at all:

  • Anonymization or aggregation before sharing.
  • AWS Clean Rooms, which lets partners analyze their combined data sets without revealing the underlying data to each other. Analysis rules restrict queries and outputs; cryptographic computing keeps data encrypted while queries run; differential privacy protects against identifying individuals; and analysis logs support audits (AWS Clean Rooms User Guide).
  • AWS Data Exchange, for finding and subscribing to third-party data products, such as weather or property data.

Worked example: a consumer goods manufacturer advertised a new shampoo online and wants to know whether the ads led to purchases in the retailer's stores. Neither company may hand the other its customer list. In a clean room, the manufacturer contributes who saw the ads and the retailer contributes who bought the product. An agreed analysis returns only aggregated results, such as "customers who saw the ads bought the shampoo 2.1 times as often", with minimum group sizes so no individual can be identified. Each company's customer data stays private.

A data strategy names owners (business data owners, stewards, custodians), treats key data as reusable products, and provides sharing frameworks that are easy and governed: catalogs with approval workflows and fine-grained access inside, agreements and privacy-enhancing techniques such as clean rooms outside.

Making IT the "owner" of business data is a common mistake: IT can store and secure it, but only the business can define it and judge its quality. Copying data into a new store for each AI project spreads silos rather than removing them.

Technology and infrastructure requirements

Skill 4.2.3Assess the foundational technology and infrastructure requirements for AI initiatives

The AWS services named in this section are for awareness only; the exam guide puts infrastructure implementation out of scope. Know what each foundation is for (data storage, integration, access control, monitoring), not how to configure it.

AI needs more than a model. A production AI system sits on layers of data storage, compute, integration, security, monitoring and operations. A leader's job is to make sure each layer is in place and scaled to the ambition: not to choose chips, but to know what must exist and what it costs.

Infrastructure layers

Data foundation

What it providesStorage for structured and unstructured data, catalogs, pipelines that move and prepare data.

On AWS (examples)Amazon S3 data lakes, AWS Glue, AWS Lake Formation, Amazon DataZone.

Model access and building

What it providesAccess to foundation models; tools to build, train and deploy custom models.

On AWS (examples)Amazon Bedrock (managed, serverless access to foundation models); Amazon SageMaker AI (build, train, deploy ML).

Retrieval and knowledge

What it providesSearch indexes for RAG; vector stores.

On AWS (examples)Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases; vector stores such as Amazon OpenSearch Serverless or Amazon Aurora.

Agents and integration

What it providesConnecting AI to business systems and tools.

On AWS (examples)Amazon Bedrock AgentCore, including Gateway to expose APIs as tools.

Compute

What it providesProcessing power for training and inference, from serverless to dedicated.

On AWS (examples)Managed by Bedrock; or dedicated instances, including GPU-based ones and AWS-designed AI chips, for custom models.

Security and networking

What it providesIdentity, encryption, private connectivity, guardrails.

On AWS (examples)IAM, KMS, VPC endpoints with PrivateLink, Amazon Bedrock Guardrails.

Operations and monitoring

What it providesDeployment pipelines, versioning, monitoring of quality, cost and health.

On AWS (examples)Amazon CloudWatch, AWS CloudTrail, AgentCore Observability, SageMaker AI MLflow.

Cost management

What it providesVisibility and control of AI spend.

On AWS (examples)Cost allocation tags, application inference profiles, AWS Cost Explorer, AWS Budgets.

Managed services or self-managed infrastructure

Managed, serverless services (for example, Amazon Bedrock)

Fits whenUsing foundation models; small specialist team; variable usage; speed matters.

Trade-offLess to operate and pay-per-use, but less low-level control.

Managed ML platform (for example, Amazon SageMaker AI)

Fits whenBuilding and training custom models on proprietary data.

Trade-offMore control and customization; needs ML engineering skills.

Self-managed infrastructure

Fits whenVery specialized needs or very large, steady workloads.

Trade-offMaximum control; highest operational burden and skills requirement.

For most business AI, managed services remove the heaviest infrastructure work. The remaining requirements are mostly about data, integration, security and operations.

Requirements to assess for each initiative

  • Integration: can the AI reach the systems it needs (order system, stock system, CRM) through APIs? Old systems are often the bottleneck.
  • Performance: how fast must answers be? A contact-center assistant needs seconds; an overnight batch job doesn't.
  • Scale and capacity: peak volumes, such as Black Friday, and service quotas.
  • Availability: what happens during an outage? Is there a fallback?
  • Location: are the needed models and services available in the AWS Regions where data must stay?
  • Security and compliance: identity, encryption, private connectivity, logging, data residency.
  • Operations: who deploys, monitors and updates it, with what tools?
  • Cost: estimated running cost at expected and peak volume.

A shared platform, not one stack per project

When every AI project builds its own data pipelines, security setup and monitoring, costs multiply and standards drift. A shared AI platform, a common set of AI services and standards run by one central team, gives teams pre-approved, reusable building blocks, so new use cases start faster and meet governance requirements by default:

  • approved model access;
  • a standard RAG setup;
  • guardrail templates;
  • monitoring;
  • cost tagging.

Worked example: the retailer's AI platform foundations

Foundation models

DecisionAmazon Bedrock, with an approved list of models enforced by policy.

Custom ML (demand forecasting)

DecisionAmazon SageMaker AI, on the governed sales data product.

Company knowledge

DecisionBedrock Knowledge Bases over governed product manuals, delivery terms and the returns policy, with document permissions.

Integration with the old stock system

DecisionA small set of APIs (stock by store, order status) built once and exposed as tools through AgentCore Gateway, reused by every assistant and agent.

Security

DecisionPrivate connectivity, the retailer's own encryption keys, a standard guardrail configuration.

Monitoring and cost

DecisionShared dashboards for quality, safety and cost per use case; tags and application inference profiles for every application.

Data residency

DecisionEU customer data processed in EU AWS Regions; models chosen from those available there.

The platform is owned by a small central team, so that the customer service assistant, the recommendation model and the demand forecast all reuse the same foundations.

AI infrastructure spans data, model access, retrieval, integration, compute, security, operations and cost management. Favor managed services where possible, assess integration, performance, scale, availability, location and compliance for each initiative, and provide a shared platform so each project doesn't rebuild the basics.

Thinking of AI infrastructure as "buying GPUs" misses the point for most organizations. The usual bottlenecks are data access, integration with existing systems, security and operations.

Practice questions

Exam-style questions on this task. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
A company's customer data sits in separate systems with different identifiers. What is the main effect on AI plans?
  1. A. None
  2. B. Data silos limit use cases until data is integrated and governed
  3. C. Models will fix it automatically
  4. D. It only affects dashboards
Answer
B
Why
Silos and inconsistent data reduce what AI can do; integration and ownership come first.
Question 2
Scenario
Who decides who may use a data set and for what purpose?
  1. A. The data owner
  2. B. Any employee with access
  3. C. The model
  4. D. The cloud provider
Answer
A
Why
The data owner is accountable for the data and approves its use; stewards manage quality; custodians operate storage.
Question 3
Scenario
A RAG assistant lets employees see documents they could not open directly. What was missing?
  1. A. Permission-aware retrieval that respects existing access rights
  2. B. A larger context window
  3. C. Batch inference
  4. D. Higher temperature
Answer
A
Why
Retrieval must respect the same permissions as direct access, or the assistant becomes a data leak.
Question 4
Scenario
Which is the best sign of data readiness for an AI use case?
  1. A. A lot of data
  2. B. Data that is accurate, complete, accessible, permitted for the purpose and owned
  3. C. Data stored in the cloud
  4. D. Data in one spreadsheet
Answer
B
Why
Readiness is about quality, access, permission and ownership, not volume alone.

Key takeaways

  • Data is the differentiator. Judge data readiness per use case on quality, accessibility and permission to use; for generative AI include document inventories, versions, permissions and owners.
  • Silos (technical, organizational, legal) weaken models, create inconsistent definitions, duplicate effort, slow delivery and hide bias. Building shared, governed data sets such as a single customer view pays off across many use cases.
  • A data strategy aligns data to business objectives, prioritizes key domains, defines architecture, ownership, quality and sharing, and builds data literacy.
  • Ownership roles: data owner (accountable business leader), steward (day-to-day quality), custodian (technical), consumer. Manage key data sets as data products.
  • Share internally through catalogs with approval workflows and fine-grained permissions (Amazon DataZone, AWS Lake Formation). Share externally through agreements and privacy-enhancing techniques (anonymization, AWS Clean Rooms); source external data through AWS Data Exchange.
  • Infrastructure covers data, model access, retrieval, integration, compute, security, operations and cost. Prefer managed services (Amazon Bedrock, Amazon SageMaker AI), assess integration, performance, scale, availability, location and compliance, and build a shared AI platform.
