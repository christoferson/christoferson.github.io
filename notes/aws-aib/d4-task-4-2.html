Task 4.2: Establish data and infrastructure foundations for AI

The foundations every AI initiative stands on: judging whether data is good enough, findable and joinable, and what silos cost; setting a data strategy with clear owners and safe ways to share data inside and outside the company; and knowing what technology and infrastructure AI needs to run reliably at scale.

Overview

Task 4.2

Establish data and infrastructure foundations for AI.

AI projects most often stall not on the model but on the data: it can't be found, can't be accessed, can't be joined, isn't trusted, or isn't allowed to be used. AWS's enterprise strategists put the stakes plainly: "At AWS we say that data is your differentiator." Models and cloud services are available to everyone; an organization's own data is what makes its AI distinctive. They urge leaders to keep data rigorous, healthy and meaningful, to break it out of silos so it flows between departments, and to secure it (AWS Executive in Residence Blog, True data-centricity, February 2026).

Infrastructure matters too, but for most organizations the choice is less about building infrastructure and more about choosing managed services and connecting them to existing systems securely. Examples use Harbor Mutual, a mid-size home and auto insurer with 3,000 employees, 1.2 million policyholders and a 400-agent contact center, plus examples from other industries.

Evaluating data readiness

Skill 4.2.1

Data readiness is whether the data a specific use case needs is good enough, available enough and permitted enough to use. It is always judged against a use case. The same claims data may be ready for a dashboard but not for a fraud model that needs ten years of consistently labeled outcomes.

Three questions of data readiness

QuestionWhat to checkExample of a problem
Is it good enough? (quality)Accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, uniqueness and representativeness for this use caseInvestigation outcomes missing for one branch
Can we get to it? (accessibility)Can teams find it, understand it, get permission and use it in a usable format, in reasonable time?Access requests take eight weeks; nobody knows which table holds "settled amount"
Are we allowed to use it? (permission)Lawful basis and consent for this purpose; contractual restrictions; data residencyCall recordings were collected "for quality and training of staff", which may not cover training AI models

For generative AI, readiness also covers unstructured content:

  • Is there an inventory of documents?
  • Are outdated versions removed?
  • Is each document tagged with who may see it?
  • Is there an owner who keeps it current?

Data silos and what they cost

A data silo is data held by one part of the organization in a way others can't readily find, access or combine. Silos form for understandable reasons: systems bought at different times, mergers, departments with separate budgets, and caution about sharing sensitive data. They can be:

  • Technical: separate systems that don't connect.
  • Organizational: a department that controls "its" data.
  • Legal or contractual: restrictions on combining data.
Effect on AIExample
Incomplete picture, weaker modelsThe fraud model can't see that the same phone number appears on claims under three different policies held in separate systems
Inconsistent definitions"Active customer" means three different things in policy, billing and marketing systems, so models and reports disagree
Duplicated effort and costThree teams each build their own copy of customer data for their own AI project
Slow deliveryMost of a project's time goes into negotiating access and matching records
Hidden biasA model trained only on one region's data, because that's the only data available, performs worse elsewhere
Poor customer experienceThe contact-center assistant can't see a claim that was filed through a broker

Worked example: the single customer view

Harbor Mutual's policy, claims and billing systems each assign their own customer ID. Its fraud and retention use cases both need one view of each customer:

  1. Measure the problemA sample of 10,000 customers shows 18% can't be matched across systems with certainty, mostly because of name spellings and address formats.
  2. Agree definitionsThe data owners for policy, claims and billing agree one definition of "customer" and of the key fields.
  3. Match recordsMatching rules link the IDs; uncertain matches go to a review queue.
  4. Publish a governed data setOne "customer and claims" data set, documented, with an owner, a quality score and controlled access.
  5. Reuse itThe fraud model, retention prediction and the contact-center assistant all use the same data set instead of building their own.

A data readiness scorecard

For each priority use case, a short scorecard turns readiness into a go, fix-first or stop decision:

Data readiness scorecard: fraud model upgrade 
Data needed:     claims, policy, payments, investigation
                 outcomes (10 years), repair shop history
Quality:         AMBER  outcomes missing for one branch (3% of
                        claims); amounts consistent after 2018
Accessibility:   RED    claims and policy IDs not linked;
                        access takes 6 to 8 weeks
Permission:      GREEN  fraud prevention is a permitted purpose
                        under the privacy notice
Representative:  AMBER  new coastal region has under 1 year
                        of history
Decision:        FIX FIRST: customer ID matching and outcome
                 recovery before model work starts

Data readiness is judged per use case on quality, accessibility and permission. Silos weaken models, create inconsistent definitions, duplicate effort and slow delivery, so breaking them down is a strategic investment, not housekeeping.

Starting model development before checking whether the data can be accessed, joined and lawfully used for this purpose is a classic cause of stalled AI projects.

Data strategy, ownership and sharing frameworks

Skill 4.2.2

Fixing data one project at a time is slow and repeats work. A data strategy sets out how the organization will manage data as an asset, so that each new AI initiative starts from usable, trusted, accessible data rather than from scratch.

Elements of a data strategy

ElementWhat it definesHarbor Mutual example
Business alignmentWhich business objectives the data must serveFaster claims, lower fraud, lower cost to serve
Priority data domainsThe data that matters most, improved firstCustomer, policy, claims, investigation outcomes, policy documents
ArchitectureWhere data lives and how it movesA central data lake on Amazon S3 with a shared catalog; documents in a governed repository
Ownership and governanceWho is accountable for each data set, and the rules for using itNamed owners per domain; classification and access policies
Quality managementHow quality is measured, reported and improvedQuality scores published for each governed data set
Sharing frameworksHow data is shared internally and externally, safelyA catalog with request and approval; agreements for partners
Skills and cultureData literacy and treating data as part of everyone's jobClaims handlers trained on why recording outcomes matters

Data ownership roles

Data without an owner degrades. Nobody fixes errors, approves access or keeps definitions consistent. Clear roles prevent that:

RoleResponsibilityHarbor Mutual example
Data ownerA business leader accountable for a data domain: its definitions, quality, permitted uses and access decisionsHead of Claims owns claims data
Data stewardDay-to-day quality, definitions and documentation on the owner's behalfA claims data analyst who maintains the glossary and fixes quality issues
Data custodianTechnical storage, security, backup and access implementationThe data platform team
Data consumerUses data within the agreed rules and reports problemsThe fraud data science team

The owner is a business role, not IT. The people who create and use the data understand what it means and what good quality looks like.

Data as a product

Many organizations now manage key data sets as data products: data sets packaged for reuse, with an owner, documentation, a quality commitment and a clear way to request access, much like an internal product with customers. This approach is often part of a "data mesh", where domain teams own and publish their own data products under shared governance standards. A data product description:

Data product: Claims with outcomes 
Owner:           Head of Claims
Steward:         Claims data analyst
Contents:        all closed claims since 2015, linked to
                 customer and policy, with investigation outcome
Refresh:         daily, by 06:00
Quality:         completeness 99.2%; outcome field 97% filled
Classification:  confidential, contains personal data
Permitted uses:  fraud detection, claims analytics,
                 reserving; NOT marketing
Access:          request through the data catalog; owner
                 approves; column-level masking of names
                 for analytics users

Sharing frameworks inside the organization

Sharing must be easy enough that teams don't make their own copies, and controlled enough that sensitive data is protected. AWS services that support this:

  • Amazon DataZone lets organizations catalog, discover, share and govern data across AWS, on-premises and third-party sources. Data is published to a catalog with business terms and a business glossary; users find it and request access; owners approve through a governed workflow; and usage can be audited (Amazon DataZone User Guide). It is reached through the Amazon SageMaker or DataZone consoles.
  • AWS Lake Formation centrally governs, secures and shares data for analytics and ML. It provides fine-grained permissions at the database, table, column, row and cell level, tag-based access control to manage permissions at scale, sharing across AWS accounts, and audit logging through AWS CloudTrail (AWS Lake Formation Developer Guide).

Together these let the fraud team see the claims data product with names masked, while the claims team sees full details, without anyone copying data into a separate spreadsheet.

Sharing with partners

Some of the most valuable AI uses combine data across organizations, which raises privacy, competition and contractual questions. A data sharing agreement sets out:

  • the purpose;
  • exactly which data;
  • permitted and prohibited uses;
  • security requirements;
  • retention and deletion;
  • who owns any results;
  • audit rights;
  • what happens when the agreement ends.

Privacy-enhancing techniques reduce what has to be shared at all:

  • Anonymization or aggregation before sharing.
  • AWS Clean Rooms, which lets partners analyze their combined data sets without revealing the underlying data to each other. Analysis rules restrict queries and outputs; cryptographic computing keeps data encrypted while queries run; differential privacy protects against identifying individuals; and analysis logs support audits (AWS Clean Rooms User Guide).
  • AWS Data Exchange, for finding and subscribing to third-party data products, such as weather or property data.

Worked example: several insurers want to detect fraud rings that file claims with more than one company, without handing each other their customer files. In a clean room, each insurer contributes its claims data. An agreed analysis can flag overlapping phone numbers, addresses and repair shops across insurers, and only the matches each insurer is entitled to see are returned. Each company's other data stays private.

A data strategy names owners (business data owners, stewards, custodians), treats key data as reusable products, and provides sharing frameworks that are easy and governed: catalogs with approval workflows and fine-grained access inside, agreements and privacy-enhancing techniques such as clean rooms outside.

Making IT the "owner" of business data is a common mistake: IT can store and secure it, but only the business can define it and judge its quality. Copying data into a new store for each AI project spreads silos rather than removing them.

Technology and infrastructure requirements

Skill 4.2.3

AI needs more than a model. A production AI system sits on layers of data storage, compute, integration, security, monitoring and operations. A leader's job is to make sure each layer is in place and scaled to the ambition: not to choose chips, but to know what must exist and what it costs.

Infrastructure layers

LayerWhat it providesOn AWS (examples)
Data foundationStorage for structured and unstructured data, catalogs, pipelines that move and prepare dataAmazon S3 data lakes, AWS Glue, AWS Lake Formation, Amazon DataZone

Retrieval and knowledgeSearch indexes for RAG; vector storesAmazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases; vector stores such as Amazon OpenSearch Serverless or Amazon Aurora
Agents and integrationConnecting AI to business systems and toolsAmazon Bedrock AgentCore, including Gateway to expose APIs as tools

Operations and monitoringDeployment pipelines, versioning, monitoring of quality, cost and healthAmazon CloudWatch, AWS CloudTrail, AgentCore Observability, SageMaker AI MLflow

Managed services or self-managed infrastructure

ApproachFits whenTrade-off
Managed, serverless services (for example, Amazon Bedrock)Using foundation models; small specialist team; variable usage; speed mattersLess to operate and pay-per-use, but less low-level control
Managed ML platform (for example, Amazon SageMaker AI)Building and training custom models on proprietary dataMore control and customization; needs ML engineering skills
Self-managed infrastructureVery specialized needs or very large, steady workloadsMaximum control; highest operational burden and skills requirement

For most business AI, managed services remove the heaviest infrastructure work. The remaining requirements are mostly about data, integration, security and operations.

Requirements to assess for each initiative

  • Integration: can the AI reach the systems it needs (policy system, claims system, CRM) through APIs? Old systems are often the bottleneck.
  • Performance: how fast must answers be? A contact-center assistant needs seconds; an overnight batch job doesn't.
  • Scale and capacity: peak volumes, such as after a storm, and service quotas.
  • Availability: what happens during an outage? Is there a fallback?
  • Location: are the needed models and services available in the AWS Regions where data must stay?
  • Security and compliance: identity, encryption, private connectivity, logging, data residency.
  • Operations: who deploys, monitors and updates it, with what tools?
  • Cost: estimated running cost at expected and peak volume.

A shared platform, not one stack per project

When every AI project builds its own data pipelines, security setup and monitoring, costs multiply and standards drift. A shared AI platform gives teams pre-approved, reusable building blocks, so new use cases start faster and meet governance requirements by default:

  • approved model access;
  • a standard RAG setup;
  • guardrail templates;
  • monitoring;
  • cost tagging.

Worked example: Harbor Mutual's AI platform foundations

NeedDecision
Foundation modelsAmazon Bedrock, with an approved list of models enforced by policy
Custom ML (fraud)Amazon SageMaker AI, on the governed claims data product
Company knowledgeBedrock Knowledge Bases over governed policy documents, with document permissions
Integration with the old policy systemA small set of APIs built once and exposed as tools through AgentCore Gateway, reused by every assistant and agent

Monitoring and costShared dashboards for quality, safety, cost per use case; tags and application inference profiles for every application

The platform is owned by a small central team, so that the after-call summaries, the policy assistant and the fraud model all reuse the same foundations.

AI infrastructure spans data, model access, retrieval, integration, compute, security, operations and cost management. Favor managed services where possible, assess integration, performance, scale, availability, location and compliance for each initiative, and provide a shared platform so each project doesn't rebuild the basics.

Thinking of AI infrastructure as "buying GPUs" misses the point for most organizations. The usual bottlenecks are data access, integration with existing systems, security and operations.

Key takeaways

  • Data is the differentiator. Judge data readiness per use case on quality, accessibility and permission to use; for generative AI include document inventories, versions, permissions and owners.
  • Silos (technical, organizational, legal) weaken models, create inconsistent definitions, duplicate effort, slow delivery and hide bias. Building shared, governed data sets such as a single customer view pays off across many use cases.
  • A data strategy aligns data to business objectives, prioritizes key domains, defines architecture, ownership, quality and sharing, and builds data literacy.
  • Ownership roles: data owner (accountable business leader), steward (day-to-day quality), custodian (technical), consumer. Manage key data sets as data products.
  • Share internally through catalogs with approval workflows and fine-grained permissions (Amazon DataZone, AWS Lake Formation). Share externally through agreements and privacy-enhancing techniques (anonymization, AWS Clean Rooms); source external data through AWS Data Exchange.
  • Infrastructure covers data, model access, retrieval, integration, compute, security, operations and cost. Prefer managed services (Amazon Bedrock, Amazon SageMaker AI), assess integration, performance, scale, availability, location and compliance, and build a shared AI platform.
