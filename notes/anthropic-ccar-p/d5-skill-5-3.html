5.3: Risk Assessment and Ranking

Going through a proposed deployment category by category (how it can be attacked, how it can fail on its own, how it can run out of tokens or budget, and what it depends on) and putting the risks in order of how exposed the system really is, taking into account the controls already in place.

Overview

Skill 5.3

Risk Assessment and Ranking: "Analyzing a proposed deployment for risk categories (attack surface, failure modes, token and quota exhaustion, dependency and supply-chain risk) and ranking them by exposure."

Exam items describe a proposed deployment and ask which risk ranks highest, or which is missing from an assessment. Specific controls are covered elsewhere: injection in skill 5.2, guardrails in skill 5.1, reliability in skill 3.8.

Find the risks in a deployment and rank them by what’s left after its controls

Skill 5.3What this skill asks, and a deployment to assess.

Questions on this skill describe a proposed deployment and ask which risk is most serious, or how the risks rank. The guide names four categories: attack surface, failure modes, token and quota exhaustion, and dependency and supply-chain risk. The ranking is by exposure, and the usual mistake is ranking by how alarming a risk sounds.

Take a city council planning a resident-services agent on its website. It answers questions, books bulky-waste collections, reports potholes through an MCP server in front of the works-management system, and reads photos and documents that residents upload.

Attack surface: how an adversary gets in, and what they could then do

Skill 5.3Jailbreaks, injected instructions and what each tool would allow.

Direct jailbreaks typed by users; instructions hidden in uploaded documents, tool results and MCP responses; and, for each tool, what a successful attack could make it do. For the council: injected instructions in an uploaded document that trigger bookings or reveal other residents’ data, and jailbreaks producing offensive replies on a public site.

Failure modes: how it goes wrong with no attacker at all

Skill 5.3Wrong answers, made-up details, duplicate actions, silent tool failures.

Out-of-date or wrong answers, invented specifics, actions done twice, and tools that fail without anyone noticing. For the council: a wrong collection day or eligibility answer from out-of-date policy pages, and a collection booked twice after a timeout.

Token and quota exhaustion: the service stops when a limit is hit

Skill 5.3The risk that is easiest to miss.

Each usage tier has a monthly spend cap. When it’s reached, API usage pauses until the next month, and requests return 429 errors with no retry-after header, so retries keep failing. Rate limits and ramp limits also cut in when traffic jumps suddenly, and a looping session can burn tokens retrying a broken tool. For the council: the spend cap reached during a surge after a storm, exactly when residents most need the service.

Dependency and supply chain: what you rely on can change under you

Skill 5.3Third-party servers, Skills and packages; models that retire; the platform itself.

Third-party MCP servers, Skills and packages (Anthropic’s guidance is to install Skills only from trusted sources and to audit them); model versions that retire, with at least 60 days’ notice; and the platform itself. For the council: a community MCP package for the works system that updates automatically from a public registry, and a model retiring before the council’s procurement cycle allows a change.

Rank by exposure: likelihood times impact, after existing controls

Skill 5.3A simple method you can explain.

  • Exposure is likelihood times impact, each scored from 1 to 5, after taking into account the controls already in the design. That ranks the risk you actually carry, not the risk before any controls.
  • Likelihood rises with how reachable the path is (a public website where anyone can upload) and how often the trigger happens (every storm, every release).
  • Impact rises with the harm and how many people it affects: everyone at once, as when a spend cap stops the whole service, outranks one resident getting a bad answer.
  • An uncontrolled risk usually tops the list, even when a better-known risk sounds more alarming, because the alarming one usually already has controls.

Worked example: the council’s risk register

Skill 5.3Eight risks across the four categories, ranked.

  • Top (15): the spend cap in a storm surge. There is no workspace limit, no alert and no fallback mode, so when it’s reached every resident gets errors at the moment demand peaks.
  • Next (12 each): injection through uploads, and out-of-date answers. Injection is the best-known risk, but uploads already go into tool results and the booking tool is limited to the signed-in resident, which limits what an injection can do.
  • Then (10): the supply chain. Rare but severe: an automatically updated community package can change under you.
  • Low (6 each): duplicate bookings (the booking tool is idempotent) and looping sessions (turns are capped).

The code below scores each risk after its existing controls and sorts the register:

risk_register.py (risk_register.py)Optional 
"""Rank the risks of a proposed deployment by exposure: a city council's resident-services agent.

The agent answers residents on the website, books bulky-waste collections and reports potholes via an
MCP server in front of the works-management system, and reads uploaded photos and documents.
Exposure = likelihood x impact (1-5 each), scored after the controls already in the design."""

RISKS = [
    # category, risk, likelihood, impact, existing control
    ("attack surface", "injected instructions in uploaded documents trigger bookings or reveal other residents' data",
     3, 4, "untrusted uploads go in tool results; booking tool scoped to the signed-in resident"),
    ("attack surface", "jailbreak produces offensive replies on a public council site", 3, 3, "harmlessness screen"),
    ("failure mode", "wrong bin-day or eligibility answer from stale policy pages", 4, 3, "weekly re-index"),
    ("failure mode", "agent books the same collection twice after a timeout", 3, 2, "booking tool idempotent per resident and date"),
    ("token and quota exhaustion", "monthly spend cap reached in a storm-damage surge; every request then fails with 429",
     3, 5, "none: no workspace limit, no alert, no degraded mode"),
    ("token and quota exhaustion", "a looping session burns tokens retrying a broken tool", 3, 2, "max_turns 15"),
    ("dependency and supply chain", "community MCP server package for the works system is updated with malicious code",
     2, 5, "none: installed from a public registry, auto-updated"),
    ("dependency and supply chain", "model version retired before the council's procurement cycle allows a change",
     2, 3, "deprecation notices go to one person's inbox"),
]


def rank(risks):
    scored = [(l * i, cat, risk, ctl) for cat, risk, l, i, ctl in risks]
    return sorted(scored, key=lambda r: -r[0])


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for exposure, cat, risk, ctl in rank(RISKS):
        print(f"{exposure:>2}  {cat:28} {risk}\n    existing control: {ctl}")

Ranking by how frightening a risk sounds (injection first, always) instead of by what’s left after controls misses the quiet, uncontrolled risk: here, a budget that runs out in the busiest week of the year.

Act on the top of the list, then score it again

Skill 5.3What the top risks would get.

  • Spend cap: a workspace spend limit below the cap, with alerts; a higher usage tier before winter; and a fallback mode (a static page of contact routes) when limits are near.
  • Injection: screening of uploads, and keeping the booking tool limited to the signed-in resident.
  • Out-of-date answers: re-index whenever a page changes rather than weekly, and add a check in the evaluation that answers are supported by current pages.
  • Supply chain: pin and review the MCP server version, run it with only the permissions it needs, and send deprecation notices to a team mailbox, not one person.

Score the register again after each change, so it ranks what’s left, not what was.

Practice questions

Exam-style questions written for these notes. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
A public booking agent has a well-known injection risk, already limited by scoped tools and content placed in tool results. Its monthly spend cap has no alert or fallback, and traffic triples in holiday weeks. Which risk should rank highest?
  1. A. Prompt injection, because it is the best-known risk
  2. B. The spend cap, because it is uncontrolled and would stop the whole service at peak
  3. C. A jailbreak producing an offensive reply
  4. D. A slow answer for one user
Answer
B
Why
Ranking is by exposure after existing controls. The spend cap has no control and would affect everyone at the busiest time; injection already has controls that limit what it can do.
Question 2
Scenario
Which risk belongs in the “dependency and supply chain” category?
  1. A. An answer based on an out-of-date policy page
  2. B. An MCP server package installed from a public registry that updates automatically
  3. C. A user typing a jailbreak
  4. D. A looping session burning tokens
Answer
B
Why
A third-party package that can change under you is a supply-chain risk. Out-of-date answers are a failure mode, jailbreaks are attack surface, and runaway sessions are token exhaustion.
Question 3
Scenario
A risk is scored before considering that the booking tool is idempotent. Why does that give a misleading ranking?
  1. A. Idempotency raises the risk
  2. B. The ranking should show the risk left after existing controls, which here is much lower
  3. C. Idempotency only matters for payments
  4. D. Scores should ignore controls to stay conservative
Answer
B
Why
Scoring after existing controls ranks the risk you actually carry. Ignoring the idempotent tool would push a well-controlled risk above uncontrolled ones.
Question 4
Scenario
After adding a workspace spend limit with alerts and a fallback page, what should the team do with the risk register?
  1. A. Nothing; it was correct when written
  2. B. Score the risks again so the list ranks what’s left
  3. C. Delete the spend-cap entry
  4. D. Move all risks to low
Answer
B
Why
The register should rank the remaining risk. After a mitigation, score again; another risk may now be at the top.

Key takeaways

  • Cover all four categories: attack surface, failure modes, token and quota exhaustion, and dependency and supply chain.
  • Rank by exposure (likelihood times impact) after existing controls, so the list shows the risk you actually carry.
  • Quota risks are easy to miss: at a tier’s monthly spend cap the API stops serving until the next month, and retries can’t help.
  • Supply-chain risks include third-party MCP servers and Skills, model retirements and the platform itself.
  • The top risk is often the one with no control, not the most familiar one; score again after every mitigation.
