1.5: Value-Based Architectural Decisions

Choosing between competing designs by what they're worth to the business, measured, inside the limits you've been given: a budget, a latency target, or both. The best option is the most valuable one that fits, not the most capable one.

Overview

Skill 1.5

Value-Based Architectural Decisions: "Selecting the architectural option that maximizes measurable business outcome under a stated cost or SLA ceiling, weighing competing feature proposals against stated business value pillars such as efficiency, transformation, productivity, solution cost, and performance SLAs."

Exam items give two to four options with figures and a ceiling, and ask which to choose. The usual trap is the option with the best raw quality that breaks the ceiling. Choosing a model tier in depth is skill 2.1; diagnosing cost problems is in Domain 4.

Choose the option with the most measured value that fits within the limits

Skill 1.5What this skill asks you to decide.

Questions on this skill give two to four ways to build something, each with figures (what it costs, how fast it is, how well it does the job), plus a limit: a monthly budget, a response-time target, or both. You choose the option that delivers the most business value while staying inside every limit.

Two things make this harder than it sounds. First, “value” has to be a number you can measure, not a general sense that one option is better; the next section shows how to get one for each kind of value the guide names. Second, the limits come first: an option that breaks one is out, however good it looks otherwise. The usual wrong answer is the option with the best quality that is over budget or too slow.

Turn each kind of value into a number

Skill 1.5The guide’s value pillars, and how to measure each one.

The guide lists five “value pillars”. Each needs a measurement before it can decide between options:

  • Efficiency: work removed or cost avoided. Measure it as the work that no longer needs a person, times what that work costs. If Claude resolves 40% of 120,000 support tickets a month, and a person costs $6.50 per ticket, that’s about $312,000 a month.
  • Productivity: more output from the people who still do the work. Measure the change in output per person, such as minutes saved on each ticket that still goes to a person because Claude has already gathered the account details.
  • Transformation: something the business couldn’t do before. For example, support around the clock in a new language. Put a figure on the revenue or retention it’s expected to bring, and say how you’ll check that figure after launch.
  • Solution cost: what it costs to run. Model spend plus the cost of building, running and reviewing the system. Usually it appears as a limit (“no more than $9,000 a month”) rather than something to maximise.
  • Performance SLAs: the service levels promised. Response time, availability and throughput targets, such as “95% of live-chat answers within 8 seconds”. These are pass or fail: an option either meets them or it doesn’t.

The figures should come from your own evaluation runs or a pilot, on your own traffic. A pillar with no number behind it can’t decide anything.

Apply the limits first, then compare value

Skill 1.5The order of the decision.

  1. Remove every option that breaks a stated limit: over the budget, or slower than the SLA. Value elsewhere can’t rescue it, because the limit is a requirement, not a preference.
  2. Among the options left, pick the one with the highest measured value: the net value (value minus cost), or the pillar the question says matters most.
  3. Check whether the answer is fragile. If a slightly lower budget or a slightly worse result would change the winner, say so; the decision depends on that number.
  4. When two options are close, prefer the simpler one. It’s cheaper to run and easier to change.

Know the prices that change the arithmetic

Skill 1.5Claude API pricing choices that move an option over or under a budget.

  • Which model. Prices are per million input and output tokens: Opus 5.5 $4 and $20, Sonnet 5.5 $2 and $10, Haiku 5.5 $0.10 and $0.50 (for prompts up to 100,000 tokens; more above that), Fable 5.2 $8 and $40. The same work can cost forty times more on one model than another.
  • Prompt caching. When every call starts with the same long text, such as instructions and a knowledge base, cache it. Reading cached text costs 0.05 times the normal input price on Opus and Sonnet 5.5; writing it to the cache costs a little more than normal (1.25 times for a 5-minute cache). A long prefix read many times is the biggest saving.
  • Batches. Work that doesn’t need an immediate answer can go through the Message Batches API at half price, if the SLA allows the wait.
  • Fast mode. Up to 2.5 times faster output at a higher price (Opus 5.5 at $8 and $40), and not available in batches. It buys speed, not better answers, so it helps an option meet a response-time SLA.
  • US-only processing. Keeping inference in the US multiplies all token prices by 1.1; count it when residency requires it.
  • Combining models. Send most traffic to a cheap model and only the hard part to a stronger one, so most tokens are billed at the lower price.

Worked example: three designs for support automation

Skill 1.5The order of the decision, applied with real figures.

A broadband provider’s support team handles 120,000 tickets a month, and each ticket a person resolves costs about $6.50. Leadership allows $9,000 a month in model spend, and 95% of live-chat answers must arrive within 8 seconds. The team measured three designs on its own evaluation set:

  • A: Opus 5.5 answers every ticket. Resolves 46% of tickets, so it saves about $359,000 a month for $6,000 of model spend. But 95% of its answers take up to 11 seconds, which breaks the 8-second SLA. It’s out, whatever it saves.
  • B: Haiku 5.5 sorts each ticket, and Sonnet 5.5 answers 70% of them with the knowledge base cached. Resolves 41%, saves about $320,000, costs about $900, and answers within 6 seconds.
  • C: as B, plus Opus 5.5 in fast mode for the hardest 30%. Resolves 45%, saves about $351,000, costs about $2,500, and answers within 7 seconds.

The decision. A breaks the SLA, so it’s removed. B and C both fit; C delivers about $30,000 a month more net value, so C is the choice at a $9,000 budget.

How fragile it is. At a $2,000 budget the answer becomes B, because C’s fast-mode answers cost about $2,500 a month. That’s worth stating alongside the recommendation.

What the figures leave out. The calculation ignores the cost of writing to the cache and assumes the measured resolution rates hold in production. A real decision would add both, plus the cost of building and reviewing each design.

The code below holds the prices and measurements, removes options that break a limit, and picks the highest net value:

value_scorecard.py (value_scorecard.py)Optional 
"""Pick the support-automation design with the most measurable value inside the cost and SLA ceilings.

Prices: Claude API pricing page, November 2026 (USD per million tokens). Value inputs (resolution
rate, latency) come from the team's own evaluation runs, not from the model card."""
from dataclasses import dataclass

PRICE = {  # input, cache read, output
    "opus-5.5": (4.00, 0.20, 20.00), "sonnet-5.5": (2.00, 0.10, 10.00), "haiku-5.5": (0.10, 0.01, 0.50),
}
TICKETS = 120_000                 # per month
AGENT_COST = 6.50                 # cost of a ticket handled by a person
CEILING = 9_000                   # monthly model spend allowed
SLA_P95_SECONDS = 8               # first answer in live chat


@dataclass
class Option:
    name: str
    steps: list                   # (model, share of tickets, input tokens, cached share, output tokens)
    resolved: float               # share resolved without a person (measured on the eval set)
    p95_seconds: float            # measured
    fast_mode: bool = False       # Opus 5.5 fast mode: 2x price ($8/$40), faster output


def model_cost(o: Option) -> float:
    total = 0.0
    for model, share, tin, cached, tout in o.steps:
        pin, pcache, pout = PRICE[model]
        mult = 2 if o.fast_mode and model == "opus-5.5" else 1
        per = (tin * (1 - cached) * pin + tin * cached * pcache + tout * pout) / 1e6 * mult
        total += per * share * TICKETS
    return round(total, 2)


def evaluate(o: Option, ceiling: float = CEILING) -> dict:
    cost = model_cost(o)
    savings = o.resolved * TICKETS * AGENT_COST          # efficiency pillar, in money
    return {"option": o.name, "model_cost": cost, "savings": round(savings), "net": round(savings - cost),
            "within_cost": cost <= ceiling, "meets_sla": o.p95_seconds <= SLA_P95_SECONDS}


OPTIONS = [
    Option("A: Opus 5.5 for every ticket", [("opus-5.5", 1.0, 9_000, 0.0, 700)], resolved=0.46, p95_seconds=11),
    Option("B: Haiku triage, Sonnet answers, cached KB", [("haiku-5.5", 1.0, 1_500, 0.0, 60),
           ("sonnet-5.5", 0.7, 9_000, 0.8, 600)], resolved=0.41, p95_seconds=6),
    Option("C: as B, Opus 5.5 fast mode for escalations", [("haiku-5.5", 1.0, 1_500, 0.0, 60),
           ("sonnet-5.5", 0.7, 9_000, 0.8, 600), ("opus-5.5", 0.3, 9_000, 0.8, 700)],
           resolved=0.45, p95_seconds=7, fast_mode=True),
]


def choose(options=OPTIONS, ceiling: float = CEILING) -> dict:
    feasible = [r for r in (evaluate(o, ceiling) for o in options) if r["within_cost"] and r["meets_sla"]]
    return max(feasible, key=lambda r: r["net"]) if feasible else {}


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for r in map(evaluate, OPTIONS):
        print(r)
    print("choose at $9,000 ceiling:", choose()["option"])
    print("choose at $2,000 ceiling:", choose(ceiling=2_000)["option"])

Compare competing proposals in the same units

Skill 1.5When several teams want the same budget for different features.

  • Express every proposal the same way: its expected change in a measured pillar, its cost, and its effect on the SLAs. Proposals described in different terms can’t be ranked.
  • Use the pillar the stakeholders named. If the brief puts efficiency first, a measurable saving beats a transformation feature with a vague benefit.
  • Count the knock-on costs: more human review, more evaluation work, slower responses somewhere else in the system.
  • Test uncertain bets small first. Pilot the transformation feature with a measure and a rule for stopping it, rather than committing the whole budget.

Key takeaways

  • Choose the option with the most measured business value that stays inside every stated limit; the usual wrong answer is the best-quality option that breaks one.
  • Measure each pillar: efficiency as work removed times its cost, productivity as output per person, transformation as expected revenue or retention with a way to check it; solution cost and SLAs are usually limits.
  • Order: remove options that break a limit, pick the highest value among the rest, say whether the answer depends on a close number, and prefer the simpler option when values are close.
  • Prices that move the sums: the model (Opus 5.5 $4/$20, Sonnet 5.5 $2/$10, Haiku 5.5 $0.10/$0.50 per million tokens), cache reads at 0.05 times input, batches at half price, fast mode at a premium for speed, US-only processing at 1.1 times, and mixing models.
  • Compare competing proposals in the same units, against the pillar the stakeholders named, and pilot uncertain bets.
