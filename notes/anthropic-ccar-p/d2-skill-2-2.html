2.2: System Prompt Diagnosis and Revision

Overview

Skill 2.2

System Prompt Diagnosis and Revision: "Diagnosing the underspecification in a system prompt that produces degraded or inconsistent output, and selecting the revision that constrains scope, format, and behavior without over-constraining legitimate variation."

Exam items show a system prompt and the symptoms it causes, then offer revisions: one too vague, one too rigid, one that addresses the gap. The developer view of revising prompts under regression tests is CCDV-F 6.2; choosing a technique such as few-shot or structured output is skill 2.3.

A vague system prompt makes the model guess, and guesses vary

Skill 2.2What this skill asks you to do.

When a system prompt leaves something out, Claude fills the gap with its best guess, and the guesses differ from one conversation to the next. That shows up as replies that are inconsistent, too long, out of scope or simply wrong. Questions on this skill show a system prompt and the problems it causes, then offer several revisions. Usually one is still too vague, one is so strict that it blocks legitimate requests, and one adds the missing information. You pick that one.

Anthropic suggests a simple test: show the prompt to a colleague who knows nothing about the task, and ask them to follow it. If they would have to guess, so will Claude. Its guidance describes Claude as a brilliant new employee who doesn’t yet know your norms.

Trace each symptom to what the prompt left out

Skill 2.2Each kind of bad output points to a particular gap.

  • It answers things it shouldn’t, such as legal advice or another customer’s details. The prompt never said what’s in scope, so the model is generally helpful. Fix: state the scope, and what to do with requests just outside it.
  • It invents specifics such as dates, prices or policy details. The prompt gave no rule for what to do when the answer isn’t known, and named no sources. Fix: name the sources to use, and say what to do when they don’t have the answer.
  • The format varies widely from one reply to the next. No length or layout was stated, so every reply guesses. Fix: state the format, with the reason for it.
  • It treats an emergency like a routine request. The prompt didn’t say which cases matter most. Fix: list the special cases and what to do in each.
  • Different users get different behaviour for the same request. The prompt relies on the user to supply context it should hold itself. Fix: put that context in the prompt.

Example: one prompt in three versions

Skill 2.2Too vague, then too strict, then balanced.

A property manager runs a chat assistant that answers tenants’ questions about repairs, rent and the building. A review of 300 logged chats found inconsistent and sometimes harmful replies. The three versions of its system prompt below were each tried on those same 300 chats.

  • Version 1 is too vague. It names a role and nothing else, so scope, what to do when unsure, format and priorities are all left to chance. The assistant gave legal advice on evictions, promised repair dates it couldn’t know, wrote anything from one line to 600 words, and treated water pouring through a ceiling as a routine ticket.
  • Version 2 is too strict. It answers each problem with an absolute rule in capitals. Each rule fixes one problem from version 1 and blocks something legitimate: a message with two topics is refused, the assistant can’t ask which flat the tenant lives in, and an emergency is squeezed into three bullet points instead of starting with the number to call. Refusals rose from 4% to 31%.
  • Version 3 adds the missing information. It states the scope and what to do just outside it (send legal questions to the property office), what to do when unsure (never promise a date; say what happens next), how to handle several topics and emergencies, and a format with its reason (“tenants write from their phones”).
tenant_prompts.txt (Tenant assistant prompts)Optional 
=== v1: underspecified (outputs drift) ===============================================
You are a helpful assistant for Harbourline Properties. Help tenants with their questions.

Observed on 300 logged chats:
  - answers questions on eviction law and rent disputes (scope never stated)
  - promises repair dates it has no way to know (no rule for unknowns)
  - replies range from one line to 600 words with headings (no format)
  - treats "water pouring through the ceiling" like a routine ticket (no priorities)

=== v2: over-constrained (fixes v1, breaks legitimate variation) ======================
CRITICAL: You MUST ONLY answer using the FAQ. NEVER say anything not in the FAQ.
ALWAYS reply in EXACTLY 3 bullet points. If in doubt, REFUSE.

Observed on the same 300 chats:
  - refuses "my lock is broken and I also need a parking permit" (two topics)
  - can't ask a clarifying question ("which unit?") because that isn't in the FAQ
  - forces an emergency into 3 bullets instead of "call this number now"
  - refusal rate up from 4% to 31%

=== v3: constrains scope, format and behaviour; leaves room for real variation ========
You answer tenants of Harbourline Properties about repairs, rent payments, parking,
moving in and out, and building rules. Tenants write from their phones, often while
something is going wrong, so keep replies short and practical.

Scope
- Use the tenant handbook and the tenant's open tickets (provided below) as your sources.
- For legal questions (eviction, deposit disputes, discrimination), don't give advice:
  say the property office handles these and give its contact details.

Behaviour
- Never promise a repair date or cost; say what happens next and who will confirm it.
- If you need a detail to help (unit number, which appliance), ask one short question.
- If a message covers several topics, answer each in turn.
- Emergencies (flooding, gas smell, fire, no heat below 10 C, someone hurt): first line is
  the 24-hour emergency number, then one sentence on what to do while waiting.

Format
- Plain text, under 120 words unless the tenant asks for detail; no headings.
- End with the next step the tenant should expect.

<handbook>...</handbook>
<open_tickets>...</open_tickets>

Too many rigid rules is the opposite failure

Skill 2.2When the fix becomes the problem.

Signs that a prompt is over-constrained:

  • Refusals rise on requests that are clearly in scope.
  • Every reply has the same shape, because an exact count (“exactly 3 bullets”) is forced onto content that doesn’t fit it.
  • The model can’t ask the question it needs to give a good answer.
  • Rules are shouted. Anthropic notes that newer models follow the system prompt closely, so wording such as “CRITICAL: You MUST” now tends to make them over-apply the rule. Its advice is to write it calmly, for example “Use this tool when...”, and its migration guidance names lines like “If in doubt, use [tool]” as a cause of over-use.

“Add CRITICAL and NEVER to the rules it’s breaking” is the tempting revision, and the wrong one on current models. What fixes a vague prompt is information (scope, sources, special cases, reasons), not louder rules.

A good revision states scope, format and behaviour, and leaves room

Skill 2.2The three things the guide names, and nothing more.

  • Scope: what’s in, what’s out, and what to do at the edge (send the tenant to the property office) rather than a bare refusal.
  • Format: length and layout, with the reason behind them. Anthropic notes that Claude generalises from the reason, so it also handles cases the rule didn’t list.
  • Behaviour: the cases that matter (unknown answers, several topics, emergencies) and the sources to use, so specifics come from data rather than invention.
  • Room for real variation: ranges (“under 120 words unless asked for more”) instead of exact counts, and permission to ask one short clarifying question.
  • Say what to do, not only what not to do: “say what happens next and who will confirm it” rather than just “don’t promise dates”.

Prove the revision on the same chats, in both directions

Skill 2.2Fewer failures, and no rise in refusals.

  • Check both directions. The revision must reduce the original failures (promised dates, missed emergencies, overlong replies) and must not raise the refusal rate, which is the sign of over-constraining.
  • Use the same chats for every version, including messages with several topics and emergencies, so the comparison is fair.
  • Combine automatic checks with reading. Simple pattern checks catch the obvious regressions; a person still needs to read a sample of replies.

The code below runs those pattern checks over a set of replies and counts each kind of failure:

check_replies.py (check_replies.py)Optional 
"""Score logged replies against the behaviours a system prompt revision is meant to fix.

Run it on the same eval chats for each prompt version: a revision should fix the v1 failures
without raising the refusal rate (the sign of over-constraining)."""
import re

DATE_PROMISE = re.compile(r"\b(by|on|before)\s+(mon|tue|wed|thu|fri|sat|sun|tomorrow|\d{1,2}(st|nd|rd|th)?\b)", re.I)
REFUSAL = re.compile(r"\b(can't help with that|unable to assist|outside what I can)\b", re.I)
EMERGENCY_IN = re.compile(r"\b(flood|water pouring|gas|fire|no heat|hurt|injur)", re.I)


def check(tenant_msg: str, reply: str) -> list[str]:
    issues = []
    if DATE_PROMISE.search(reply):
        issues.append("promises a date")
    if len(reply.split()) > 120:
        issues.append("too long")
    if EMERGENCY_IN.search(tenant_msg) and "0800 555 0199" not in reply.splitlines()[0]:
        issues.append("emergency number not first")
    if REFUSAL.search(reply):
        issues.append("refusal")
    return issues


def score(pairs) -> dict:
    results = [check(m, r) for m, r in pairs]
    n = len(results)
    rate = lambda label: sum(label in r for r in results) / n
    return {"clean": sum(not r for r in results) / n, "refusal_rate": rate("refusal"),
            "date_promises": rate("promises a date"), "emergency_misses": rate("emergency number not first")}

Practice questions

Exam-style questions written for these notes. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
A banking assistant sometimes quotes interest rates that don’t exist. Its system prompt names no sources and says nothing about what to do when unsure. Which revision addresses the gap?
  1. A. Add “NEVER make up numbers” in capitals
  2. B. Name the rate table as the source, and say to state that a rate isn’t available when it isn’t there
  3. C. Ask for shorter replies
  4. D. Add more examples of greetings
Answer
B
Why
Invented specifics come from no named sources and no rule for unknowns. Adding that information fixes the gap; shouted rules tend to make current models over-apply them.
Question 2
Scenario
After a prompt revision, failures dropped but the refusal rate rose from 3% to 25% on requests that are clearly in scope. What does this show?
  1. A. The revision worked
  2. B. The prompt is now over-constrained
  3. C. The model is broken
  4. D. The evaluation set is wrong
Answer
B
Why
Rising refusals on legitimate requests are the sign of over-constraining. A good revision reduces failures without raising refusals.
Question 3
Scenario
Which instruction gives the model room for legitimate variation while still constraining the format?
  1. A. “Reply in exactly 3 bullet points”
  2. B. “Keep replies under 120 words unless the user asks for detail; customers read on their phones”
  3. C. “Never ask questions”
  4. D. “Always use headings”
Answer
B
Why
A range with the reason behind it constrains length while fitting different content; Anthropic notes Claude generalises from the reason. Exact counts force one shape onto everything.
Question 4
Scenario
How should a prompt revision be tested?
  1. A. On new chats only
  2. B. On the same chats as the old version, checking that failures fall and refusals don’t rise
  3. C. By reading the prompt carefully
  4. D. By asking the model whether it is better
Answer
B
Why
Using the same chats makes the comparison fair, and checking both directions catches over-constraining as well as the original failures.

Key takeaways

  • A gap in the system prompt makes the model guess, and its guesses vary; Anthropic’s test is whether a colleague with no context could follow the prompt without guessing.
  • Trace each symptom to its gap: out-of-scope answers to missing scope, invented specifics to no sources or rule for unknowns, varying replies to no format, mishandled emergencies to missing priorities.
  • A good revision states scope (and what to do at its edge), format with a reason, and behaviour for the cases that matter, and leaves room with ranges and a clarifying question.
  • Over-constraining shows as rising refusals and forced shapes; on current models, shouted rules (“CRITICAL”, “MUST”, “If in doubt”) make the model over-apply them.
  • Test every version on the same chats: failures down, refusals not up, plus a human read of a sample.

Official sources
