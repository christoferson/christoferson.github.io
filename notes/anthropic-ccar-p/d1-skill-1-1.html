Overview

Skill 1.1

Task Delegation Between Claude and Humans: "Determining which components of a described business process should be delegated to Claude, retained by humans, or handled collaboratively, and justifying each assignment against reversibility, stakes, and accountability."

Exam items describe a process and a proposed split, and ask which assignment is wrong or which revision fixes it, including splits that are too cautious. The business-user view of the same idea is in the Associate notes (CCAO-F 4.2); this page adds the architect's job of making the split enforceable.

Each step goes to Claude, to Claude and a person, or to a person

Skill 1.1Three ways to assign a step, and what each looks like in a running system.

A business process is a chain of steps: read a document, check a rule, write a letter, pay someone. Putting Claude into the process means deciding, step by step, who does what. There are three possible answers.

  • Delegated to Claude. Claude does the step and its result is used without a person checking each one. This fits steps that can be undone, that cost little when wrong, or whose output a person looks at later anyway. You still watch quality, by sampling results and tracking error rates, but nobody approves each item.
  • Collaborative. Claude does the work up to the point where something happens, then a person approves, edits or rejects it before it takes effect. Or the other way round: a person decides, and Claude gathers the evidence they need. This fits steps that are hard to undo or expensive to get wrong, but where most of the work can still be automated.
  • Retained by a person. The decision is a person's. Claude may prepare a summary for them, but it has no way to perform the step itself. This fits decisions that a named person must answer for, or that the law requires a person to make.

Exam questions describe a process and a proposed split, and ask which assignment is wrong or which change fixes it. A split can be wrong in either direction: Claude acting alone where it shouldn’t, or people checking steps that don’t need it.

Ask first: can the effect be undone?

Skill 1.1Reversibility: how much it costs to put a mistake right.

Some effects can be corrected cheaply and completely. If Claude reads an invoice and records the wrong date, someone can fix the field and nothing else has happened. Other effects can’t simply be withdrawn: a payment that has left the account, a letter the customer has already read, a decision that has been announced.

The more a step’s effect can be undone, the more safely it can be delegated. A step that can’t be undone needs a person to look before it happens, because afterwards it’s too late. This is also why it helps to make reversible steps reversible in practice: if every record keeps its earlier versions and every change is logged, more steps can be delegated safely.

Then ask: what does a wrong result cost, in this case?

Skill 1.1Stakes: the harm a mistake does, and to whom.

Stakes are what a wrong result costs: money, someone’s home or income, legal exposure, a finding from a regulator. The important detail is that stakes belong to the case, not only to the kind of step. Paying out 300 and paying out 300,000 use the same payment tool, but a mistake in the second one is a thousand times worse.

So the assignment can depend on the case. A rule such as “small payments that pay a request in full go through automatically; anything larger waits for a person” lets most cases flow and stops the risky ones. Write such rules down as code that checks the amount and the other conditions; don’t leave the choice to how confident the model sounds.

Finally ask: who has to answer for the decision?

Skill 1.1Accountability: a person, by law, regulation or role.

Some decisions must be owned by a named person: someone licensed for that work, a doctor, a manager who signs off. An agent can’t hold that responsibility. When a person is accountable, that person makes the decision or approves it, however accurate the model is.

Anthropic’s Usage Policy turns this into rules for its high-risk areas, such as insurance, finance and employment. When an AI recommendation affects a person, a qualified person must review it meaningfully before it’s acted on, must be able to change it, and stays responsible for the outcome; the affected person must also be told that AI was used. There is one exception: an outcome entirely in the person’s favour, such as paying a request in full, can skip the review where the law allows.

“The agent is accurate enough, so let it reject clearly invalid requests” mixes up accuracy and accountability. A rejection is an adverse decision that a named person must answer for, and in a high-risk area it needs qualified human review whatever the model’s error rate.

The three answers together decide the assignment

Skill 1.1Two questions settle almost every step.

In practice reversibility and stakes combine into one question: is this step risky, meaning hard to undo or costly if wrong for this particular case? Accountability is the second question.

  • Not risky: delegate it, and monitor with sampling.
  • Risky, but no person has to own the decision: make it collaborative. Claude prepares, and a person approves before the effect.
  • A person is accountable: retain it. Claude can assemble the evidence, but the decision stays with that person.

Worked example: the steps of a payment request

Skill 1.1Each step, its assignment, and the reason for it.

Take any process where customers ask for money back and send documents to support the request. The steps, and where each belongs:

  • Reading the documents and pulling out the dates and amounts: delegated. Nothing changes in the world, any mistake can be corrected, and a reviewer sees the extracted fields before any decision is made.
  • Checking the request against the written rules: delegated, but to ordinary code, not to Claude. Fixed rules are applied the same way every time by a rules engine; Claude only explains the result in plain words.
  • Spotting signs of misuse: delegated, as a flag only. A flag sends the request to a person. It decides nothing by itself, so a wrong flag costs a little of someone’s time.
  • Drafting the reply to the customer: delegated. Sending it: collaborative. A draft can be rewritten; a message the customer has read can’t be unsent.
  • Paying: collaborative, with one delegated case. Small payments that pay the request in full, passed the rules check and carry no flag go through automatically: they’re entirely in the customer’s favour, and the business accepts the risk at that size. Every other payment waits for a reviewer.
  • Rejecting a request, or paying only part of it: retained. It goes against the customer, and a named person is accountable for it.

Enforce the split in code, not in the prompt

Skill 1.1“Retained” should mean the agent can’t do it, not that it was asked not to.

A sentence in the system prompt such as “never reject a request” is guidance: the model usually follows it, but a confusing case or a cleverly worded document can lead it astray. The assignment has to be enforced by code the model can’t talk its way past. In the Agent SDK that code is a permission callback, can_use_tool, which runs before every tool call that hasn’t already been approved some other way.

The code below gives the agent one rule per tool and a gate that applies them:

  • Every call reaches the gate. The Agent SDK checks hooks, deny rules, ask rules, the permission mode and allow rules first, and only then calls can_use_tool. Leaving allowed_tools empty means no tool is pre-approved, so nothing skips the gate.
  • Delegated tools run. Reading, extracting, flagging and drafting are allowed straight away, and each is written to the audit trail with its reason.
  • Collaborative tools wait for a person. Sending a letter or making a payment goes to a reviewer’s work queue, and the agent waits for the decision.
  • The payment exception is a rule. The automatic-payment check looks at the amount, the rules-check result, the flag and whether the payment covers the whole request. The model’s confidence plays no part.
  • Retained tools are always refused, with a message telling the agent to summarise the request and hand it over. Simpler still, don’t give the agent that tool at all.
  • Anything else is refused. A tool that isn’t in the list is denied by default, so a new tool can’t slip through unassigned.

"""Delegation for an agent that handles customer payment requests, enforced outside the model.

Each tool the agent can call is assigned AI-executed, collaborative (a person approves) or
human-retained (the agent can only hand over). The gate runs as the Agent SDK's can_use_tool
callback, so the assignment holds whatever the model decides to do."""
from dataclasses import dataclass

from claude_agent_sdk import ClaudeAgentOptions, PermissionResultAllow, PermissionResultDeny

AUTO_PAY_LIMIT = 1_500          # small payments that pay a request in full, after a rules check, need no review


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Rule:
    assignment: str             # "ai" | "collaborative" | "human"
    why: str                    # reversibility, stakes, accountability: kept for the audit trail


MATRIX = {
    "mcp__requests__read_documents":  Rule("ai", "read-only; nothing to reverse"),
    "mcp__requests__extract_fields":  Rule("ai", "draft data; a reviewer sees it before any decision"),
    "mcp__requests__flag_risk_signal": Rule("ai", "a flag only routes the request to a person"),
    "mcp__requests__draft_letter":    Rule("ai", "draft only; sending is a separate step"),
    "mcp__requests__send_letter":     Rule("collaborative", "external and can't be unsent"),
    "mcp__requests__issue_payment":   Rule("collaborative", "money leaves; small full payments excepted"),
    "mcp__requests__reject_request": Rule("human", "adverse decision: a named, accountable reviewer decides"),
}


def auto_payable(args: dict) -> bool:
    """The only payment the agent may make alone: small, passed the rules check, wholly in the customer's favour."""
    return (args.get("amount", 0) <= AUTO_PAY_LIMIT and args.get("rules_check") == "passed"
            and not args.get("risk_flag") and args.get("pays_in_full") is True)


def gate(approve, audit):
    async def can_use_tool(tool_name, tool_input, ctx):
        rule = MATRIX.get(tool_name)
        if rule is None:
            audit(tool_name, "denied", "not in the delegation matrix")
            return PermissionResultDeny(message=f"{tool_name} isn't assigned to this agent.")
        if rule.assignment == "ai" or (tool_name.endswith("issue_payment") and auto_payable(tool_input)):
            audit(tool_name, "allowed", rule.why)
            return PermissionResultAllow()
        if rule.assignment == "human":
            audit(tool_name, "handed over", rule.why)
            return PermissionResultDeny(message="This decision belongs to a named, accountable reviewer. Summarise the request "
                                        "and your findings with mcp__requests__draft_letter, then stop.")
        approved = await approve(tool_name, tool_input, rule.why)      # e.g. a reviewer's work queue
        audit(tool_name, "approved" if approved else "declined", rule.why)
        return PermissionResultAllow() if approved else PermissionResultDeny(message="The reviewer declined this step.")
    return can_use_tool


def options(approve, audit) -> ClaudeAgentOptions:
    return ClaudeAgentOptions(
        allowed_tools=[],                     # nothing pre-approved: every call reaches the gate
        disallowed_tools=["Bash", "Write", "Edit", "WebFetch", "WebSearch"],
        can_use_tool=gate(approve, audit),
        permission_mode="default",
        max_turns=25,
    )

Too many approvals is a design fault too

Skill 1.1Put the approval just before the effect, and nowhere it isn’t needed.

What goes wrong with too many. If a person must approve every lookup and every draft, queues build up, the process slows down, and reviewers learn to click “approve” without reading. A rubber-stamp review is worse than an honest automated step that is monitored, because it looks like control and isn’t.

What goes wrong with too few. A payment, a letter or a denial happens on the model’s say-so, or a decision a licensed person must own is made with nobody owning it.

What fixes it. Place each approval immediately before the action that can’t be undone or matters most (sending, paying, rejecting), not before the reading and drafting that lead up to it. Route by case, using the amount, the flags and the effect on the customer, so most claims flow through and only the risky ones stop.

A good justification names the effect, the risk and the control

Skill 1.1What to say when you defend an assignment.

  • What the step changes in the world when it runs: nothing (a read), a draft, money moving, a letter sent.
  • How it scores on each question: can it be undone, what does a mistake cost in this case, and who has to answer for it.
  • Any rule that applies, such as the Usage Policy’s review requirement for high-risk areas, or the company’s own limits on risk.
  • The control that enforces it: the agent has no tool for it, an approval gate, an amount threshold, a review queue, or sampling.
  • What evidence would change it: for example, months of monitoring showing that a collaborative step is approved unchanged 99% of the time might justify delegating its low-value cases.

Practice questions

Exam-style questions written for these notes. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
An agent helps process applications that a business approves or rejects. Which step should be retained by a person?
  1. A. Extracting figures from the supporting documents
  2. B. Drafting a summary of the application
  3. C. The final decision to reject an application
  4. D. Flagging documents that look altered
Answer
C
Why
A rejection is an adverse decision a named person must answer for, so it stays with that person. Extraction and drafting can be corrected and are checked later; a flag only routes the case to someone.
Question 2
Scenario
An agent pays small requests automatically when the payment covers the request in full, the rules check passed and there is no flag. Every other payment waits for a reviewer. Which assignment is this?
  1. A. Retained
  2. B. Collaborative, with a delegated case defined by a rule
  3. C. Fully delegated
  4. D. Delegated only for flags
Answer
B
Why
Payments are collaborative (a person approves before money moves), with one narrow delegated case for small payments entirely in the customer’s favour, defined by a rule in code rather than the model’s confidence.
Question 3
Scenario
A design gives the agent a reject_request tool, with a system-prompt line saying “never reject requests”. What is wrong?
  1. A. Nothing; the instruction is clear
  2. B. “Retained” should mean the agent can’t do it: remove the tool, or refuse it in code
  3. C. The tool should be renamed
  4. D. The prompt needs CRITICAL in capitals
Answer
B
Why
A prompt instruction is guidance the model usually follows; a retained decision must be enforced in code the model can’t talk past, by not giving it the tool or by refusing every call to it.
Question 4
Scenario
Reviewers must approve every lookup, draft and payment the agent makes. Approvals now take two days, and 99% are approved in seconds. What should change?
  1. A. Add more reviewers
  2. B. Gate only the actions that can’t be undone or matter most, and let reads and drafts run
  3. C. Remove all approvals
  4. D. Ask the agent to be more careful
Answer
B
Why
Too many approvals create queues and rubber-stamping. Put approval just before the effect that can’t be undone (paying, sending, rejecting) and route by case, so most work flows and the risky cases stop.

Key takeaways

  • Each step is delegated to Claude, collaborative (Claude prepares, a person approves before the effect), or retained by a person. A split can be wrong in either direction.
  • Three questions decide it: can the effect be undone, what does a mistake cost in this particular case, and who has to answer for the decision.
  • In high-risk areas such as insurance and finance, Anthropic’s Usage Policy requires meaningful review by a qualified person and telling the affected person that AI was used; outcomes entirely in the person’s favour may skip review where the law allows.
  • Accuracy isn’t accountability: a decision a licensed person must own stays with that person however good the model is.
  • Enforce the split in code the model can’t talk past, such as an Agent SDK can_use_tool gate with nothing pre-approved, rules for exceptions, refusal by default and an audit trail.
  • Put approvals just before the effect that can’t be undone, not before every read and draft; too many approvals turn into rubber-stamping.
