4.2: Deriving Evaluations from Business Processes

Starting from the business process rather than from a benchmark: finding the step Claude actually changes, turning the business outcome into a pass mark for that step, and choosing a mix of tests that looks like real traffic, different for a prompt tweak, a model swap and a release.

Overview

Skill 4.2

Deriving Evaluations from Business Processes: "Determining which portion of a business process constitutes a valid evaluation for a decision it must support (model change, prompt change, release gate), deriving the success criterion from the business outcome, and selecting the test-methodology mix that covers the deployed distribution."

Exam items describe a business process and a decision, and ask what to measure, against what, and with which tests. Finding a missing dimension after an incident is skill 4.1; designing a fair comparison between two configurations is skill 4.4.

Build the evaluation from the business process it serves

Skill 4.2What this skill asks you to decide.

Questions on this skill describe a business process and a decision to support: a prompt change, a model change, or a release gate. They ask three things: which part of the process to evaluate, what pass mark to set, and which mix of tests covers the inputs the system will really see.

Take a business that receives requests for a price quote by email. Claude reads each request, lists the items asked for and drafts a quote; a salesperson corrects it and sends it; some customers place an order weeks later. The team wants to change the prompt, is thinking about changing the model, and needs a release gate.

Evaluate the step Claude changes, labelled by what people actually did

Skill 4.2Four tests of whether a step makes a good evaluation.

  • Claude’s change shows up there. The draft quote is Claude’s work. Whether the customer orders depends on price, stock and the salesperson as much as on the draft.
  • The process already provides the right answer. The quote the salesperson actually sent is a correct answer for every past request, produced without any extra labelling work.
  • It’s fast enough for the decision. An order that arrives weeks later can’t judge a prompt change made this afternoon; a comparison against sent quotes can.
  • It’s tied to why the feature exists. Drafts the salesperson sends unchanged save time and avoid wrong prices, which is the point of the feature.

The slow outcome, whether customers order, still matters. It belongs in monitoring of the live system, not in the gate for a prompt change.

Turn the business outcome into a pass mark

Skill 4.2From “save salespeople time, never send a wrong price” to numbers.

  • The metric: the share of drafts sent with no change to any item (product code, quantity, unit price).
  • The gate: at least 85% overall, at least 75% in every segment, and no quote in the wrong currency at all, because one of those costs more than many small edits.
  • Why that shape: Anthropic’s guidance asks for criteria that are specific, measurable, achievable and relevant to the application’s purpose, and for several dimensions rather than one. A hard zero for the costly error is one way to say that some mistakes aren’t averaged away.

Sample the way production looks, and check every segment

Skill 4.2An overall average can hide a segment that fails completely.

  • Sample in proportion. 600 past requests, split to match the production mix, so scanned PDF attachments (7% of requests) and follow-up revisions (5%) appear as often as they really do. Anthropic’s test guidance says to mirror the real distribution of tasks and include edge cases.
  • Gate each segment. In the test, a release that scores 90% overall but 0% on scanned PDFs fails the gate; an overall average alone would have passed it.
  • Add edge cases history doesn’t contain: empty or irrelevant emails, very long requests, unclear quantities, hostile input.
  • Prefer many automatically graded cases to a few graded by hand, as Anthropic’s guidance does; comparing each field against the sent quote can be done in code.

The code below builds the sample from the production mix and applies the gate, overall and per segment:

derive_eval.py (derive_eval.py)Optional 
"""Derive an evaluation from a quote-to-order process.

Process: a quote request arrives by email -> Claude lists the items and drafts a quote -> a salesperson corrects and
sends it -> the customer orders (or doesn't) weeks later.
Claude affects only the draft. Conversion depends on price, stock and the salesperson, so it can't judge a
prompt change; the salesperson's corrections can. Label = the quote the salesperson actually sent."""
import random
from collections import Counter

PRODUCTION_MIX = {"standard catalogue": 0.62, "custom cut lengths": 0.18, "mixed currencies": 0.08,
                  "scanned PDF attachments": 0.07, "follow-up revisions": 0.05}

CRITERION = {
    "metric": "share of drafts sent with no item changed (product code, quantity, unit price)",
    "why": "a no-change draft is the business outcome the feature exists for: salesperson time saved, no wrong price sent",
    "release_gate": ">= 0.85 overall and >= 0.75 in every segment; no wrong-currency quote",
}

METHODS = {   # which decision needs which mix of tests
    "prompt change": ["replay 600 past quote requests, stratified by segment", "edge-case set"],
    "model change": ["replay 600 past quote requests, stratified by segment", "edge-case set",
                     "shadow mode on live requests for 1 week (drafts not shown to salespeople)"],
    "release gate": ["replay set at the gate thresholds", "edge-case set", "red-team set (injected instructions in emails)"],
}


def sample(history: list[dict], n: int, seed: int = 7) -> list[dict]:
    """Stratified by the production mix, so rare-but-real segments are present in proportion."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    by_seg = {}
    for r in history:
        by_seg.setdefault(r["segment"], []).append(r)
    picked = []
    for seg, share in PRODUCTION_MIX.items():
        k = max(1, round(n * share))                 # at least one case per segment
        picked += rng.sample(by_seg[seg], min(k, len(by_seg[seg])))
    return picked


def gate(results: list[dict]) -> tuple[bool, dict]:
    seg_total, seg_ok = Counter(), Counter()
    for r in results:
        seg_total[r["segment"]] += 1
        seg_ok[r["segment"]] += r["no_change"]
    overall = sum(seg_ok.values()) / len(results)
    per_seg = {s: seg_ok[s] / seg_total[s] for s in seg_total}
    wrong_currency = any(r.get("wrong_currency") for r in results)
    ok = overall >= 0.85 and min(per_seg.values()) >= 0.75 and not wrong_currency
    return ok, {"overall": round(overall, 3), **{s: round(v, 2) for s, v in per_seg.items()}}

Match the mix of tests to the decision

Skill 4.2The same process, three decisions.

  • A prompt change: replay the sampled past requests and the edge cases. It’s fast, cheap, and enough to catch regressions in the step that changed.
  • A model change: the same, plus a shadow period on live requests, where drafts are produced but not shown to salespeople, because a new model can behave differently on inputs the history doesn’t contain. Anthropic’s advice is to rerun your evaluations on your own prompts and data when you change model.
  • A release gate: the replay set at the gate thresholds, the edge cases, and a red-team set (instructions hidden in emails), because a release faces every kind of input.
  • Afterwards: monitor the order rate and how much salespeople edit drafts, the slow measures the gate couldn’t use.

“Use the order rate as the release metric” sounds closest to the business, but it’s slow and depends on things Claude doesn’t control. “Use a public benchmark” doesn’t measure the inputs this system will see at all.

Practice questions

Exam-style questions written for these notes. Decide your answer before reading the explanation.

Question 1
Scenario
An insurer uses Claude to draft claim summaries that adjusters edit before deciding. The team wants to test a prompt change this week. Which evaluation fits?
  1. A. Claim approval rates over the next quarter
  2. B. Drafts compared with the summaries adjusters actually finalised, on past claims
  3. C. A public summarisation benchmark
  4. D. Customer satisfaction scores
Answer
B
Why
The draft is the step Claude changes, the adjusters’ final summaries are ready-made correct answers, and the comparison runs today. Approval rates and satisfaction are slow and depend on much more than the draft; a public benchmark doesn’t reflect these claims.
Question 2
Scenario
An evaluation of an extraction feature scores 92% overall. Handwritten forms are 6% of production and score 10%. What should the release gate do?
  1. A. Pass, because the overall score is above target
  2. B. Fail, because a per-segment floor is broken
  3. C. Remove handwritten forms from the evaluation set
  4. D. Raise the overall target to 95%
Answer
B
Why
A gate needs floors for each segment, or an average hides a segment that barely works. Removing the segment hides the problem; a higher overall target can still be met while one segment fails.
Question 3
Scenario
A team is switching to a newer model. Besides replaying past requests, what does Anthropic’s guidance and good practice suggest?
  1. A. Nothing more; newer models are always better
  2. B. A shadow period on live requests whose outputs aren’t shown to users
  3. C. Testing only on the provider’s published benchmarks
  4. D. Releasing to everyone and watching complaints
Answer
B
Why
A new model can behave differently on inputs the history doesn’t contain, so run it on live traffic without acting on its outputs, and rerun your own evaluations. Published benchmarks aren’t your distribution.
Question 4
Scenario
For a quoting feature, which pass mark is best derived from the business outcome “save salespeople time and never send a wrong price”?
  1. A. Average quality rating of 4 out of 5
  2. B. At least 85% of drafts sent unchanged, at least 75% per segment, and zero wrong-currency quotes
  3. C. Fewer than 10 seconds per draft
  4. D. The model’s confidence above 0.9
Answer
B
Why
It measures the saving (drafts sent unchanged), protects every segment, and makes the costly error a hard limit. A rating is vague, speed isn’t the stated outcome, and the model’s confidence isn’t a business measure.

Key takeaways

  • A valid evaluation sits at the step Claude changes, uses what people actually did as the answer, is fast enough for the decision, and is tied to the business outcome.
  • Turn the outcome into a measurable pass mark, with floors for each segment and hard limits for the costly mistakes.
  • Sample in proportion to production, add edge cases, grade automatically where possible, and gate every segment, not just the average.
  • Match the tests to the decision: replay for a prompt change; replay plus shadow traffic for a model change; replay, edge cases and red-team sets for a release gate; monitoring for slow outcomes.
  • Slow, confounded business metrics and public benchmarks make poor gates.
