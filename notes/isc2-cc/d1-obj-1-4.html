1.4: Understand cybersecurity controls

The safeguards that actually reduce risk: rules and people-based measures, technology, and protection of buildings and equipment, plus what each control is meant to do and how to check that it works.

Overview

Objective 1.4

Understand cybersecurity controls.

In objective 1.2, "mitigate" meant applying controls to lower a risk. This objective is about the controls themselves. They are usually sorted two ways: by how they work (administrative, technical or physical, the three sub-topics of this objective) and by what they do (prevent, deter, detect, correct, compensate). Exam questions often give a control and ask for its category, or describe a problem and ask which kind of control fixes it.

Examples come mainly from the NIST SP 800-53 control catalog and CISA's Cybersecurity Performance Goals. The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer from the earlier objectives: 800 staff, 60 stores with tills and stockrooms, a head office with a small server room, an online shop, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools.

What a control is

Objective 1.4Knowing what counts as a control, and the three broad ways controls are grouped by how they work.

NIST defines a security control as "the safeguards or countermeasures prescribed for an information system or an organization to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the system and its information." The words control, safeguard and countermeasure mean the same thing: NIST SP 800-12 calls safeguards "synonymous with security controls and countermeasures". A privacy control is the same idea aimed at privacy: "the administrative, technical, and physical safeguards employed within an agency to ensure compliance with applicable privacy requirements and manage privacy risks."

CategoryAlso calledHow it worksRetailer examples
AdministrativeManagerial; NIST's older "management" and much of "operational"Policies, procedures and decisions that direct what people doSecurity policy, background checks, training, separation of duties
TechnicalLogicalCarried out by hardware, software or firmwareMFA, firewalls, encryption, antivirus, audit logging
PhysicalPhysical and environmentalProtect buildings, equipment and people in the physical worldLocks, badge readers, CCTV, guards, fire suppression

The US health law HIPAA uses exactly these three words. Its administrative safeguards are "administrative actions, and policies and procedures" to manage security measures and "the conduct of the ... workforce"; physical safeguards are "physical measures, policies, and procedures to protect ... electronic information systems and related buildings and equipment, from natural and environmental hazards, and unauthorized intrusion"; technical safeguards are "the technology and the policy and procedures for its use" that protect health information and "control access to it".

One control can belong to more than one category. NIST dropped fixed categories from its catalog in SP 800-53 Rev. 4, because "many security controls ... have various combinations of management, operational, and technical properties". Rev. 5 says simply: "Controls can include administrative, technical, and physical aspects." A badge system is physical (the door) and technical (the software checking the badge), and is governed by an administrative policy. On the exam, pick the category that describes the control's main mechanism.

Older NIST terms. FIPS 200 and SP 800-53 Rev. 3 used management controls ("focus on the management of risk"), operational controls ("implemented and executed by people (as opposed to systems)") and technical controls. Under that scheme, physical protection and personnel security were both "operational". The exam's administrative/technical/physical split is the one used in HIPAA and in NIST's privacy control definition.

Administrative controls

Objective 1.4Controlling risk through rules, decisions and how people are hired, trained, organized and supervised.

Administrative controls set the rules and manage people. They include the policies, standards and procedures from objective 1.3, and the risk management processes from 1.2. Many incidents start with a person, such as a phished password or a careless action, so these controls matter as much as any technology.

ControlWhat it does (NIST SP 800-53)Retailer example
Separation of duties (AC-5)Splits sensitive tasks so one person cannot misuse them alone; it reduces the risk of "malevolent activity without collusion"The person who adds a new supplier cannot also approve payments to it
Least privilege (AC-6)Allows "only authorized accesses ... that are necessary to accomplish assigned organizational tasks"Store staff can sell and refund, but only managers can change prices
Personnel screening (PS-3)Checks people before giving access, for example "background investigations"Reference and background checks for staff who handle cash or customer data
Personnel termination (PS-4)Disables access, revokes credentials, holds an exit interview and retrieves company property when someone leavesBadge and accounts disabled on a leaver's last day; laptop returned
Training and awareness (AT-2)Training when people join and regularly after, updated with lessons from incidentsAnnual phishing and card-fraud training for all store staff
Rules of behavior (PL-4)Written rules for using systems, with a "documented acknowledgment" before access is givenNew staff sign the acceptable use policy before getting an account

Two more administrative controls come from banking, where they are long-established fraud controls. The US FDIC's examination manual says banks should require staff to be away from their duties for "not less than two consecutive weeks" (mandatory vacation), because fraud "usually require[s] a perpetrator's ongoing presence to manipulate records". Job rotation works the same way: someone else doing the job may spot irregularities, and the FDIC advises that rotations "should not be announced ahead of time".

Separation of duties, dual control (two people needed together, such as opening a safe) and job rotation all target the insider. After them, "only collusion can bypass" the control, as the FDIC puts it: two or more people would have to conspire.

Technical controls

Objective 1.4Using hardware and software to enforce security automatically, without relying on people to remember the rules.

FIPS 200 describes technical controls as those "primarily implemented and executed by the information system through mechanisms contained in the hardware, software, or firmware components of the system." Their strength is consistency: a firewall rule applies every time, whether or not anyone is watching.

ControlWhat it doesRetailer example
Access enforcement (AC-3)Enforces "approved authorizations for logical access"The till software blocks price changes for non-managers
Identification and authentication (IA-2)Uniquely identifies and authenticates each user, often with MFASecurity keys for head-office staff (factors are covered in objective 1.1)
Boundary protection (SC-7)Monitors and controls traffic at network edges, using "gateways, routers, firewalls" and similarA firewall separating the till network from the guest Wi-Fi
Encryption (SC-8, SC-28)Protects data in transit and at restTLS to the payment processor; encrypted loyalty database
Malicious code protection (SI-3)Detects and removes malware "at system entry and exit points", and updates itselfAnti-malware on every till and laptop
Logging and monitoring (AU-2, AU-6, SI-4)Records events ("an event is an observable occurrence in a system"), reviews them and watches for attacksAll login and refund events sent to a central log system with alerts

CISA's Cybersecurity Performance Goals 2.0 (December 2025) pick out the technical controls that matter most for small and medium organizations, including multi-factor authentication "using the strongest available method" (phishing-resistant, such as FIDO/WebAuthn), patching known vulnerabilities "in a timely manner", least privilege, central log collection, malicious code detection, revoking departing staff's credentials, and backups.

A technical control still needs an administrative one behind it. HIPAA's own definition of technical safeguards includes "the policy and procedures for its use". A firewall with no rule-change procedure, or logs nobody is assigned to review, gives far less protection than it appears to.

Physical controls

Objective 1.4Protecting buildings, equipment and people from intruders, accidents and the environment, without ever putting people's safety at risk.

A perfect password policy is useless if someone can walk into the server room and carry the server out. NIST SP 800-12 describes physical and environmental security as "measures taken to protect systems, buildings, and related supporting infrastructure against threats associated with their physical environment." In SP 800-53 these controls make up the Physical and Environmental Protection (PE) family.

Controlling who gets in

  • Access devices: "keys, locks, combinations, biometric readers, and card readers" (PE-3). Keys and combinations are changed when they are lost or when someone who holds them leaves.
  • Visitors are escorted and their activity controlled, and physical access is logged.
  • Barriers such as "bollards, concrete slabs, jersey walls, and hydraulic active vehicle barriers" stop vehicles reaching a building.
  • An access control vestibule is "a space between two sets of interlocking doors", designed to stop someone following an authorized person inside, which is called piggybacking or tailgating.

"Mantrap" is the old name. NIST's December 2020 errata to SP 800-53 Rev. 5 replaced "mantrap" with access control vestibule. You will still see the old term in older study material.

Watching and protecting the environment

ControlPurposeRetailer example
Monitoring physical access (PE-6)Detect and respond to physical security incidents using "guards, video surveillance equipment (i.e., cameras), and sensor devices"CCTV over tills and stockroom doors; alarm on the server room
Emergency power (PE-11)An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) gives near-instant power for an orderly shutdown or switch to a generatorUPS units for the head-office server room
Fire protection (PE-13)Fire detection and suppression "supported by an independent energy source"Smoke detectors and suppression in the server room
Environmental controls (PE-14)Keep temperature and humidity within limits, and monitor themServer-room air conditioning with a temperature alarm
Water damage protection (PE-15)Shut-off valves that are accessible, working and "known to key personnel"Staff know where the water shut-off is above the server room
Emergency lighting (PE-12)Automatic lighting covering "emergency exits and evacuation routes"Exit lighting in every store

People come first

Security controls must never trap people in an emergency. US workplace safety rules (OSHA, 29 CFR 1910.36) require that "employees must be able to open an exit route door from the inside at all times without keys, tools, or special knowledge." A panic bar that locks only from the outside is allowed; a lock that stops people leaving is not. When a question sets the protection of assets against the safety of people, human safety always wins.

The retailer's stockroom door can be locked against entry from the shop floor, but must open freely from inside. Electronic badge doors are linked to the building's life safety systems; NIST SP 800-116 describes this as connectivity that "will ensure egress in the event of an emergency".

What controls do

Objective 1.4Sorting controls by their purpose: stopping an attack, discouraging it, spotting it, limiting the damage, or standing in for a control that cannot be used.

The second way to classify a control is by its function. NIST IR 8286 Rev. 1 gives these types:

FunctionNIST IR 8286 Rev. 1Examples
Preventive"Reduce or eliminate specific instances of a vulnerability"MFA, a locked door, separation of duties
Deterrent"Reduce the likelihood of a threat event by dissuading a threat actor"Login warning banners, "the threat of prosecution", visible cameras and signs
Detective"Provide warning of a successful or attempted threat event"Log monitoring with alerts, intrusion detection, a door alarm, an audit
Corrective"Reduce exposure by offsetting the impact of consequences after a risk event"Removing malware, restoring from backup, patching after an incident
Compensating"Apply one or more controls to adjust for a weakness in another control"Extra monitoring of an old till that cannot run MFA

Two more labels appear in many study guides, though NIST does not define them: recovery controls restore normal operations after an incident (backups, disaster recovery sites), and directive controls tell people what to do (policies, signs, procedures). Treat them as common industry terms.

Combining the two views

Every control has both a category and a function, so the two views form a grid:

FunctionAdministrativeTechnicalPhysical
PreventiveBackground checksFirewall rulesLocked server room
DeterrentPublished disciplinary policyLogin warning banner"CCTV in operation" signs
DetectiveReviewing access lists every quarterAlerts on unusual refundsMotion sensors, CCTV recording
CorrectiveRetraining after a phishing failureRestoring files from backupFire suppression putting out a fire

Deterrent or preventive? A deterrent works by persuading the attacker not to try; a preventive control stops them even if they try. A sign saying "alarmed door" deters; the lock on the door prevents. A camera that someone watches live can also detect, so read the question for what the control is being used for.

Compensating controls

Sometimes the expected control cannot be used: old equipment cannot run it, or it would stop the business working. A compensating control is "employed in lieu of" the normal control and provides "equivalent or comparable protection" (SP 800-53). It is a documented decision, not a shortcut. The FDIC gives an example: a bank without a two-week absence policy "should establish appropriate compensating controls". At the retailer, a payment terminal that cannot be patched is placed on its own isolated network segment and monitored closely until it is replaced.

Checking that controls work

Objective 1.4Confirming that a control is in place and actually reduces risk, rather than assuming it does.

A control that exists on paper may not work in practice. NIST measures control effectiveness as "whether a security or privacy control contributes to the reduction of information security or privacy risk." NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5 (January 2022) gives three assessment methods:

MethodWhat the assessor doesRetailer example
ExamineReviews, inspects or observes documents, mechanisms or activitiesRead the leavers procedure and the access logs for last month's leavers
InterviewTalks with people to understand how the control is really carried outAsk a store manager what happens on a leaver's last day
TestExercises the control "to compare the actual state ... to the desired state or expected behavior"Try a leaver's badge on the stockroom door; it should fail

Assessors usually combine the methods: documents and interviews show what is supposed to happen, and a test shows what actually does. Measuring effectiveness over time with metrics is covered in objective 2.4, and security testing such as penetration tests in objective 5.5.

Controls for AI systems

Objective 1.4Protecting AI tools with the same three kinds of control as any other system, adapted to what makes AI valuable: its data and its model.

The CC outline expects candidates to know that AI tools need a mix of technical, administrative and physical controls. Joint guidance from the NSA, CISA, the FBI and allied agencies, Deploying AI Systems Securely (April 2024) and AI Data Security (May 2025), gives examples in all three categories:

CategoryExamples from the guidance
TechnicalPhishing-resistant MFA and privileged access workstations for administrators; role- or attribute-based access control; running models in hardened containers; firewalls with allow lists; encrypting data; immutable backups; monitoring and logging
AdministrativeDefining each stakeholder's roles; requiring a threat model from the AI system's developer; checking the system is "within the organization's risk tolerance"; training users and developers; external audits; two-person control for access to model weights; classifying AI outputs at the same level as their input data
PhysicalStoring model weights in a protected vault in a highly restricted zone; hardware protections against side-channel attacks; certified storage devices; securely erasing drives before disposal

At the retailer, the customer-service chatbot gets an owner and a risk assessment (administrative), access only to the order data it needs and full logging of its conversations (technical), and runs in a cloud data center whose physical controls are the provider's responsibility under the shared responsibility model, covered in objective 4.3.

A NIST control set for AI is in progress. NIST is building SP 800-53 "control overlays" for AI systems: a concept paper appeared in August 2025 and an annotated outline in January 2026, but no public draft yet. In October 2026 NIST renamed the project "Control Overlays for Super Intelligence Systems (COSIS)", citing a September 2026 US Executive Order that tells federal agencies to say "super intelligence" instead of "artificial intelligence". The CC outline still says AI; expect both terms in US government sources from late 2026.

  • Cybersecurity Performance Goals 2.0 (CISA): a short list of high-impact baseline practices.

Key takeaways

  • Control, safeguard and countermeasure are synonyms: anything that protects confidentiality, integrity and availability.
  • By mechanism: administrative (policies, procedures, people management), technical or logical (hardware and software), physical (buildings, equipment, environment). One control can span several; choose its main mechanism.
  • Administrative examples: separation of duties, least privilege, background checks, leaver procedures, training, acceptable use agreements, mandatory vacation, job rotation.
  • Technical examples: MFA, access enforcement, firewalls, encryption, anti-malware, logging and monitoring. They still need procedures and people behind them.
  • Physical examples: locks, badges, guards, CCTV, bollards, access control vestibules (formerly "mantraps") against tailgating, UPS, fire suppression, climate control. Life safety comes first: exit doors must always open from inside.
  • By function: preventive stops, deterrent discourages, detective warns, corrective limits damage afterwards, compensating stands in with equivalent protection. Recovery and directive are industry terms NIST does not define.
  • Assess controls by examine, interview and test (SP 800-53A); a control only counts if it actually reduces risk.
  • AI systems need all three categories, focused on protecting data and model weights.
