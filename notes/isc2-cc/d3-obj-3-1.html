3.1: Understand identity life cycle management

How an identity is created, used, changed and removed: defining roles, giving people the access they need on day one, checking that access stays right as they change jobs, removing it the moment they leave, and the frameworks and tools that make this work, for staff, bots and AI agents alike.

Overview

Objective 3.1

Understand identity life cycle management. Sub-topics: roles definition, provision, review, deprovision, and frameworks and tools.

Identification, authentication and authorization (objective 1.1) assume each person or system already has an identity with the right access. This objective covers how that identity is managed from start to finish. Most access problems are life cycle problems: an account nobody removed, a person who kept old permissions after changing jobs, a shared admin password that never changed. How access decisions are made (least privilege, separation of duties and access control models) is objective 3.2.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools. Stores hire many seasonal staff before the holidays and let most of them go in January. Identities live in one cloud identity provider, fed from the HR system.

The identity life cycle

Objective 3.1Following an identity through its stages, from creation to removal, and knowing what should trigger each stage.

NIST defines a digital identity as "an attribute or set of attributes that uniquely describes a subject within a given context" (SP 800-63-4). A subject can be a person, a device or software. Identity and access management (IAM) is, in NIST's words, "establishing and managing the roles and access privileges of individual network users." The US federal identity playbook on idmanagement.gov describes life cycle management as handling a digital identity "throughout its lifecycle - creation, modification, and deactivation." For staff this is often called joiner-mover-leaver:

  1. Define rolesDecide in advance which access each job needs.
  2. Joiner: provisionVerify who the person is, create the account and give the access of their role.
  3. Mover: modifyWhen they change job, add the new access and remove what the old job needed.
  4. ReviewAt regular intervals, check that everyone still needs what they have.
  5. Leaver: deprovisionWhen they leave, disable the account and revoke all access, then delete it later.

The trigger for each stage should be an authoritative record, not an email. The federal playbook puts it plainly: "The HR/personnel system is the sole driver of identity creation and termination." At the retailer, a new hire entered in the HR system creates their account automatically; a termination entered by a store manager disables it.

NIST SP 800-53 control AC-2 Account Management covers the whole cycle: define which account types are allowed, assign account managers, require approval to create accounts, "create, enable, modify, disable, and remove accounts" according to policy, monitor their use, review them regularly, and "align account management processes with personnel termination and transfer processes."

Defining roles

Objective 3.1Grouping access by job function so that each person gets a known, approved set of permissions rather than one-off grants.

NIST describes a role as "the set of named duties or job functions within an organization", and, in role-based access control, "a collection of permissions" tied to a position. Defining roles first means access is decided once per job, not argued case by case for every new hire. AC-2 asks organizations to set the prerequisites and criteria for joining each group or role, and to record for each account its authorized users, roles and privileges.

Many organizations give everyone a small set of birthright access (email, the intranet, HR self-service) and add role access on top. The retailer's role catalog has entries like this:

Role: Store associate (seasonal)
Birthright access
Staff email, scheduling app, HR self-service
Role access
Till login at their own store; stock lookup (read only)
Not included
Refunds above $50, back-office PC, loyalty database

Role owner

Each role has an owner who decides what it contains, and roles are kept as few and as simple as possible: hundreds of nearly identical roles are as hard to manage as individual grants. Access that a role does not include, such as an administrator right, needs a separate request and approval. Access control models, including role-based access control, are covered in objective 3.2.

Provisioning

Objective 3.1Creating an identity safely: confirming who the person is, getting approval, issuing unique credentials, and giving only the access the role allows.

Provisioning is creating an identity's accounts and granting its access. It starts with identity proofing, which NIST describes as the processes used to "collect, validate, and verify information about a subject", followed by enrollment, which gives the proofed person a subscriber account and binds their authenticators (password, security key, phone) to it. NIST SP 800-63A-4 (July 2025) sets three identity assurance levels, IAL1 to IAL3; IAL3 requires an in-person (or supervised kiosk) check with a trained agent. For an employer, HR checks of identity documents usually do this job.

Good provisioning, drawn from SP 800-53 and CISA's guidance:

  • Approved: access requests are approved by named people (AC-2), usually the manager for role access and the system owner for anything extra.
  • Unique: one identity per person, with a username that is not reused. SP 800-53 IA-4 calls for "preventing reuse of identifiers" for a defined period, so a new joiner never inherits a leaver's history or access.
  • Separate admin accounts: anyone who administers systems gets a second, privileged account used only for that work (CISA Performance Goals 2.0, goal 3.G).
  • Safe first credentials: default and temporary passwords are changed before first use (IA-5), and MFA is enrolled on day one.
  • Automated: the federal playbook aims for "'Day One' access is automated based on HR data", which is faster and makes fewer mistakes than manual tickets.

Automation between systems usually uses SCIM (System for Cross-domain Identity Management, RFC 7643 and 7644), a standard for creating, updating and removing users and groups across systems, for example from a company's identity provider into a cloud application. A simplified request that creates a user:

SCIM request: POST /scim/v2/Users (body, simplified) 
{
  "schemas": ["urn:ietf:params:scim:schemas:core:2.0:User"],
  "userName": "a.rivera@retailer.example",
  "name": { "givenName": "Alex", "familyName": "Rivera" },
  "active": true,
  "groups": [{ "value": "store-associate-0417" }]
}

Setting "active": false in a later update disables the same user, which is how the leaver step can be automated too.

Reviewing access

Objective 3.1Checking regularly that every account and permission is still needed, and catching access that has built up as people change jobs.

Access drifts. People change jobs, cover for colleagues and join projects, and each change adds permissions that are rarely taken away. This build-up is called privilege creep (or entitlement creep). Two controls counter it:

  • Mover checks: SP 800-53 PS-5 requires the organization to review and confirm the ongoing need for current access when someone is transferred, and to change it to match the new job. AC-2 asks for account managers to be told when a user is "terminated or transferred" or their need-to-know changes.
  • Periodic reviews (also called access recertification or attestation): AC-2 requires reviewing accounts "for compliance with account management requirements" at a defined frequency. CISA's Performance Goals 2.0 suggest "quarterly reviews of access permissions and role assignments" (goal 3.H).

In a review, each manager or system owner confirms or removes each person's access. The federal playbook calls for "regular, mandatory review of user entitlements by managers, with automated revocation if uncertified": access nobody vouches for is removed. Privileged accounts and access to sensitive data are reviewed more often and more carefully. A typical review item at the retailer:

Access review, Q3: loyalty database (production)
Reviewer
Head of e-commerce (system owner)
Accounts with access
14 (9 people, 3 service accounts, 2 unassigned)
Kept
7 people, 3 service accounts with named owners
Removed
2 people who moved to marketing in spring; 2 accounts with no owner
Follow-up
Add a mover trigger: department changes in HR now open a review task

Rubber-stamping defeats the review. A manager who clicks "approve all" on 300 lines has not reviewed anything. Reviews work when they are short, show what each permission allows and when it was last used, and go to someone who actually knows whether it is needed. Monitoring for unusual account use (AC-2(12)) complements them between reviews.

Deprovisioning

Objective 3.1Removing access promptly and completely when it is no longer needed, and finding the accounts that slipped through.

Deprovisioning removes an identity's access when someone leaves, a contract ends, or a system or bot is retired. SP 800-53 PS-4 Personnel Termination requires the organization, when someone leaves, to disable their system access within a defined time, revoke their credentials, hold an exit interview, retrieve badges, laptops and tokens, and keep access to the information they controlled. CISA's Performance Goals 2.0 (goal 3.D) call for "a defined and enforced administrative process to off board staff", covering contractors and vendors too, and "the revocation of all access to systems and facilities". Good practice:

  • Fast: the federal playbook aims for revocation "within minutes" through automation. Urgent cases (a dismissal, a suspected insider) are disabled before the person is told.
  • Disable first, delete later: disabling keeps logs and files for investigations and handover (AC-2(3), PS-4); the account is deleted after the retention period.
  • Complete: every system, including cloud apps, VPN, building badges and shared passwords the person knew, which are changed (AC-2 requires changing shared account passwords when someone leaves the group).
  • Inactive accounts disabled: CISA suggests disabling accounts "when inactive for a specified period (e.g., 30 days)", and AC-2(2) asks for temporary and emergency accounts to be removed automatically.

An account that stays active after its owner has gone is an orphaned account; the federal playbook describes it as an account that "is not linked to a valid, current digital identity or identifiable subject". Orphaned accounts are attractive to attackers because nobody notices when they are used.

A real case. CISA advisory AA24-046A (February 2024) describes attackers who logged in to a US state government organization's VPN with a former employee's administrator account, which "was not disabled immediately following the employee's departure". The password probably came from an unrelated data breach, and the account had no MFA. Inside, they found stored credentials for a second admin account and sold the data they gathered. CISA's first recommendation: "remove and disable accounts and groups from the enterprise that are no longer needed, especially privileged accounts."

At the retailer, the January end of seasonal contracts is an HR date, so 300 seasonal accounts disable themselves automatically, and a monthly report lists any account with no matching HR record or no login for 30 days.

Frameworks and tools

Objective 3.1Recognizing the main IAM frameworks, the standards that let systems share identities, and the categories of tools that run the life cycle.

Frameworks

  • NIST CSF 2.0, PR.AA (Identity Management, Authentication, and Access Control): "identities and credentials for authorized users, services, and hardware are managed by the organization" (PR.AA-01), and "access permissions, entitlements, and authorizations are defined in a policy, managed, enforced, and reviewed, and incorporate the principles of least privilege and separation of duties" (PR.AA-05).
  • FICAM, the US federal Identity, Credential, and Access Management architecture, splits IAM into five areas: identity management (creating, proofing, provisioning and deactivating identities), credential management (issuing and revoking credentials), access management (authentication, authorization and privileged access), federation (trusting identities from other organizations) and governance.
  • NIST SP 800-63-4 (July 2025), the Digital Identity Guidelines, in four volumes: the base model, 63A identity proofing and enrollment (IAL), 63B authentication (AAL, see objective 1.1), and 63C federation (FAL).
  • Zero trust: NIST SP 800-207 treats identity as the centre of access decisions, with no trust based on network location. CISA's Zero Trust Maturity Model 2.0 makes Identity the first of five pillars and moves organizations from permanent access with periodic review toward "just-in-time and just-enough access".

OAuth is not a login protocol. OAuth grants an application permission to do something; OpenID Connect, built on it, tells the application who the user is. SAML and OpenID Connect handle sign-in; SCIM handles creating and removing accounts. OAuth 2.1 is still a draft, so current security rules (no password grant, no implicit grant) come from RFC 9700.

Tools

  • Directory: the store of identities and groups; NIST describes a directory service as one that "stores identity information and enables the authentication and identification of people and machines."
  • Identity provider (IdP): "the party in a federation transaction that creates an assertion for the subscriber" (SP 800-63-4), in other words the service that signs users in and vouches for them to applications.
  • Single sign-on (SSO): "one account and its authenticators are used to access multiple applications". Fewer passwords, one place to enforce MFA, and one switch to turn off a leaver.
  • Identity governance and administration (IGA): tools that run access requests and approvals, connect to HR, push accounts out (often through SCIM) and run access reviews.
  • Privileged access management (PAM): tools that vault admin passwords, grant admin rights only for a limited time, and record privileged sessions.

NIST has no glossary definitions for IGA and PAM; they are product categories, and FICAM lists privileged access management as part of access management. CISA's required cloud settings for US agencies (the SCuBA baselines, mandatory under BOD 25-01) show these ideas as concrete rules for Microsoft Entra ID, for example "Legacy authentication SHALL be blocked", two to eight global administrators, and "Permanent active role assignments SHALL NOT be allowed for highly privileged roles."

Bots, service accounts and AI agents

Objective 3.1Running the same life cycle for identities that are not people: an owner, a defined role, unique credentials, regular review and removal when retired.

The CC outline states that AI "bots" and automated service accounts must go through the same formal life cycle as human users. NIST calls these non-person entities: "an entity related to information technology with a digital identity that acts in cyberspace, but is not a human actor." In many organizations they now outnumber staff, and they are easy to forget: nobody leaves HR when a script is retired.

The US federal Digital Worker Identity Playbook (April 2025) applies the life cycle to "an automated, software-based tool, application, or agent that performs a business task or process similar to a human user":

  • Roles: decide what the bot or agent needs before creating it, and give it only that.
  • Owner: a named human sponsor is accountable for it, with a custodian who runs it day to day.
  • Unique credentials: no shared service-account passwords. CISA's Performance Goals add: "No universal non-person entity (NPE) account passwords should be deployed." Credentials are never written into code (SP 800-53 IA-5(7)) and are rotated.
  • Review: recertified regularly, every year at moderate and high impact and every six months for critical ones.
  • Deprovision: when retired, revoke access, disable or remove the account, and archive its logs.

AI agents add a new question: when an agent acts for a user, whose authority is it using? NIST's National Cybersecurity Center of Excellence published a concept paper in February 2026 on software and AI agent identity and authorization, covering how to identify agents separately from people, use OAuth-style authorization, link each agent's actions back to the user who delegated them, and log what it does. CISA's Entra ID baseline added a rule in March 2026 that "Risky AI agents SHALL be blocked."

At the retailer, the customer chatbot has its own service identity with read-only access to order status and loyalty points, owned by the head of e-commerce, reviewed each quarter with the loyalty database, and listed in the AI inventory (objective 2.1). When a pilot AI tool was dropped, its account and API keys were revoked the same week.

Further reading

  • Identity Lifecycle Management Playbook (US General Services Administration, idmanagement.gov, updated 2026): creation, modification and deactivation, with orphaned accounts and automation.
  • AA24-046A: Threat Actor Leverages Compromised Account of Former Employee to Access State Government Organization (CISA and MS-ISAC, February 2024): what an undeprovisioned account led to.
  • CISA and NSA Release Enduring Security Framework Guidance on Identity and Access Management (CISA, March 2023): IAM best practices for administrators.
  • Accelerating the Adoption of Software and AI Agent Identity and Authorization (NIST NCCoE concept paper, February 2026): open questions for agent identities.

Key takeaways

  • The life cycle: define roles, provision (joiner), modify (mover), review, deprovision (leaver). HR or another authoritative record should trigger each step. SP 800-53 AC-2 covers it all.
  • Roles group the access a job needs; birthright access plus role access; each role has an owner. Extra or admin access needs separate approval.
  • Provisioning: proof identity (IAL1-3 in SP 800-63A-4), get approval, one unique identity per person, no reuse of identifiers (IA-4), separate admin accounts, change default credentials, enrol MFA. SCIM automates it.
  • Review: privilege creep builds up as people move. Re-check access on transfer (PS-5) and in periodic recertifications (CISA suggests quarterly); remove what nobody vouches for. Avoid rubber-stamping.
  • Deprovision fast and completely: disable first, delete after retention; revoke credentials, retrieve property, change shared passwords; disable inactive accounts (e.g. 30 days). Orphaned accounts are a favourite target (CISA AA24-046A).
  • Frameworks: CSF 2.0 PR.AA, FICAM (identity, credential, access, federation, governance), SP 800-63-4 (IAL, AAL, FAL), zero trust (SP 800-207, CISA ZTMM).
  • Standards: SAML and OpenID Connect for sign-in, OAuth for authorization, SCIM for provisioning, LDAP for directories, Kerberos inside networks. Tools: directory, IdP, SSO, IGA, PAM.
  • Bots, service accounts and AI agents follow the same life cycle: a human owner, least access, unique credentials not in code, regular recertification, and removal when retired.
