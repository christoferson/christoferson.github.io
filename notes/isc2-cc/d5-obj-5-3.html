5.3: Understand incident response

What happens once an incident is declared: the plan and playbooks that guide the response, containing and removing the attacker, handling evidence and sensitive incident data, telling the right people at the right time, and rehearsing it all in tabletop and other exercises.

Overview

Objective 5.3

Understand incident response (IR). Sub-topics: the data handling policy that implements the incident response plan (IRP), and IR exercises such as testing and tabletops.

Objective 5.2 covered spotting and triaging an incident. This objective covers responding to it: who does what, how damage is limited, how evidence and sensitive information are handled, and how the organization practises so the plan works under pressure. Keeping the business running during a disruption (business continuity and disaster recovery) is objective 2.2; the two plans should be kept in step.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools. The CISO is the incident lead; a retained incident response firm and outside lawyers are on call; the managed SOC detects and escalates.

The incident response life cycle

Objective 5.3Knowing the classic four phases of incident response and how NIST's 2025 guidance maps them onto the CSF 2.0 functions.

For years, incident response was taught as a four-phase cycle from NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 (2012):

  1. PreparationPolicy, plan, team, tools, training and exercises in place before anything happens.
  2. Detection and analysisSpotting, validating and prioritizing the incident (objective 5.2).
  3. Containment, eradication and recoveryLimiting the damage, removing the attacker and restoring operations.
  4. Post-incident activityLessons learned and improvements, feeding back into preparation.

NIST replaced that guide in April 2025 with SP 800-61 Rev. 3, which maps incident response onto the CSF 2.0 functions. It explains that the old cycle fit a time when incidents were rare, narrow and over "within a day or two"; today recovery can take "weeks or months." In the new model, "the preparation activities of Govern, Identify, and Protect are not part of the incident response itself" but broader risk management that supports it; incident response itself is Detect, Respond and Recover; and improvement (CSF category ID.IM) runs throughout, because "lessons learned ... should often be shared as soon as they are identified, not delayed until after recovery concludes."

Both models are still taught. Expect exam questions on the four phases (and on the order: contain before you eradicate). NIST says organizations "should use the incident response life cycle framework or model that suits them best", and CISA's federal playbooks still use phases: preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication and recovery, post-incident activities, and coordination.

Policy, plan, procedures and playbooks

Objective 5.3Knowing what the IR policy, plan, procedures and playbooks each contain, and who has a role in a response.

Incident response is documented in layers, like other policy (objective 1.3):

  • Policy: SP 800-61 Rev. 3 lists its elements: management commitment, purpose and objectives, scope, definitions of events and incidents, roles, responsibilities and authorities (including "which roles have the authority to confiscate, disconnect, or shut down technology assets"), guidelines for prioritizing and estimating severity, and performance measures.
  • Plan: "the roadmap for implementing the incident response capability" (CSF ID.IM-04): how the team is organized, how it communicates internally and externally, how it connects to business continuity, and how it will mature. It is reviewed periodically and after significant changes.
  • Procedures and playbooks: step-by-step instructions for common incident types. NIST: "Playbooks provide actionable steps or tasks for people to perform during various scenarios or situations." Typical playbooks cover ransomware, phishing, account compromise, lost devices and data leaks.

CISA's #StopRansomware Guide adds two practical points: keep the plan and its communications plan in hard copy and offline (attackers may encrypt the file server it lives on), and have it approved in writing by the chief executive or equivalent.

Who is involved

SP 800-61 Rev. 3 lists the roles: leadership (funding and decisions on high-impact actions "such as shutting down or rebuilding critical services"), incident handlers who "verify that an incident has occurred, collect and analyze data and evidence, prioritize ..., and act appropriately to limit damage, find root causes, and restore operations", technology staff, legal, public affairs, human resources (if an employee may be involved), physical security, the owners of affected assets, and third parties such as a managed SOC or cloud provider, whose duties should be "clearly defined in a contract". The retailer's plan lists each role with a named person, a deputy and out-of-hours contacts.

Contain, eradicate, recover

Objective 5.3Choosing containment actions, removing the attacker completely, and restoring safely, in the right order.

  • Containment means "preventing the expansion of an incident" (CSF RS.MI-01). CISA's playbooks put the aim as reducing impact "by removing the adversary's access": isolating affected hosts and network segments, blocking malicious addresses, disabling compromised accounts, rotating passwords and keys. Many tools now contain automatically, for example by moving an infected laptop to an isolated network.
  • Eradication removes the cause: "eliminate persistence mechanisms and entry points, such as deleting malware, disabling breached user accounts, and identifying and mitigating all exploited vulnerabilities" (RS.MI-02). CISA warns not to start until all means of persistent access are accounted for and evidence has been collected.
  • Recovery restores operations: "restoring systems from clean backups, rebuilding systems, replacing compromised files with clean versions, installing patches, changing passwords, and tightening security controls." NIST asks organizations to verify integrity and "remediate the root causes of the incident before production use" (RC.RP-05), restore in order of business priority, and formally declare the end of recovery with an after-action report (RC.RP-06).

Do not wipe the evidence, and do not tip off the attacker. Capture forensic images during containment, before reimaging. Shutting a machine down loses what is in memory. Visible actions such as resetting one password can alert an attacker who still has other ways in, so containment is planned across all known footholds at once. NIST adds that deliberately watching an attacker in a sandbox "delays containment" and should be discussed with legal first.

Handling incident data and evidence

Objective 5.3Applying a data handling policy during incidents: protecting sensitive incident records, collecting evidence in order of volatility, and keeping a chain of custody.

Incidents generate sensitive data of their own. NIST warns that "incident response records can contain sensitive information, such as data on exploited vulnerabilities, recent data breaches, and users who may have performed inappropriate actions", and asks organizations to protect their confidentiality and integrity and "ensure that only authorized personnel have access" (RS.AN-06). The retailer's data handling policy for incidents applies the classification scheme from objective 5.1:

  • Incident tickets, chats and reports are classified Restricted and kept in a separate case system, not the general help desk.
  • Discussion happens on an out-of-band channel, in case email or chat is compromised.
  • Copies of breached personal data are kept only as long as needed for the investigation and notification, then destroyed.
  • Information shared outside carries a TLP label (objective 5.2).

Evidence

Not every incident ends in court, but NIST notes that "collected incident data is still considered evidence" and should be collected and retained under the organization's evidence and retention procedures (RS.AN-07). NIST's forensics guide (SP 800-86) describes four steps: collection, examination, analysis and reporting, always preserving integrity, for example by comparing a cryptographic hash of the original with the copy and working only on the copy.

Collect the most short-lived data first. The order of volatility in RFC 3227 runs from CPU registers and cache, through memory and running processes, temporary files, disk, and remote logs, to archival media. Its advice: "Don't shutdown until you've completed evidence collection."

NIST describes the chain of custody as a process that tracks the movement of evidence through its collection, safeguarding and analysis, documenting each person who handled it, when it was collected or transferred, and the purpose of each transfer. A gap in it can make evidence unusable. One entry from the retailer's ransomware case:

Communication and reporting

Objective 5.3Telling the right people at the right time, through designated spokespeople, and knowing the reporting rules that apply.

NIST groups incident communication into four kinds: coordination among responders, formal notification of affected customers, staff, partners and regulators, public communication through the media, and voluntary information sharing (RS.CO). Procedures should say "what must be reported to whom and at what times (e.g., initial notification, regular status updates)", and contact with law enforcement and regulators goes through designated individuals "based on criteria in the incident response plan and management approval."

Legal deadlines vary: GDPR, state breach laws, payment card rules and the SEC each have their own clocks (objective 1.3), so the plan lists every one that applies. In US federal guidance, a breach of personal information "is a type of incident" that includes lost paper files and spoken disclosure, not only hacking (OMB M-17-12).

Organizations can report incidents to CISA (through its online form or report@cisa.gov) and to the FBI through a field office or ic3.gov. The Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act (CIRCIA, 2022) will require covered critical infrastructure entities to report significant incidents within 72 hours and ransom payments within 24 hours, but only once CISA issues its final rule.

CIRCIA is not in force yet. CISA proposed the rule in April 2024 and missed the October 2025 statutory deadline for the final rule; as of late 2026 it still says entities "are not required to submit" reports under CIRCIA. Do not treat the 72-hour and 24-hour deadlines as current law, though sector rules and contracts may already set similar ones.

Incident response exercises

Objective 5.3Choosing between discussion-based and operations-based exercises, running a tabletop, and turning the results into improvements.

Plans that have never been practised fail when needed. SP 800-61 Rev. 3 says procedures "can be tested or exercised periodically to verify their accuracy and can be used to help train new personnel", and that improvements should come from exercises "including those done in coordination with suppliers and relevant third parties" (ID.IM-02). CISA's Performance Goals ask for incident response plans to be drilled at least once a year. CISA runs its exercises using FEMA's Homeland Security Exercise and Evaluation Program (HSEEP, 2020), which defines seven types:

  • Discussion-based, to understand and develop plans: seminar (an orientation or overview), workshop (to produce a plan, policy or procedure), tabletop exercise and game (structured play, no real resources).
  • Operations-based, to validate plans with real-time action: drill ("validate a single operation or function", such as restoring one server), functional exercise (realistic and real-time, but "movement of resources is usually simulated") and full-scale exercise ("the most complex and resource-intensive", with real movement of resources across organizations).

HSEEP describes a tabletop exercise as "a discussion-based exercise in response to a scenario, intended to generate a dialogue of various issues to facilitate a conceptual understanding, identify strengths and areas for improvement". It needs an experienced facilitator, and participants decide "in a no-fault environment". CISA's free Tabletop Exercise Packages (CTEPs), over 100 of them, provide ready-made objectives, scenarios and discussion questions on topics such as ransomware, insider threats, supply chain compromise and executive decision-making; CISA's ransomware package reminds players "there is no hidden agenda, and there are no trick questions."

Retailer tabletop plan: ransomware in peak season
Objectives
Test escalation to leadership; decide on shutting down tills; check the notification clocks
Players
CEO, CFO, CISO, IT lead, legal, communications, HR, store operations, IR firm, managed SOC
Module 1
SOC reports unusual logins on a finance laptop; inject: a supplier says invoices look odd
Module 2
Store tills stop working on a Saturday; ransom note; inject: a reporter calls
Roles
Facilitator, note-taker, evaluator from internal audit
Afterwards
Hotwash straight away; after-action report and improvement plan with owners and dates within two weeks

Evaluation closes the loop. HSEEP's hotwash is "a meeting that provides an opportunity to discuss exercise strengths and areas for improvement immediately following the conduct of an exercise." It feeds an after-action report and an improvement plan: a "consolidated list of corrective actions, responsible parties, and a timeline for completion." HSEEP recommends a progressive program, from seminars and tabletops toward functional and full-scale exercises. At national level, CISA's Cyber Storm exercise brought together more than 2,200 players in April 2024. Testing of disaster recovery plans, with its parallel and full-interruption tests, is covered in objective 2.2.

Incident response and AI

Objective 5.3Extending plans, playbooks and exercises to cover attacks on AI systems and AI-driven attacks.

The CC outline expects playbooks that "account for these automated threats": attacks that move at machine speed, and incidents involving the organization's own AI systems. In June 2024 CISA's Joint Cyber Defense Collaborative ran what it called "the federal government's inaugural tabletop exercise with the private sector" on AI security incidents, followed by a second in September 2024. The result, the AI Cybersecurity Collaboration Playbook (January 2025), defines an AI cybersecurity incident as an occurrence that jeopardizes "the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of the AI system, any other system enabled and/or created by the AI system, or information stored on any of these systems", and suggests what to record and share: the affected model and version, how it was attacked, the harm, and fixes such as rolling back a model.

NIST's Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1) recommends tracking AI "errors, near-misses, and negative impacts", setting minimum fields for AI incident reports, and rehearsing response plans for third-party AI services "at a regular cadence". It uses a broader, harm-based definition of an AI incident: one where an AI system "directly or indirectly contributes" to harm such as injury, disruption of critical infrastructure, rights violations or damage to property.

The retailer added an AI playbook and two tabletop injects. The playbook covers the chatbot leaking another customer's order details: disable the chatbot's data connection, preserve the conversation logs, roll back the last prompt or model change, and assess notification. The injects: a deepfake video call from the "CFO" asking finance to pay a new supplier, and an alert that an attacker's automated tool is trying thousands of logins per minute, to test whether automatic containment fires before humans arrive.

Key takeaways

  • Classic cycle: preparation; detection and analysis; containment, eradication and recovery; post-incident activity. SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (2025) maps it to CSF 2.0: Govern, Identify and Protect support IR; IR itself is Detect, Respond, Recover; improvement runs throughout.
  • Policy (commitment, scope, definitions, roles and authorities, prioritization, measures), plan (the roadmap), playbooks (step-by-step by scenario). Keep a hard copy offline; leadership approves. Roles include leadership, handlers, legal, PR, HR, asset owners and third parties under contract.
  • Contain (stop the spread) before eradicate (remove persistence and entry points) and recover (clean restore, fix root cause, declare end, after-action report). Preserve evidence first; avoid tipping off the attacker.
  • Incident data is sensitive: restrict access, use out-of-band channels, retain only as needed, label sharing with TLP. Collect evidence in order of volatility (memory before disk), hash and work on copies, and keep a chain of custody.
  • Communicate through designated people; know every legal clock. Report to CISA or the FBI. CIRCIA (72 h incidents, 24 h ransom payments) awaits a final rule.
  • Exercises (HSEEP): discussion-based (seminar, workshop, tabletop, game) and operations-based (drill, functional, full-scale). Tabletops: facilitator, scenario modules, injects, no-fault. Then hotwash, after-action report and improvement plan with owners and dates. At least yearly; include third parties.
  • AI: playbooks for automated, machine-speed attacks and for incidents in the organization's own AI systems; record model versions and roll back; rehearse deepfake and AI scenarios.
