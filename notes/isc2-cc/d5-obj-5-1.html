5.1: Understand data security

Protecting data from creation to destruction: deciding how sensitive it is, labeling and handling it, hiding what does not need to be seen, wiping media properly, and the encryption and hashing that protect it, including the move to quantum-resistant algorithms.

Overview

Objective 5.1

Understand data security. Sub-topics: data handling (classification, labeling, masking and sanitization) and encryption (symmetric, asymmetric, hashing and quantum-resistant cryptography).

Confidentiality and integrity (objective 1.1) are goals; this objective covers the day-to-day ways of reaching them for data. Objective 1.1 already showed file hashing and digital signatures, so this page focuses on how data is classified and handled, and on how encryption, password hashing and the coming post-quantum algorithms work.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments, a cloud loyalty database with customer names, emails and purchase histories, and new AI tools. Staff increasingly paste text into AI assistants.

Data owners and the data life cycle

Objective 5.1Knowing who decides how data is protected, and the stages data passes through from creation to disposal.

Every important data set needs someone accountable for it. NIST describes the information owner as the official "with statutory or operational authority for specified information and responsibility for establishing the controls for its generation, collection, processing, dissemination, and disposal." In practice organizations use three roles:

  • Data owner: a business leader who decides the classification and who may access the data (the head of e-commerce for the loyalty database).
  • Data custodian: usually IT, which carries out the owner's decisions day to day: backups, access settings, encryption.
  • Data steward: looks after quality and correct use, and applies the policy consistently.

You cannot protect data you do not know about. NIST CSF 2.0 expects that "inventories of data and corresponding metadata for designated data types are maintained" (ID.AM-07). Data then moves through a life cycle; one common way to describe it is create, store, use, share, archive and destroy. NIST's draft classification guide (IR 8496) used four phases, identify, use, maintain and dispose, though NIST stopped developing that draft in December 2025. Controls apply at every stage, and the last stage is the one most often forgotten.

Classification

Objective 5.1Assigning data to sensitivity levels so that each level gets consistent protection, and recognizing the US government schemes.

NIST's draft guide describes data classification as "the process an organization uses to characterize its data assets using persistent labels so those assets can be managed properly." Each organization chooses its own levels; there is no official commercial scheme. The retailer uses four:

Retailer classification scheme
Public
Approved for anyone: prices on the website, press releases
Internal
Staff only, low harm if leaked: store rotas, intranet news
Confidential
Named groups only: supplier contracts, sales figures, customer emails
Restricted
Strictly need to know, encrypted, logged: card data, staff health records, security keys

The owner sets the level based on the harm if the data's confidentiality, integrity or availability were lost (the FIPS 199 impact levels from objective 1.1). Data that combines several sources can need a higher level than any one source.

US government schemes

  • Classified information (Executive Order 13526) has only three levels: Top Secret (disclosure could cause "exceptionally grave damage" to national security), Secret ("serious damage") and Confidential ("damage"). "No other terms shall be used", and when in doubt the lower level applies.
  • Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI, 32 CFR Part 2002) is unclassified information that a law, regulation or government-wide policy requires to be protected. Documents carry a "CUI" (or "CONTROLLED") banner, categories are listed in the National Archives' CUI Registry, older markings such as FOUO are no longer valid, and companies handling CUI for the government follow NIST SP 800-171.

Labeling, handling and retention

Objective 5.1Marking data so people and systems know its level, applying handling rules in every state, and keeping it only as long as needed.

A classification only helps if it is visible. NIST SP 800-53 has two controls for this:

  • MP-3 Media Marking: human-readable markings on media and documents showing "distribution limitations, handling caveats, and applicable security markings", such as a "Confidential" footer or a label on a backup drive.
  • AC-16 Security and Privacy Attributes: machine-readable labels attached to data "in storage, in process, and/or in transmission", which tools can act on automatically.

Handling rules then say what each level requires: who may access it, whether it may be emailed outside, printed or copied to USB, and how it must be stored. CSF 2.0 expects data protected in all three states: at rest (PR.DS-01), in transit (PR.DS-02) and in use (PR.DS-10).

Data loss prevention (DLP) tools enforce these rules. The US national security glossary describes DLP as identifying, monitoring and protecting data in use (on endpoints), in motion (on the network) and at rest (in storage), by inspecting content and context. At the retailer, DLP blocks emails to outside addresses that contain card numbers, and warns staff before they upload files labeled Confidential to personal cloud storage.

Retention limits how long data is kept. SP 800-53 SI-12 requires managing and keeping information according to laws, regulations and policy, and disposing of it at the end of the retention period. Keeping data "just in case" increases what a breach can expose; privacy laws such as GDPR require deleting personal data once it is no longer needed.

Masking, tokenization and de-identification

Objective 5.1Hiding or replacing sensitive values so that people and systems that do not need them never see them, and knowing the limits of each technique.

Many uses of data do not need the sensitive part. NIST's terms:

  • Masking: "systematically removing a field or replacing it with a value", for example "replacing a phone number with asterisks". Receipts and screens show only part of a card number.
  • Redaction: "the removal of information from a document or dataset for legal or security purposes", such as blacking out names before a document is released.
  • De-identification: "a general term for any process of removing the association between a set of identifying data and the data subject."
  • Pseudonymization: replacing an identifier with a pseudonym (such as a random customer number), so records can still be linked to each other but not directly to the person.
  • Anonymization: removing the association completely, so the data can no longer be linked to the person.
  • Tokenization (a payments term, not in NIST's glossary): the real value is swapped for a random token with no mathematical link to it, and the real value is kept in a secured token vault. The retailer's tills store tokens, not card numbers.

The payment card industry limits what may be kept or shown: its guidance treats the first six and last four digits of a card number as the baseline maximum for truncated numbers. NIST SP 800-53 (SI-12(2)) asks organizations to use "de-identification or synthetic data" instead of real personal data for testing, training and research, which is why the retailer's developers test with generated customers.

De-identified is not the same as anonymous. NIST warns that "some de-identified data can sometimes be re-identified", for example by combining a postcode, birth date and purchase history with another dataset. Its de-identification guide (SP 800-188, 2023) recommends formal review before release, and notes that not all tools that "merely mask personal information" are enough.

Sanitization and disposal

Objective 5.1Choosing between clear, purge and destroy before media is reused or leaves the organization, and knowing what changed in NIST's 2025 guidance.

Deleting a file usually leaves the data recoverable. Sanitization means "to render access to target data on the media infeasible for a given level of effort." NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 (September 2025, replacing the 2014 edition) defines three levels:

  1. ClearLogical techniques, such as overwriting or a factory reset, that protect against "simple, non-invasive data recovery techniques using the same interface that is available to the user." Fine for reuse inside the organization.
  2. PurgePhysical or logical techniques that make recovery "infeasible using state-of-the-art laboratory techniques", such as a drive's built-in secure erase or cryptographic erase. Needed before media leaves the organization's control.
  3. DestroyPurge-level protection that also leaves the media unusable: shredding, disintegrating, pulverizing, melting or incinerating.

Cryptographic erase is a purge technique: if data was always stored encrypted, destroying the encryption keys makes it unreadable in seconds. NIST cautions that it should not be relied on if the keys were backed up or escrowed somewhere else.

Old rules that no longer hold. SP 800-88 Rev. 2 says multi-pass overwrites (the obsolete "DoD 5220.22-M" style) are not needed for clear. Overwriting SSDs gives "very little confidentiality protection", because flash memory keeps spare copies the user cannot reach, so use purge or destroy. Degaussing works only on magnetic media, does nothing to SSDs, and "does not currently constitute a destroy sanitization technique", even if the drive stops working.

Rev. 2 shifts the focus to running an enterprise sanitization program, with records: a certificate of sanitization for each device showing its make, model and serial number, the method (clear, purge or destroy), the technique used and whether it was verified. SP 800-53 MP-6 requires sanitization before disposal, release or reuse, and the US Federal Trade Commission's Disposal Rule requires "reasonable measures" when disposing of consumer report information. The retailer's old tills are sent to a certified recycler that shreds the storage and returns certificates.

Symmetric and asymmetric encryption

Objective 5.1Comparing secret-key and public-key encryption, knowing the current standard algorithms and key sizes, and how the two are combined with certificates.

Encryption is "the process of changing plaintext into ciphertext using a cryptographic algorithm and key"; decryption reverses it. The algorithm can be public; the security depends on the key, "a parameter used in conjunction with a cryptographic algorithm that determines its operation."

Symmetric

Symmetric (secret-key) encryption uses "the same secret key" to encrypt and decrypt, and "the key must be kept secret if the data is to remain protected." It is fast, so it protects bulk data. The standard is AES (FIPS 197), with 128-, 192- or 256-bit keys and 128-bit blocks; modes such as GCM add integrity checking. Older ciphers are gone: DES was withdrawn in 2005, and NIST disallowed encryption with Triple DES after December 31, 2023 (decrypting old data is still allowed). The weakness is key distribution: both sides need the same secret key without anyone else seeing it.

Asymmetric

Asymmetric (public-key) cryptography "uses two separate keys": a public key anyone may have and a private key only the owner holds, where "given the public key, it is computationally infeasible to derive the private key." Data encrypted with the public key can be decrypted only with the private key, and signatures made with the private key can be verified with the public key (objective 1.1). Common algorithms are RSA, elliptic curve methods such as ECDSA and EdDSA, and Diffie-Hellman key agreement. Asymmetric operations are slow, and key sizes differ: RSA needs 2048 bits for 112-bit security (3072 for 128-bit), while the elliptic curve P-256 gives 128-bit security with a much shorter key.

Using both: TLS and certificates

Real systems combine them. When a browser connects to the online shop over TLS, asymmetric cryptography authenticates the server and agrees a fresh symmetric session key, and AES then encrypts the traffic. The server proves its identity with a certificate: a document "digitally signed by the private key of a certification authority", which binds the public key to the shop's domain. A public key infrastructure (PKI) is "a framework that is established to issue, maintain and revoke public key certificates." Key management covers keys from generation to destruction: storing them safely (for example in a hardware security module), rotating and revoking them.

Certificates are getting shorter-lived. Under rules adopted by the CA/Browser Forum in April 2025, public TLS certificates may be valid for at most 200 days from March 15, 2026, 100 days from March 2027 and 47 days from March 2029, down from 398. That makes automatic renewal essential. Checking a certificate's dates:

Check who issued a site's certificate and when it expires 
$ openssl s_client -connect shop.example:443 \
    -servername shop.example </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -issuer -dates
issuer=C = US, O = Example CA, CN = Example TLS CA
notBefore=Oct  1 00:00:00 2026 GMT
notAfter=Apr 19 23:59:59 2027 GMT   # 200 days

Hashing and passwords

Objective 5.1Knowing the properties of hash functions, which ones are retired, and why passwords are stored as salted, slow hashes rather than encrypted.

A hash function is "a function on bit strings in which the length of the output is fixed." Unlike encryption, it has no key and cannot be reversed. A cryptographic hash must resist three attacks: finding an input for a given output (preimage), finding a second input with the same output as a known one (second preimage), and finding any two inputs with the same output (collision). Current standards are SHA-2 (FIPS 180-4, for example SHA-256) and SHA-3 (FIPS 202). MD5 is "no longer acceptable where collision resistance is required", and NIST announced in December 2022 that SHA-1 must be phased out by December 31, 2030.

Passwords should never be stored in plain text or with reversible encryption. NIST SP 800-63B-4 says passwords "SHALL be salted and hashed using a suitable password hashing scheme", with a salt of at least 32 bits and a work factor "as high as practical." The salt is a random value stored with each hash, so identical passwords give different hashes and precomputed tables are useless; the deliberate slowness makes guessing millions of passwords expensive. A sketch in Python:

Storing a password as a salted, slow hash (Python) 
import hashlib, os

salt = os.urandom(16)            # 128-bit random salt
digest = hashlib.pbkdf2_hmac(
    "sha256",
    b"correct horse battery staple",
    salt,
    600_000)                     # iterations: slow on purpose
# store salt and digest; at login, recompute and compare

PBKDF2 is the NIST-approved scheme today (SP 800-132); NIST has said its revision will add a memory-hard function, which resists attackers using graphics cards.

Quantum-resistant cryptography

Objective 5.1Understanding which algorithms a future quantum computer would break, the new NIST standards that replace them, and the timeline for moving over.

A large enough quantum computer could break today's public-key algorithms (RSA, elliptic curve and Diffie-Hellman) using Shor's algorithm. Symmetric encryption and hashing are much less affected: NIST says symmetric algorithms are "significantly less vulnerable", and AES with 128-bit or longer keys remains fine. The urgent threat is harvest now, decrypt later: "adversaries collect encrypted data now with the goal of decrypting it once quantum technology matures." Data that must stay secret for ten years or more is already at risk. NIST notes that signatures "are not vulnerable to HNDL attacks", because a forged signature only matters once a quantum computer exists.

NIST published the first post-quantum standards in August 2024 and said "they can and should be put into use now":

  • FIPS 203, ML-KEM: key establishment (agreeing a shared key), replacing RSA and Diffie-Hellman key exchange. NIST recommends ML-KEM-768 as the default.
  • FIPS 204, ML-DSA: digital signatures.
  • FIPS 205, SLH-DSA: hash-based digital signatures, a backup built on different mathematics.

In March 2025 NIST selected HQC as a backup key-establishment algorithm, with its standard still in development. Browsers and servers are already adopting hybrid TLS key exchange that combines ML-KEM with a classical algorithm, so the connection stays safe if either one fails.

NIST's transition guide (IR 8547), finalized in October 2026, sets the deadline: "NIST will deprecate quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography after 2030 and disallow its use under NIST standards and guidelines after 2035." The guide also cites newer federal requirements for high-impact government systems to use post-quantum key establishment by the end of 2030 and signatures by the end of 2031.

Getting ready. CISA, NSA and NIST's "Quantum-Readiness" fact sheet (2023) recommends starting now: build a cryptographic inventory (where each algorithm is used and how long the data must stay protected), ask vendors for their post-quantum plans, and build crypto agility, which NIST defines as "the capabilities needed to replace and adapt cryptographic algorithms ... while preserving security and ongoing operations." CISA's 2026 list of product categories notes that many "PQC-capable" products so far protect only key exchange and are "not considered to be fully quantum resistant."

Data security in the AI workspace

Objective 5.1Preventing sensitive data from leaking into public AI services, and applying classification, masking and encryption to the data AI systems use and produce.

The CC outline highlights the risk of "data leakage when employees interact with public AI services." Text pasted into a consumer chatbot may be stored by the provider and, depending on its terms, used to train future models. NIST's Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1) lists data privacy among the main risks: "leakage and unauthorized use, disclosure, or de-anonymization" of personal or sensitive data, including models that memorize and repeat their training data. The US Federal Trade Commission has warned AI providers that "there is no AI exemption from the laws on the books" and that quietly changing terms to train on customer data may be unfair or deceptive.

The data handling controls on this page apply directly:

  • Policy: an acceptable use policy for AI (NIST AI 600-1 recommends one) saying which classifications may go into which tools: Public into anything, Confidential only into approved enterprise tools, Restricted into none.
  • Approved tools: enterprise AI services whose contracts exclude training on company data and keep data in agreed locations (objective 4.3).
  • DLP on browsers and endpoints to warn or block when card numbers, customer lists or keys are pasted into AI sites.
  • Masking before data reaches an AI model, and classification of outputs: joint CISA, NSA and FBI guidance (May 2025) says "the output of AI systems should be classified at the same level as the input data."
  • Encryption and secure deletion of training data and model files, as for any other Restricted data.

At the retailer, staff use an approved enterprise assistant; the browser's DLP blocks pasting card numbers or the customer export into any AI site, and the analytics team masks names and emails before customer data is used to fine-tune the chatbot.

Key takeaways

  • Roles: the data owner decides classification and access; the custodian carries it out; the steward keeps use consistent. Keep a data inventory (CSF ID.AM-07) and control every life cycle stage, including destruction.
  • Classification: each organization sets its own levels (for example public, internal, confidential, restricted). US classified levels: Top Secret, Secret, Confidential only. CUI is unclassified but protected, marked "CUI".
  • Labeling and handling: human-readable marks (MP-3) and machine-readable labels (AC-16); protect data at rest, in transit and in use; DLP enforces the rules; keep data only as long as required (SI-12).
  • Masking hides values; tokenization swaps them for random tokens; pseudonymization keeps records linkable; anonymization removes the link. De-identified data can sometimes be re-identified.
  • Sanitization (SP 800-88 Rev. 2, 2025): clear (logical, for reuse), purge (lab-proof, including cryptographic erase), destroy (unusable). No multi-pass overwrites; do not overwrite SSDs; degaussing is not destroy and does nothing to SSDs. Keep certificates.
  • Symmetric (AES): one shared key, fast, key distribution problem. Asymmetric (RSA, ECC): key pair, slow, solves distribution and enables signatures. TLS uses both; certificates and PKI bind keys to identities; certificate lifetimes are falling to 47 days by 2029.
  • Hashing is one-way with fixed-length output; use SHA-2 or SHA-3; MD5 is broken, SHA-1 retires by 2030. Store passwords salted and slowly hashed, never encrypted.
  • Post-quantum: quantum threatens public-key crypto, not AES. FIPS 203 ML-KEM, 204 ML-DSA, 205 SLH-DSA; HQC as backup. NIST: deprecate quantum-vulnerable algorithms after 2030, disallow after 2035. Start with a crypto inventory and crypto agility.
  • AI workspace: AI use policy by classification, approved enterprise tools, DLP on AI sites, masking before use, and classify AI outputs like their inputs.
