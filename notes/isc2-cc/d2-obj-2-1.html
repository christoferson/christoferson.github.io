Overview

Objective 2.1

Plan governance, risk, and compliance (GRC).

Domain 1 introduced the parts separately: risk management in 1.2, laws, frameworks and policies in 1.3, controls in 1.4. GRC is the practice of running them together, so that leadership sets direction once, risks are managed against it, and the organization can show it meets its obligations. This objective covers the purpose of GRC, why it matters, and the frameworks and tools used to plan and run it. Measuring how well it works is objective 2.4.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools. It has a board, a chief information security officer (CISO), a small compliance team and an internal auditor.

What GRC is for

Objective 2.1Seeing governance, risk management and compliance as one joined-up system instead of three separate jobs.

The term comes from OCEG, a non-profit that first used the acronym in 2002. It defines GRC as "the integrated collection of capabilities that enable an organization to achieve Principled Performance", which it explains as "the ability to reliably achieve objectives, address uncertainty, and act with integrity." The three parts line up with that sentence:

PartQuestion it answersRetailer example
GovernanceWhere are we going, who decides, and who is accountable? (achieve objectives)The board sets the risk appetite and approves the security strategy
Risk managementWhat could stop us, and what are we doing about it? (address uncertainty)The risk register ranks ransomware first and funds the response
ComplianceAre we meeting our legal, contractual and internal obligations, and can we prove it? (act with integrity)Yearly PCI DSS assessment; GDPR records; policy acknowledgments

The value is in the connections. Without them, the security team, the compliance team and the risk team each keep their own spreadsheets, test the same controls separately, and give leadership three different pictures. With GRC, one control (say, MFA) is recorded once, linked to the risk it reduces and to every rule that requires it, and tested once for all of them.

NIST does not define "GRC" (its glossary lists only the abbreviation), but it describes the same joining-up as risk governance: "the process by which risk management evaluation, decisions, and actions are connected to enterprise strategy and objectives" (SP 800-221).

Why GRC matters

Objective 2.1Understanding what weak governance costs: personal liability for leaders, fines, and programs that look good on paper but fail in practice.

Laws now make leaders answer for security

LawWhat it requires of leadership
NIS2 Directive, Article 20 (EU)Management bodies must "approve the cybersecurity risk-management measures", "oversee its implementation" and "can be held liable" for failures. Their members are "required to follow training". For essential entities, authorities can seek a temporary ban on a chief executive holding management roles
DORA, Article 5 (EU, financial sector)The management body must "bear the ultimate responsibility for managing the financial entity's ICT risk", approve the framework and allocate the budget
SEC rule, Regulation S-K Item 106 (US public companies)Each year, describe "the board of directors' oversight of risks from cybersecurity threats" and "management's role in assessing and managing" them

NIST's Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 says the same in its first governance outcome on roles: "Organizational leadership is responsible and accountable for cybersecurity risk" (GV.RR-01).

A program must work, not just exist

When US prosecutors decide how to treat a company that broke the law, the Department of Justice's guidance asks three questions about its compliance program:

  1. Is it well designed?Does it address the company's real risks with clear policies, training and controls?
  2. Is it applied earnestly and in good faith?Is it "adequately resourced and empowered to function effectively"?
  3. Does it work in practice?Prosecutors look for whether it is a "paper program" or one that is actually implemented, reviewed and improved.

The US Sentencing Guidelines (§8B2.1) list what an effective compliance and ethics program contains: written standards and procedures; a board that is knowledgeable and exercises "reasonable oversight"; care over who is given authority; training; monitoring, auditing and a way to report concerns anonymously "without fear of retaliation"; consistent discipline and incentives; and fixing the program after any failure.

Compliant does not mean secure. Passing an audit shows that required controls existed when they were checked; it does not prove the organization is safe. In 2025 the FTC finalized an order against the web host GoDaddy, which had advertised "award-winning security" but, the FTC said, lacked basics such as MFA and threat monitoring; it must now run a comprehensive security program checked by an independent assessor. The US government's FedRAMP program describes its 2025 overhaul as moving "beyond traditional compliance to focus on the security decisions that matter most."

Who does what

Objective 2.1Knowing which roles own risk, which advise and check, and which give independent assurance to the board.

The most widely used model for GRC roles is the Three Lines Model from the Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA). Its current version is a statement published on July 8, 2026, which replaced the 2020 paper; the IIA says "the model's core structure remains unchanged".

RoleIIA descriptionAt the retailer
Board (governing body)Responsible for oversight; "provides resources and delegates authority to management"The board and its audit and risk committee
First line"Management (first line) owns and manages risks and is responsible for the design and operation of processes and controls"Store operations, IT operations, the e-commerce team
Second lineProvides "specialized expertise, support, monitoring, and challenge" for risk management, compliance and controlThe CISO's security team, the compliance team, the privacy officer
Third lineInternal audit "delivers independent and objective assurance" on governance, risk management, compliance and controlThe internal auditor, reporting to the audit committee

The 2026 statement sets out five principles: the board sets purpose, risk appetite and expectations; oversight needs reliable, balanced information; the board and senior management create accountability by defining roles; distinct sources of assurance, including an independent one, give the board confidence; and advisory work complements assurance.

Only the third line is independent. The IIA's 2020 paper noted that second-line roles "are never fully independent from management, regardless of reporting lines": the security team works for management. Internal audit's independence typically comes from reporting functionally to the board. It "should not be interpreted as isolation", and the IIA suggests at least 12 months should pass before someone gives assurance on a process they used to run.

Outsourcing does not move accountability. The IIA statement has a section headed "Outsourcing Does Not Change Accountability", and DORA puts "ultimate responsibility" on the management body. If the retailer's payment provider is breached, the retailer still answers to its customers and regulators. Managing suppliers is part of governance (CSF 2.0 category GV.SC).

Planning a GRC program

Objective 2.1Laying out the steps of a GRC program, from listing obligations to reporting results back to leadership.

The Govern function of NIST CSF 2.0 gives a sensible order. Each step names the CSF category it matches:

  1. Understand the context (GV.OC)What the organization does, who depends on it, and its "legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements regarding cybersecurity" (GV.OC-03).
  2. Set the risk strategy (GV.RM)Objectives, risk appetite and tolerance, and how risks are calculated and prioritized (objective 1.2).
  3. Assign roles (GV.RR)Who owns each risk and control, with "adequate resources" (GV.RR-03), and security built into HR practices (GV.RR-04).
  4. Write and enforce policy (GV.PO)Policy that is "established, communicated, and enforced" (objective 1.3).
  5. Manage suppliers (GV.SC)Extend the same expectations to the companies the organization relies on.
  6. Oversee and adjust (GV.OV)Review results and use them "to inform, improve, and adjust the risk management strategy".

The first step usually produces an obligations register: a list of every requirement that applies, linked to the controls that meet it. One entry at the retailer might look like this:

Obligations register entry: OB-007
Source
PCI DSS v4.0.1 (contract with the retailer's bank)
Requirement
MFA for all access into the cardholder data environment
Controls that meet it
C-12 security keys for administrators; C-13 MFA on remote access
Also satisfies
Internal access control standard; CSF 2.0 PR.AA
Owner
Head of IT operations (first line)
Evidence
Monthly MFA coverage report; annual assessment by a qualified assessor
Status
Compliant; next assessment in June

The "also satisfies" line is the point of GRC: one control, tested once, provides evidence for several obligations.

Frameworks for running GRC

Objective 2.1Recognizing the frameworks organizations use to structure governance, internal control and compliance, beyond the security frameworks themselves.

The security frameworks from objective 1.3 (NIST CSF, ISO/IEC 27001, CIS Controls) say what to protect. These say how to govern and control the organization as a whole:

FrameworkWhat it covers
NIST CSF 2.0, Govern functionSix categories: Organizational Context (GV.OC), Risk Management Strategy (GV.RM), Roles, Responsibilities, and Authorities (GV.RR), Policy (GV.PO), Oversight (GV.OV), Cybersecurity Supply Chain Risk Management (GV.SC)
COSO Internal Control–Integrated Framework (2013)Internal control as "a process, effected by an entity's board of directors, management, and other personnel, designed to provide reasonable assurance" about operations, reporting and compliance. Five components: control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information and communication, monitoring activities
COSO Enterprise Risk Management (2017)Ties risk management to strategy and performance across the whole organization
COBIT 2019 (ISACA)Governance and management of enterprise IT, with 40 governance and management objectives
ISO 31000:2018General risk management guidelines for any organization
ISO 37301:2021Requirements for a compliance management system
NIST SP 800-221Governing technology risk within enterprise risk management. A Rev. 1 draft was released October 22, 2026, for comment until December 7, 2026

Governance and management are different jobs. Governance (the board) sets direction, makes the big decisions and oversees; management runs the organization within that direction. COBIT is built around this split, and the Three Lines Model reflects it: the board oversees, management owns the risks.

GRC tools

Objective 2.1Knowing the kinds of tools that keep a GRC program manageable: platforms, machine-readable control data and framework crosswalks.

Small organizations often start with spreadsheets. As obligations grow, they move to GRC platforms: software that holds the risk register, the obligations register, policies and their acknowledgments, control tests, audit evidence and reports in one place. NIST's RMF guide encourages organizations "to maximize the use of automated tools", noting that "many commercially available governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) tools can be employed to reduce or eliminate hard copy documentation."

Machine-readable controls: OSCAL

NIST's Open Security Controls Assessment Language (OSCAL) aims "to modernize and automate the processes of security and compliance" by writing control catalogs, system security plans and assessment results as data (XML, JSON or YAML) instead of documents. Version 1.2.0 (December 2025) added a control mapping model for showing how controls in different frameworks relate; the current version is 1.2.3 (August 2026). A catalog entry looks like this (simplified: real files also carry identifiers and metadata):

Part of an OSCAL control catalog (JSON) 
{
  "catalog": {
    "groups": [{
      "id": "ac",
      "title": "Access Control",
      "controls": [{
        "id": "ac-2",
        "title": "Account Management"
      }]
    }]
  }
}

Because the data is structured, a GRC tool can import a framework, show which controls the organization has implemented, and produce evidence automatically. The US government's FedRAMP cloud authorization program is moving in this direction: its "20x" approach, announced in 2025, relies on key security indicators that can be validated automatically and show "security posture in near real time".

Crosswalks between frameworks

Most organizations must satisfy several frameworks at once. A crosswalk (or mapping) shows where their requirements overlap, so one control can be counted for several. NIST publishes these through its Online Informative References (OLIR) program, which maps other documents to NIST frameworks such as CSF 2.0, SP 800-53 and the AI RMF, and its Cybersecurity and Privacy Reference Tool (CPRT), which offers NIST reference data in spreadsheet and JSON form.

Free starting points. NIST's CSF 2.0 Quick-Start Guides include guides for small businesses (SP 1300), creating Organizational Profiles (SP 1301), using Tiers (SP 1302), linking cybersecurity to enterprise risk management (SP 1303) and supply chain risk (SP 1305).

GRC and AI

Objective 2.1Bringing AI tools into the GRC program: knowing which ones exist, who owns them, and which rules apply.

The CC outline expects entry-level staff to support GRC planning for AI, including tracking AI-specific risk indicators on dashboards and reports (covered in objective 2.4). You cannot govern what you do not know about, so the first step is an AI inventory. The NIST AI RMF asks for "mechanisms ... to inventory AI systems" (GOVERN 1.6), an understanding of the "legal and regulatory requirements involving AI" (GOVERN 1.1), and executive leadership that "takes responsibility for decisions about risks associated with AI system development and deployment" (GOVERN 2.3).

  • Inventories in practice: US federal agencies must "inventory [their] AI use cases at least annually" and publish them, under OMB memo M-25-21 (April 2025), which replaced M-24-10.
  • Compliance programs: the DOJ's compliance guidance (updated September 2024) asks whether AI risk management is "integrated into broader enterprise risk management (ERM) strategies".
  • Internal control: COSO published guidance in 2026 on applying its internal control framework to generative AI, covering risks such as hallucinations, prompt injection and model drift.
  • Management systems: ISO/IEC 42001 lets an organization run, and certify, an AI management system alongside its ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS.

At the retailer, the chatbot and the recommendation engine each appear in the AI inventory with a business owner, the data they use, the obligations that apply (GDPR, state privacy laws, the AI use policy from objective 1.3) and their entries in the risk register.

Key takeaways

  • GRC joins governance (direction and accountability), risk management (uncertainty) and compliance (obligations) into one program. OCEG: capabilities to "reliably achieve objectives, address uncertainty, and act with integrity."
  • Leaders are accountable: NIS2 managers must approve, oversee and train and "can be held liable"; DORA's management body bears "ultimate responsibility"; US public companies must describe board oversight. CSF GV.RR-01 says the same.
  • A program must work in practice, not be a "paper program" (DOJ). Compliant is not the same as secure.
  • Three Lines Model (IIA, updated July 2026): the board oversees; the first line (management) owns risk; the second line (security, compliance) supports and challenges; the third line (internal audit) gives independent assurance. Outsourcing does not move accountability.
  • Plan with CSF 2.0 Govern: context and obligations, risk strategy, roles, policy, supply chain, oversight. An obligations register maps each requirement to controls, owners and evidence.
  • Governance frameworks: COSO internal control (five components), COSO ERM, COBIT, ISO 31000, ISO 37301. Governance directs and oversees; management runs.
  • Tools: GRC platforms, OSCAL machine-readable controls (v1.2.3), framework crosswalks (NIST OLIR, CPRT), CSF Quick-Start Guides.
  • For AI: keep an AI inventory, assign owners, map obligations, and track AI risk indicators.
