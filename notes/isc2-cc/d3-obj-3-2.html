Overview

Objective 3.2

Understand logical access controls. Sub-topics: Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP), Separation of Duties (SoD) and access control models.

Objective 3.1 covered how identities and their access are created, reviewed and removed. This objective is about the rules that decide what each identity may do once it is signed in. Objective 1.4 introduced least privilege and separation of duties as administrative controls; here they are explained in depth, alongside the access control models systems use to enforce them.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools, including a customer chatbot. A small head-office finance team pays suppliers; two IT administrators look after the systems.

What logical access control does

Objective 3.2Understanding access control as a decision about a subject, an object and an action, enforced by software rather than doors and locks.

NIST defines access control as "the process of granting or denying specific requests for obtaining and using information and related information processing services" (SP 800-53). Every decision has three parts:

  • a subject: the user, or a process "acting on behalf of users", making the request;
  • an object: the file, record, application, device or service being accessed;
  • an operation: what the subject wants to do, such as read, write, delete or execute.

Logical access controls are enforced by software: operating systems, databases, applications, cloud services and network devices. Physical access controls (badges, locks, guards) protect buildings and equipment and are covered with the other physical controls in objective 1.4. SP 800-53 draws the same line: control AC-3 Access Enforcement covers logical access inside systems, while physical access is a separate family.

A sound starting rule is deny by default: anything not explicitly allowed is refused. NIST's firewall guide defines it as blocking all traffic "that has not been expressly permitted", and SP 800-53 has a control called "Deny by Default – Allow by Exception" (SC-7(5)). The same idea applies to files and applications: new users and new systems start with no access and gain only what is granted.

Principle of least privilege

Objective 3.2Giving every user, process and system only the access it needs for its task, for no longer than it needs it.

NIST defines least privilege as restricting "the access privileges of users (or processes acting on behalf of users) to the minimum necessary to accomplish assigned tasks." Its design guide (SP 800-160 Vol. 1) applies the same principle to every part of a system, "human and machine elements" alike. The benefit is containment: if an account is phished, misused or makes a mistake, the damage is limited to what that account could do.

The related idea of need to know applies least privilege to information: even someone with the right level of access sees only the records their duties require. A store manager can see their own store's sales, not every store's payroll.

In practice

NIST SP 800-53 control AC-6 Least Privilege and its enhancements turn the principle into concrete rules:

  • Separate admin accounts: people with privileged access use a normal account for everyday work such as email (AC-6(2)). CISA's Performance Goals 2.0: "User accounts do not have administrator privileges."
  • Few privileged accounts: restrict them to named people or roles (AC-6(5)).
  • Ordinary users cannot run privileged functions, such as disabling security controls or creating accounts (AC-6(10)).
  • Log privileged actions: "Log the execution of privileged functions" (AC-6(9)).
  • Review privileges regularly and remove what is no longer needed (AC-6(7)); this is the cure for privilege creep from objective 3.1. CISA suggests quarterly reviews.

The modern refinement is just-in-time (JIT) access: admin rights are not held permanently but granted for a short time when needed, then removed automatically. CISA's Zero Trust Maturity Model describes the journey from "permanent access with periodic review" (the traditional starting point) to "just-in-time and just-enough access tailored to individual actions and individual resource needs" (the optimal stage). NIST calls the time dimension least persistence: resources are available "only for the time for which they are needed."

At the retailer, the two IT administrators read email from normal accounts. To change the identity provider's settings they request the administrator role through a privileged access tool, a colleague approves, and the role expires after two hours.

One of the most common real weaknesses. CISA and NSA's list of the ten most common misconfigurations found by their red and blue teams (AA23-278A, 2023) includes "improper separation of user/administrator privilege": excessive account privileges built up over time, service accounts with more rights than needed, and admin accounts used for everyday work. In one assessment, a phished user with admin rights let the testers take over the whole domain "in under a business day."

Separation of duties

Objective 3.2Splitting sensitive tasks so that no single person can carry out and hide misuse alone, and knowing the static, dynamic and two-person forms.

NIST describes separation of duty as "the principle that no user should be given enough privileges to misuse the system on their own" (SP 800-192), with the example that whoever approves paychecks should not also prepare them. SP 800-53 control AC-5 asks organizations to identify the duties that must be split and to set up access to enforce it, because it "helps to reduce the risk of malevolent activity without collusion." Its own example: the security staff who manage access control should not also manage the audit logs that would show their misuse.

Typical splits:

  • Finance: the person who enters a new supplier or changes bank details is not the one who approves payments to it.
  • Software: the developer who writes a change is not the person who approves and deploys it to production.
  • Security: administrators cannot edit or delete the logs of their own actions; a separate team reviews them.
  • Access: the person who requests access for themselves cannot approve it.

Static, dynamic and two-person

  • Static separation of duties makes roles mutually exclusive: nobody may hold both "create supplier" and "approve payment".
  • Dynamic separation of duties allows someone to hold both roles but not use them on the same transaction: a finance clerk can approve payments, just not ones they entered.
  • Dual authorization (also called two-person control) requires two authorized people to act together for a critical step. SP 800-53 notes it "reduces risk related to insider threats" (AC-3(2)) and suggests rotating who does it.

Collusion and small teams. Separation of duties stops one person acting alone; it does not stop two people working together, which is why it is often paired with job rotation and mandatory vacation (objective 1.4). Small organizations without enough people to split every duty use compensating controls instead, such as a manager reviewing a report of every payment and every log change.

At the retailer, the payment system enforces it: a supplier payment needs one person to enter it and a different person to approve it, and the system refuses if the same account tries both.

Discretionary and mandatory models

Objective 3.2Knowing who decides access in owner-controlled and centrally labelled models, and the confidentiality and integrity rules used with labels.

An access control model is the overall scheme a system uses to decide access. The first question to ask about any model is: who decides?

Discretionary access control (DAC)

DAC "leaves a certain amount of access control to the discretion of the object's owner" (NIST SP 800-192). The person who creates a file decides who else may read or change it. This is how most desktop operating systems and file shares work: NIST notes that access control lists and owner/group/other permissions "are by far the most common mechanism for implementing DAC policies." DAC is flexible and simple, but NIST calls it "inherently weak": anyone allowed to read a file can copy it and share the copy, and the owner never knows.

Mandatory access control (MAC)

Under MAC, "access control policy decisions are made by a central authority, not by the individual owner of an object" (SP 800-192). Information carries a security label (such as Confidential, Secret or Top Secret), users have a clearance, and the system compares them. Users, including the owner, cannot change labels or pass access on. MAC is used mainly in military and government systems handling classified data. Any model where users cannot change the rules, MAC included, is described as non-discretionary.

Two classic models describe the label rules:

  • Bell-LaPadula protects confidentiality: "no read up" (a Secret user cannot read Top Secret files) and "no write down" (they cannot copy Secret information into a Confidential file, where lower-cleared users would see it).
  • Biba protects integrity and reverses the rules: no read down (do not rely on less trustworthy data) and no write up (do not corrupt more trustworthy data).

MAC does not mean "the administrator decides". In MAC the decision follows labels and clearances set by policy; even the data owner cannot override them. In DAC the owner decides. And "write down" being forbidden surprises people: Bell-LaPadula is about stopping leaks to lower levels, not about who may create files.

Role, rule and attribute-based models

Objective 3.2Comparing access based on job roles, fixed rules and attributes, and recognizing when each fits.

Role-based access control (RBAC)

RBAC is "access control based on user roles" (SP 800-53): permissions are attached to roles, and people get them by being assigned the role. It matches how organizations think about jobs, and it makes joiners and movers simple: change the role, and the access follows (the role catalog from objective 3.1). The NIST model, standardized as ANSI/INCITS 359 in 2004 and revised in 2012, has four levels: core RBAC, role hierarchies (a senior role inherits a junior role's permissions), and static and dynamic separation of duties between roles. RBAC is the most widely used model in businesses.

Its known weakness is role explosion: NIST's ABAC guide notes that handling every special case "would require the creation of numerous roles that are ad hoc and limited in membership", until there are more roles than anyone can manage.

Rule-based access control

Here access follows fixed rules set by administrators that apply to everyone, such as a firewall allowing connections only from certain addresses, or a system allowing logins only during business hours. NIST notes that, unlike the other models, there is "no commonly understood definition or formally defined standard" for it. It is often combined with RBAC.

Attribute-based access control (ABAC)

NIST SP 800-162 describes ABAC as granting or denying requests based on attributes of the subject (department, clearance, employment status), the object (classification, owner, type), the environment ("the current time, day of the week, location of a user, or the current threat level") and policies written in terms of them. NIST's example: nurse practitioners in the cardiology department may view the records of heart patients. A new nurse needs no new rule; their attributes are enough. NIST also points out that access control lists and RBAC are in a sense special cases of ABAC, using only the attribute "identity" or "role".

Real systems mix models. The retailer's cloud services use roles for most access, add attribute conditions (company device, location) for sensitive data, and let staff share their own documents with colleagues, which is DAC.

How access rules are stored and enforced

Objective 3.2Telling access control lists from capability lists, and knowing the parts that make and enforce access decisions.

NIST describes an access control matrix as "a table in which each row represents a subject, each column represents an object", with the allowed actions in each cell. Systems store it in one of two ways:

  • An access control list (ACL) is attached to each object: "a list of entities, together with their access rights, that are authorized to have access to a resource." It is one column of the matrix, and makes "who can open this file?" easy to answer.
  • A capability list is attached to each subject: "a list attached to a subject ID specifying what accesses are allowed to the subject." It is one row, and makes "what can this user reach?" easy to answer.

Linux file permissions are a simple DAC mechanism with an owner, a group and everyone else:

Linux: viewing and changing file permissions 
$ ls -l q3-pricing.xlsx
-rw-r----- 1 jlee buyers 48213 Oct 2 q3-pricing.xlsx
# owner jlee: read and write (rw-)
# group buyers: read only (r--)
# everyone else: no access (---)

# the owner removes the group's read access
$ chmod g-r q3-pricing.xlsx

Attribute-based systems separate deciding from enforcing. In NIST SP 800-162, a policy decision point (PDP) "computes access decisions by evaluating the applicable digital policies", a policy enforcement point (PEP) enforces them in front of the resource, a policy information point supplies attributes, and a policy administration point is where policies are written. Zero trust uses the same pair: in NIST SP 800-207, "access is granted through a policy decision point (PDP) and corresponding policy enforcement point (PEP)", per session and "with the least privileges needed to complete the task." A simplified attribute-based rule might be written like this (the format is generic, not a specific product):

Example ABAC policy rule (generic JSON) 
{
  "rule": "finance-view-payroll",
  "effect": "permit",
  "action": "read",
  "subject": { "department": "finance", "employment": "active" },
  "object": { "type": "payroll-record" },
  "environment": { "device": "company-managed", "country": "US" }
}

Any request that matches no permit rule is denied by default.

Access control for bots and AI agents

Objective 3.2Applying least privilege and separation of duties to automated systems, so a bot or AI agent can do only its designated task.

The CC outline expects automated systems, including AI bots and service accounts, to work strictly within the organization's access control models, with only the permissions their tasks require. The NIST and CISA rules already cover them: AC-6 applies to "processes acting on behalf of users", and CISA's Performance Goals 2.0 say "all user accounts, system roles, and processes operate with the minimum privileges necessary to perform their tasks."

AI agents need particular care because their actions are driven by text that an attacker may control. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025) calls this risk Excessive Agency: the ability to take "damaging actions ... in response to unexpected, ambiguous or manipulated outputs from an LLM." It traces it to excessive functionality, excessive permissions and excessive autonomy. Its mitigations map directly onto this objective:

  • Least privilege: give the agent only the tools and permissions it needs; read-only where possible.
  • Act as the user: run actions in the requesting user's context, so the agent can never see more than that user could.
  • Separation of duties: require a human to approve high-impact actions instead of letting the agent both decide and act.
  • Complete mediation: check every request against policy in the backend system, not just in the agent's instructions.

The retailer's chatbot can look up order status and loyalty points only for the customer who is signed in, because it calls the order system with that customer's session. It can propose a refund, but a human agent approves anything over $20. A prompt asking it to "show all orders" fails at the order system, whatever the chatbot is persuaded to try.

Further reading

  • NSA and CISA Red and Blue Teams Share Top Ten Cybersecurity Misconfigurations (CISA and NSA, AA23-278A, October 2023): improper separation of user and admin privilege, with mitigations.
  • NIST SP 800-162, Guide to Attribute Based Access Control (ABAC) Definition and Considerations (NIST, 2014, updated 2019): ABAC concepts and the PDP and PEP.
  • NIST IR 7316, Assessment of Access Control Systems (NIST, 2006): DAC, MAC, RBAC, rule-based control, ACLs and capability lists in one place.
  • LLM06:2025 Excessive Agency (OWASP Gen AI Security Project, 2025): least privilege and human approval for AI agents.

Key takeaways

  • Access control decides whether a subject may perform an operation on an object. Logical controls are enforced by software; physical controls by locks and badges. Start from deny by default.
  • Least privilege: minimum access for users and processes, for the minimum time. Separate admin accounts, few privileged accounts, log privileged actions, review regularly, and prefer just-in-time admin access. Need to know applies it to information.
  • Separation of duties: no one can misuse a process alone (enter vs approve payments, develop vs deploy, admin vs audit). Static (exclusive roles), dynamic (not on the same transaction), dual authorization (two people together). It does not stop collusion.
  • DAC: the owner decides (file permissions, ACLs); flexible but weak. MAC: central labels and clearances decide; users cannot change them. Bell-LaPadula: no read up, no write down (confidentiality). Biba: the reverse (integrity).
  • RBAC: permissions follow job roles; most common in business; risk of role explosion. Rule-based: fixed administrator rules for everyone (not the same as role-based). ABAC: policies on subject, object and environment attributes; the most flexible.
  • An ACL is attached to the object (a column of the access matrix); a capability list to the subject (a row). The PDP decides, the PEP enforces.
  • Bots and AI agents get least privilege too: minimal tools and permissions, actions in the user's context, human approval for high-impact actions, and checks in the backend (OWASP Excessive Agency).
