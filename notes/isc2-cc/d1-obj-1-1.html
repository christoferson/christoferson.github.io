1.1: Understand cybersecurity concepts

The vocabulary every other objective is built on: the three properties security protects, how systems decide who someone is and what they may do, how actions are made undeniable, and how privacy differs from keeping secrets.

Overview

Objective 1.1

Understand cybersecurity concepts.

This objective is the foundation of the whole exam. Almost every later question — which control fits, what went wrong in an incident, which risk matters most — comes down to these ideas: confidentiality, integrity and availability (the "CIA triad"), the steps a system uses to let someone in and keep a record of what they did, the ability to prove who did something, and privacy.

The definitions on this page come from public US government standards, mainly the NIST glossary, FIPS 199 and NIST SP 800-53, so you can check them yourself. The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. These notes follow a mid-sized retail company with about 800 staff, 60 stores and an online shop. It takes card payments, keeps a customer loyalty database in the cloud, and has started using AI tools for product recommendations and customer service. Each concept is shown in that setting.

Confidentiality

Objective 1.1Keeping information away from people and systems not allowed to see it.

US federal law (FISMA) defines confidentiality as "preserving authorized restrictions on information access and disclosure, including means for protecting personal privacy and proprietary information". FIPS 199 describes its opposite plainly: "A loss of confidentiality is the unauthorized disclosure of information."

Notice the word authorized. Confidentiality does not mean nobody sees the data; it means only the right people do. The retailer's payroll team must read salary data, so confidentiality is about stopping everyone else.

How confidentiality is lost

  • Theft by attackers: breaking into the loyalty database and copying customer records.
  • Interception: reading data as it crosses a network that is not encrypted.
  • Insider misuse: an employee looking up a celebrity customer's purchase history out of curiosity.
  • Accidental exposure: a spreadsheet of customer emails sent to the wrong address, or a storage bucket left open to the internet.

How it is protected

  • Access control: only give access to the people whose job needs it (the AAA steps in this page's AAA section).
  • Encryption: scramble data so it is useless without the key. NIST's glossary (from CNSSI 4009) describes confidentiality as "prevention of compromise of data to unauthorized entities through techniques such as encryption".
  • Classification and handling rules: label data (public, internal, confidential) so staff know how to treat it (covered in objective 5.1).
  • Training: people who recognize phishing are less likely to hand over access.

Data in its three states

Data needs protecting wherever it is. NIST SP 800-53 separates information at rest ("when it is not in process or in transit and is located on system components", control SC-28) from information in transit across networks (control SC-8). The third state, in use (being processed in memory), is the hardest to protect, because data usually has to be decrypted to be worked on.

StateRetailer exampleTypical protection
At restCustomer records in the cloud database; laptops of the buying teamDisk and database encryption, access control
In transitCard details sent from a store till to the payment processorEncrypted connections (TLS), VPNs
In useA report being built from loyalty data on an analyst's screenAccess control, screen locks, limiting who can run the report

Confidentiality and AI

AI systems add new things to keep secret: the training data and the model itself. NIST's taxonomy of attacks on machine learning (NIST AI 100-2, March 2025) lists attacks that pull private information back out of a model, such as training data extraction and membership inference (working out whether a particular person's record was in the training data). If the retailer trains a recommendation model on purchase histories, those histories must be protected inside the model too.

NIST's AI documents tend to say "privacy" where traditional security says "confidentiality" — AI 100-2 uses "privacy compromise" for attacks that expose a model or its training data. Treat the two words as pointing at the same harm when a question is about AI.

Integrity

Objective 1.1Making sure information and systems are not changed or destroyed without permission.

FISMA defines integrity as "guarding against improper information modification or destruction, and includes ensuring information non-repudiation and authenticity". In FIPS 199's words, "a loss of integrity is the unauthorized modification or destruction of information."

Integrity covers two things. Data integrity means the information is accurate and complete: a price list says $19.99 because someone with authority set it, not because an attacker changed it. System integrity means the software runs as intended, not altered by malware.

How integrity is lost

  • Tampering: an attacker changes the bank account on a supplier's invoice so the payment goes to them.
  • Malware: ransomware encrypts files; a backdoor changes how a system behaves.
  • Errors: NIST SP 800-53 notes that unauthorized changes "can occur due to errors or malicious activity" — a bad data import that overwrites prices damages integrity as surely as an attacker does.

How it is protected

  • Hashing: a hash function produces a fixed-length "fingerprint" of a file. If even one character changes, the fingerprint changes, so comparing fingerprints detects alteration. A plain hash shows that something changed, not who changed it.
  • Digital signatures: prove a file came from a particular signer and has not changed since (non-repudiation).
  • Change control: changes to systems are reviewed and approved before they are made (SP 800-53 control CM-3).
  • Integrity verification tools that "detect unauthorized changes" to software and information (SP 800-53 control SI-7), and write-once storage for records that must never change.
  • Access control: most people who can read data should not be able to change it.
Fingerprint a file, then check it later 
# record the hash when you publish
sha256sum price-list.csv

# later: a change gives a new value
sha256sum price-list.csv

If the two outputs differ, the file has been changed. The retailer can publish the expected hash of a software update so stores can check the download was not tampered with on the way.

Integrity and AI

Poisoning is the AI-era integrity attack. NIST AI 100-2 explains that in a data poisoning attack "an adversary controls a subset of the training data by either inserting or modifying training samples", and in model poisoning "the adversary controls the model and its parameters". A competitor who floods the retailer's review system with fake reviews could skew what the recommendation model learns.

Poisoning is not only an integrity attack. NIST notes that "poisoning attacks are powerful and can cause availability or integrity violations" — a poisoned model can be made to fail outright, not just to give attacker-chosen answers.

Availability

Objective 1.1Making sure authorized users can get to information and systems when they need them.

FISMA defines availability as "ensuring timely and reliable access to and use of information". FIPS 199: "A loss of availability is the disruption of access to or use of information or an information system."

For the retailer, availability is money: if the payment system is down on a Saturday, the stores cannot sell. Availability is the property customers notice first.

How availability is lost

  • Denial of service: flooding a website with traffic so real customers cannot reach it. CISA's guidance groups distributed attacks into volumetric (using up bandwidth), protocol (exploiting weaknesses in network protocols) and application attacks (targeting a particular service).
  • Ransomware: files encrypted and unusable — an availability and integrity loss at once.
  • Failures and disasters: a failed disk, a power cut, a flood at the data center. NIST notes denial of service can also come from "a lack of planning" for capacity.

How it is protected

  • Backups, taken often enough to meet recovery goals (SP 800-53 control CP-9).
  • Redundancy: spare servers, network links and power, so one failure does not stop the business (objective 2.2 covers continuity and recovery).
  • Denial-of-service protection that absorbs or filters attack traffic (control SC-5).
  • Patching and monitoring, so weaknesses are fixed and problems spotted early.

AI services can be attacked the same way. NIST AI 100-2 describes an availability breakdown as interference that disrupts "the ability of other users or processes to obtain timely and reliable access to its services" — including energy-latency attacks that send inputs designed to make a model slow and expensive to run.

Rating how much each property matters

Not every system needs the same protection. FIPS 199 (still the current standard, from 2004) rates the potential impact of losing each property:

ImpactMeaning in FIPS 199Retailer example
LowA limited adverse effectThe staff canteen menu page goes offline
ModerateA serious adverse effectLoyalty points cannot be redeemed for a day
HighA severe or catastrophic adverse effectCustomer card data is stolen, or no store can take payments

Each property is rated separately: a public product catalog has low confidentiality (it is public anyway) but moderate integrity (wrong prices cause real losses). A system holding several kinds of information takes the high water mark — the highest rating of each property across everything it holds.

The three properties can pull against each other. NIST SP 800-82 notes that IT security programs usually put confidentiality, integrity and availability "in that order", while operational technology (factories, building controls) "prioritizes safety, followed by availability, integrity, and confidentiality". Read questions for what the system actually does: for a till or a payment system, availability may matter as much as secrecy.

Some study material uses "DAD" — disclosure, alteration, destruction — as the opposite of CIA. It is a memory aid, not a NIST term; the standards' own words are unauthorized disclosure, unauthorized modification or destruction, and disruption of access.

Authentication, authorization and accounting

Objective 1.1How a system confirms who someone is, decides what they may do, and records what they did.

Every access decision passes through the same steps. Together they are often called AAA, with identification as the step before them:

  1. IdentificationThe user claims an identity, for example by typing a username or tapping a badge. On its own this proves nothing.
  2. AuthenticationThe system verifies the claim. NIST: "verifying the identity of a user, process, or device, often as a prerequisite to allowing access to resources in a system."
  3. AuthorizationThe system decides what the verified identity may do: "the decision to permit or deny a subject access to system objects" (CNSSI 4009).
  4. AccountingThe system records what was done, so actions can be traced. NIST calls the goal accountability: actions "traced uniquely to the entity".

At the retailer: a store manager types their username (identification), enters a password and approves a prompt on their phone (authentication), is allowed to issue refunds up to $500 but not to change prices (authorization), and every refund they issue is logged with their name and the time (accounting).

Authentication is not authorization. Authentication answers "who are you?"; authorization answers "what are you allowed to do?". A user can be perfectly authenticated and still be refused. Also, "authorization" has a second meaning in US government risk management — a senior official's formal approval for a system to operate — which is not what this objective means.

Authentication factors

NIST SP 800-63B: "The three types of authentication factors are something you know, something you have, and something you are."

FactorExamplesWeakness
Something you knowPassword, PINCan be guessed, phished or reused across sites
Something you havePhone app, hardware security key, smart cardCan be lost or stolen
Something you areFingerprint, faceCannot be changed if copied; NIST does not accept a biometric as an authenticator on its own

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) "requires more than one distinct type of authentication factor". The word that matters is distinct: a password plus a security question is still two things you know, so it is not MFA. NIST requires biometrics to be used only "as part of multi-factor authentication with a physical authenticator".

Current NIST rules (SP 800-63-4)

NIST published the fourth version of its Digital Identity Guidelines, SP 800-63-4, on July 31, 2025, and withdrew SP 800-63-3 the next day. Older study material may quote the previous rules. The current ones include:

  • Three assurance levels (AAL1 to AAL3). AAL2 needs two distinct factors, and providers must offer at least one phishing-resistant option. AAL3 needs a phishing-resistant authenticator whose private key cannot be exported.
  • Passwords: at least 15 characters when a password is the only factor (8 when used inside MFA); no forced composition rules (such as "must include a symbol"); and no forced periodic changes — change a password only when there is evidence it was compromised.
  • Syncable authenticators (the cryptographic credentials many people know as passkeys, copied across a person's devices) are allowed up to AAL2, but not at AAL3, because syncing means the key can be exported.

Expect older guidance to still say "change passwords every 90 days" or "require upper case, numbers and symbols". NIST's current guidance says the opposite. When the exam asks for best practice, follow the current standard.

Phishing-resistant MFA

Not all MFA is equal. NIST defines phishing resistance as preventing secrets from reaching an impostor "without reliance on the vigilance of the claimant" — the protection works even if the user is fooled. Codes the user types in, such as one-time passwords or SMS codes, "SHALL NOT be considered phishing-resistant", because a fake website can simply ask for the code and pass it on.

CISA ranks MFA methods from strongest to weakest: phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO/WebAuthn security keys and platform authenticators, or smart cards); app push notifications with number matching; app one-time codes; push without number matching; and SMS or voice codes, which "should only be used as a last resort". The retailer should give head-office administrators hardware security keys rather than SMS codes.

Accounting: logs and audit trails

Accounting depends on good records. NIST SP 800-53 (control AU-3) says each audit record should show what happened, when, where, from what source, the outcome, and who was involved. Time stamps must be consistent (AU-8), and the logs themselves must be protected from tampering (AU-9) — a log an attacker can edit proves nothing.

One refund event, as a log record 
{
  "time": "2026-11-07T14:32:05Z",
  "event": "refund.issued",
  "user": "m.okafor",
  "store": "store-017",
  "amount": 120.00,
  "outcome": "success",
  "source_ip": "10.17.4.22"
}

The three A's come from network access systems: RADIUS (RFC 2865, with accounting in RFC 2866) and its successor Diameter (RFC 6733) were built as authentication, authorization and accounting protocols, for example for Wi-Fi and VPN log-ins.

Non-repudiation

Objective 1.1Making sure someone cannot credibly deny an action they took.

NIST SP 800-63-4 defines non-repudiation as "the capability to protect against an individual falsely denying having performed a particular transaction." If a supplier signs a contract electronically, non-repudiation means they cannot later claim they never agreed to it.

In US law, non-repudiation is part of integrity: the FISMA definition of integrity "includes ensuring information non-repudiation and authenticity".

How it is achieved: digital signatures

A digital signature uses a key pair. The signer creates the signature with their private key, which only they hold; anyone can check it with the matching public key. NIST's signature standard (FIPS 186-5) says a digital signature "provides a mechanism for verifying origin authentication, data integrity, and signatory non-repudiation". Because only the signer has the private key, a valid signature points to them.

Sign a purchase order, then verify it 
# the buyer signs with their private key
openssl dgst -sha256 -sign buyer-key.pem \
  -out order.sig order.json

# the supplier checks with the public key
openssl dgst -sha256 \
  -verify buyer-pub.pem \
  -signature order.sig order.json

If anyone changes order.json after signing, verification fails — so the signature protects integrity as well as proving origin. NIST stresses this works "when properly implemented with a supporting infrastructure and policy": if the private key is stolen or shared, the signature no longer proves who acted.

A MAC does not give non-repudiation. A message authentication code (such as an HMAC) uses a shared secret key. NIST SP 800-63-4 is explicit: "MACs provide authenticity and integrity protection but not non-repudiation protection." Both parties hold the same key, so either could have produced the code, and an outsider cannot tell which. Only asymmetric digital signatures give non-repudiation.

Logs and time stamps

Non-repudiation also relies on accountability: tamper-protected audit logs that record who did what and when, tied to individual (not shared) accounts. If ten store staff share one login, nobody can be held to a refund. NIST SP 800-53 control AU-10 names "digital signatures and digital message receipts" as ways to provide "irrefutable evidence" of actions.

NIST's newer signature standard FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), published August 2024, is designed to resist future quantum computers and gives the same non-repudiation property. Quantum-resistant cryptography is covered in objective 5.1.

Privacy

Objective 1.1Respecting people's rights over information about them, which goes beyond keeping it secret.

Privacy is about people and their control over information about themselves. One NIST definition calls it "the right of a party to maintain control over and confidentiality of information about itself". The NIST AI Risk Management Framework puts it more broadly: norms that "safeguard human autonomy, identity, and dignity".

The data at the center of privacy is personally identifiable information (PII): "information that can be used to distinguish or trace an individual's identity", alone or combined with other information. Names, addresses, card numbers and purchase histories in the retailer's loyalty database are all PII.

Privacy is not the same as confidentiality

The NIST Privacy Framework explains the difference with two overlapping circles. Cybersecurity risks come from a loss of confidentiality, integrity or availability. Privacy risks come from processing personal data at all. Where they overlap are "cybersecurity-related privacy events", such as a data breach. But privacy problems can also happen with no security failure: NIST's example is smart meters "operating as intended", whose data could still leave people "feeling surveilled".

The retailer could keep its loyalty data perfectly secure and still harm privacy — for example by using purchase histories to infer a customer is pregnant and sending baby-product offers before she has told anyone.

Encryption and access control protect confidentiality; they do not make data collection fair or lawful. A question about collecting more data than needed, using data for a new purpose, or keeping it too long is a privacy question, even if nothing was leaked.

Privacy principles

Most privacy rules descend from the Fair Information Practice Principles (FIPPs), which grew from a 1973 US government report. The US Office of Management and Budget (Circular A-130) lists nine: access and amendment, accountability, authority, minimization, quality and integrity, individual participation, purpose specification and use limitation, security, and transparency.

The European Union's GDPR sets out similar principles in Article 5: personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly and transparently; collected for specified purposes (purpose limitation); limited to what is needed (data minimisation); accurate; kept no longer than necessary (storage limitation); and protected (integrity and confidentiality). Article 5(2) adds accountability: the organization must be able to show it complies.

GDPR ruleWhat it means for the retailer
Rights of the person (Articles 15–22)Customers can ask to see their data, correct it, have it erased, move it, or object to its use, including automated profiling
Response timeRequests are answered "without undue delay and at the latest within one month"
Breach notification (Article 33)Notify the regulator "not later than 72 hours" after becoming aware of a breach, where feasible
Data protection by design and by default (Article 25)Build privacy into new systems from the start, and collect only what is needed by default

Laws and regulations in general are covered in objective 1.3.

Privacy and AI

AI raises privacy risks even without an attack. NIST's Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1) notes that models "may leak, generate, or correctly infer sensitive information about individuals", and lists "Data Privacy" — leakage and unauthorized use or disclosure of personal or sensitive data — as a key risk. A practical case: a customer-service employee pastes a customer's complaint, with their address and order history, into a public AI chatbot to draft a reply. The data has now left the company's control. Policy and approved tools prevent this, which is why the CC outline returns to it in Domain 5.

NIST's Privacy Framework version 1.0 (January 2020) is the current final version. A version 1.1 aligned with the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 and adding AI guidance was published as a draft in April 2025 but is not final.

Key takeaways

  • Confidentiality stops unauthorized disclosure (access control, encryption, classification); integrity stops unauthorized modification or destruction (hashing, signatures, change control); availability keeps systems usable (backups, redundancy, denial-of-service protection).
  • Protect data at rest, in transit and in use. FIPS 199 rates the impact of losing each property as low (limited), moderate (serious) or high (severe or catastrophic), and a system takes the high water mark. Priorities depend on context: operational technology puts safety and availability first.
  • AI adds model and training-data confidentiality, data and model poisoning (which can hit integrity or availability), and attacks on AI services' availability. NIST's AI documents often say "privacy" where security says "confidentiality".
  • Access runs identification (claim) → authentication (prove) → authorization (permit or deny) → accounting (record). Authentication is not authorization.
  • Factors: something you know, have and are. MFA needs distinct factor types; a biometric alone is not an authenticator. SP 800-63-4 (July 2025) replaced 800-63-3: no forced password expiry or composition rules, 15-character minimum for password-only, syncable authenticators allowed up to AAL2.
  • Typed codes (SMS, one-time passwords) are never phishing-resistant; FIDO/WebAuthn and smart cards are. CISA calls SMS a last resort.
  • Good accounting needs complete, time-stamped, tamper-protected logs tied to individual accounts.
  • Non-repudiation comes from asymmetric digital signatures (private key signs, public key verifies) plus accountable logs. A MAC or HMAC does not provide it, because the key is shared.
  • Privacy is broader than confidentiality: it can be harmed by lawful, secure processing. Know PII, the FIPPs, and GDPR's principles (purpose limitation, data minimisation, storage limitation, accountability), rights, one-month response and 72-hour breach notice.
