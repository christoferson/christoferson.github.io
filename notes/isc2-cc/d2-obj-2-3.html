2.3: Understand security awareness

Why people are part of every security program: how culture and leadership shape behaviour, how an awareness program is built and measured, and the attacks staff need to recognize, from phishing and pretexting to AI voice clones, plus the current rules for passwords.

Overview

Objective 2.3

Understand security awareness. Sub-topics: organizational culture and awareness concepts.

Most attacks need a person to do something: click a link, read out a code, approve a payment, hold a door open. Technical controls (objective 1.4) reduce how often that matters, but they never remove it. Security awareness is the part of governance that prepares people, and security culture is whether they actually behave that way when nobody is checking. This objective covers both, plus the attacks and password habits every employee should know. Measuring how well awareness works is objective 2.4.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools. Most store staff are part-time and share tills; head office has a small finance team that pays suppliers.

Why security culture matters

Objective 2.3Understanding that how people habitually behave decides whether security policies work, and why that makes culture a governance issue.

NIST has no glossary definition of "security culture", but its learning-program guide, SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (2024), sets the goal: "The culture of the organization should emphasize, reinforce, and drive its desired behaviors toward cybersecurity and privacy." A useful working definition: the shared habits and beliefs that decide what people do about security when it is inconvenient.

Signs of a healthy culture:

  • People report quickly, including their own mistakes, because they expect thanks rather than blame.
  • Security is part of the job, not a separate task: a store manager challenges an unknown "engineer" asking for the back office, without checking with anyone first.
  • Rules make sense to the people who follow them. Workarounds (passwords on sticky notes, shared logins) are a sign that a control is too hard to use.
  • Leaders follow the same rules as everyone else.

Usability matters. NIST research on security fatigue found that people faced with too many security decisions give up and take risks. Its advice: limit the decisions users must make, make the secure choice the easy one, and keep decisions consistent. Single sign-on and a company password manager do more for the retailer than another policy reminder.

NIST CSF 2.0 places culture under Govern, not Protect: leadership is accountable for cybersecurity risk and "fosters a culture that is risk-aware, ethical, and continually improving" (GV.RR-01). Training itself sits under Protect (PR.AT).

Security leadership

Objective 2.3Knowing what leaders do to build a security culture: setting the tone, funding it, taking part themselves and rewarding reporting.

Culture follows what leaders do, not what they say. CISA's Cyber Essentials guide for small organizations starts with the leader ("Yourself"), whose job is to "drive cybersecurity strategy, investment and culture", and then the staff, where the aim is to "develop a culture of awareness to encourage employees to make good choices online." SP 800-50 Rev. 1 expects senior leaders to champion the program by "leading by example and participating in their own CPLP training", and suggests a senior leadership committee that meets regularly with the people running it.

  • Setting the tone: leaders talk about security in all-staff messages, and use real events (a competitor's breach, a near miss) as examples. CSF 2.0 suggests sharing "leaders' expectations regarding a secure and ethical culture" in exactly this way.
  • Paying for it: time for training, tools that make the secure path easy, and staff to run the program. NIST's study of federal awareness programs (IR 8420, 2022) found many lacked "leadership backing, resources" and skilled staff.
  • Being trained too: senior leaders are prime targets, and both CSF 2.0 (PR.AT-02) and CISA's Performance Goals list them among the roles needing specific training.
  • Building it into HR: security in job descriptions, onboarding, and the consequences for breaking policy (CSF 2.0 GV.RR-04).
  • Rewarding reports: CISA's 2026 insider threat guidance asks leaders to foster "a culture of trust where employees feel empowered to report concerns".

Blame drives silence. If a person who clicks a phishing link is punished, the next person who clicks will hide it, and the attacker gets hours instead of minutes. SP 800-50 Rev. 1 says phishing exercises "should not be punitive, nor should any employee be called out for their response." CISA's phishing guidance stresses reporting even after a suspicious email has been opened.

At the retailer, the chief executive's monthly message thanks, by team, the stores that reported the most suspicious calls, and the CEO takes the same phishing training as store staff.

Building an awareness program

Objective 2.3Describing how an awareness and training program is planned, who it is for, how often it runs and how its results are measured.

SP 800-50 Rev. 1, "Building a Cybersecurity and Privacy Learning Program", replaced NIST's 2003 awareness guide and its 1998 training guide (SP 800-16), both withdrawn in September 2024. It defines awareness as "the ability of the user to recognize and avoid behaviors that could compromise cybersecurity and to act wisely and cautiously to increase cybersecurity", a definition about behaviour, not just knowledge. It describes a program in four phases:

  1. Plan and strategyLeadership support, goals, budget, and who the audiences are.
  2. Analysis and designFind what each group needs to know and do, and design the content.
  3. Development and implementationBuild or buy the content and deliver it through the year.
  4. Assessment and improvementMeasure results and feed them back into the next cycle.

Who it is for

SP 800-50 Rev. 1 groups learners into all users, holders of privileged accounts, and staff with significant security or privacy responsibilities. NIST SP 800-53 splits the controls the same way: AT-2 Literacy Training and Awareness for every user (including managers, senior executives and contractors) and AT-3 Role-Based Training for specific jobs, given "before authorizing access". AT-2 enhancements add practical exercises, recognizing insider threat indicators, and recognizing social engineering.

CISA's Cybersecurity Performance Goals 2.0 (December 2025) set a practical baseline: new staff are trained before they get access to computer systems, everyone gets training at least once a year, and specialized roles such as administrators, finance staff and senior leaders get extra training.

More than a yearly course

A yearly course alone does not change habits; CISA's small-business guidance says plainly that "once-a-year training isn't enough." Programs mix several kinds of activity:

  • Awareness activities all year: posters, logon-screen messages, short newsletters, events during Cybersecurity Awareness Month in October.
  • Awareness training: short courses for everyone on the basics.
  • Practical exercises: simulated phishing emails, tabletop exercises, role-play of a phone scam.
  • Role-based training: for example payment-fraud training for the finance team, secure coding for developers.

The old three-level model is gone. Older study material teaches the 2003 split into awareness (attention), training (skills) and education (deep knowledge). SP 800-50 Rev. 1 drops it because the terms "have different meanings and can lead to confusion", and uses one learning program instead. Expect both on exam questions: awareness reaches everyone, role-based training is for specific jobs.

Measuring it

SP 800-50 Rev. 1 suggests measuring behaviour, not course completion: phishing-simulation click and report rates, how many incidents staff report, changes such as MFA adoption, tests before and some months after training, and surveys of attitudes. NIST's Phish Scale (TN 2276, 2023) rates how hard a test email is to spot, from its visible cues and how well it fits the audience, so a rise in clicks can be explained by a harder test rather than worse staff. A summary of the retailer's quarterly simulation:

Phishing simulation, Q3: "delivery failed" text message
Audience
All store staff (620)
Phish Scale difficulty
Moderately difficult (some cues, strong fit: stores receive deliveries daily)
Clicked
11% (Q2: 14%)
Reported
38% (Q2: 22%)

Two-minute tip shown to everyone who clicked; no names shared with managers

The report rate matters more than the click rate: some people will always click, and an early report lets the security team block the link for everyone. Dashboards for these figures are objective 2.4.

Social engineering

Objective 2.3Recognizing the ways attackers manipulate people into giving up information, access or money, and the pressures they rely on.

NIST defines social engineering as "the act of deceiving an individual into revealing sensitive information, obtaining unauthorized access, or committing fraud by associating with the individual to gain confidence and trust" (SP 800-63-4). It works on people instead of technology, so firewalls and antivirus do not stop it. NIST SP 800-53 lists the common forms as "phishing, pretexting, impersonation, baiting, quid pro quo, thread-jacking, social media exploitation, and tailgating." In plain terms:

  • Pretexting: inventing a believable story to justify a request ("I'm from the bank's fraud team, I need to confirm your account").
  • Impersonation: pretending to be someone with authority or a reason to be there: IT support, a supplier, an auditor, a manager.
  • Baiting: leaving something tempting, such as a USB stick labelled "salaries", or offering a free download, so the victim infects their own machine.
  • Quid pro quo: offering something in exchange, such as "free tech support" in return for logging in to a fake page.
  • Thread-jacking: replying inside a real email conversation from a compromised account, so the message looks like part of a genuine exchange.
  • Tailgating (or piggybacking): following an authorized person through a secure door. Physical controls are in objective 1.4, and testing them is objective 5.5.
  • Social media exploitation and social mining: gathering details about staff and the organization from public profiles to make later attacks convincing.

Attackers rely on a small set of pressures: authority (the request seems to come from the boss or the police), urgency (act now or something bad happens), fear, helpfulness, curiosity, and familiarity (it looks like a colleague or a known supplier). A request that combines urgency with an unusual action, such as skipping a normal check, is the strongest warning sign.

At the retailer, a caller claiming to be from "head office IT" phones a store at closing time, says the tills will be locked overnight unless the manager reads out the code that just arrived by text. The code is the manager's MFA code; the caller is logging in to their account.

Verify through a different channel. CISA and the FBI give the same core advice: do not use the phone number, link or reply address in the message. Hang up and call back on a number you already know, or check with the person through another channel. Never read out an MFA code to anyone.

Phishing and its variants

Objective 2.3Telling apart the forms of phishing, spotting the warning signs, and knowing what to do with a suspicious message.

NIST, quoting RFC 4949, describes phishing as "a technique for attempting to acquire sensitive data, such as bank account numbers, through a fraudulent solicitation in email or on a web site, in which the perpetrator masquerades as a legitimate business or reputable person." CISA's joint phishing guidance (2023) names its two usual goals: stealing login credentials and getting malware installed. The variants differ in channel and target:

  • Spear phishing: aimed at specific people or groups, using details about them. The FBI describes it as targeting "specific people or groups by email".
  • Whaling: spear phishing aimed at senior executives.
  • Vishing: voice phishing, by phone or voicemail. Caller ID is easy to fake.
  • Smishing: phishing by text message; the same tricks now arrive through chat apps such as Teams, Slack or WhatsApp.
  • Business email compromise (BEC): using a compromised or look-alike email account to trick staff into sending money, typically a "change of bank details" from a supplier or an urgent transfer request from the "CEO".
  • Consent phishing: a link asks the user to grant a malicious app access to their account. An FBI warning from September 2026 notes it bypasses passwords and MFA and survives a password change; the app's access has to be revoked.

Phishing and spoofing are the most commonly reported crime type in the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) figures, but BEC costs far more: the IC3's 2025 report records about $3.05 billion in BEC losses, against about $216 million for phishing and spoofing.

Warning signs

CISA's current guidance for employers lists the red flags as "strange or unexpected requests, urgent language or suspicious links", and warns that fakes may include personal or company-specific details and may come from real contacts whose accounts were compromised. Other signs:

  • A sender address or link that is close to, but not quite, the real one (CISA's example: amazan.com). Hover over a link before clicking.
  • A request to change a process: new bank details, a payment outside the usual approval, a password or code read out over the phone.
  • Pressure to act quickly or keep it secret.
  • Unexpected attachments, or a login page reached from a link instead of a bookmark.

What to do

Do not click or reply. Report it through the agreed route, usually the mail client's report button or the security team; CISA asks employers to make sure staff know "to whom and how to report". If you already clicked or entered a password, report immediately: speed matters more than embarrassment. Organizations can also report phishing to CISA, and fraud to the FBI at ic3.gov; consumers can forward phishing emails to the Anti-Phishing Working Group at reportphishing@apwg.org and spam texts to 7726.

Awareness is backed by technical controls, so one click is not a disaster. CISA's guidance lists phishing-resistant MFA, email authentication (SPF, DKIM and DMARC set to reject), protective DNS and URL filtering, and not giving users administrator rights.

The retailer's finance team follows a fixed rule: any change of a supplier's bank details is confirmed by calling the supplier on the number already on file, and needs a second approver, however urgent the email sounds.

Password protection

Objective 2.3Knowing the current NIST rules for passwords, and the habits that keep them safe: length, uniqueness, password managers and MFA.

NIST's digital identity guidelines were updated in July 2025 as SP 800-63B-4, which replaced the 2017 version (withdrawn August 2025). Its password rules overturn several old habits. The main ones, with NIST's own requirement words (SHALL is mandatory, SHOULD is recommended):

Password rules from NIST SP 800-63B-4 (section 3.1.1.2) 
# Length
SHALL   at least 15 characters if the password is the only factor
SHALL   at least 8 characters if it is part of MFA
SHOULD  allow at least 64 characters, spaces and Unicode

# Things systems must no longer do
SHALL NOT  require mixes of character types (composition rules)
SHALL NOT  force periodic changes (only on evidence of compromise)
SHALL NOT  allow password hints visible before login
SHALL NOT  ask security questions such as "first pet's name"

# Things systems must do
SHALL   check new passwords against a blocklist of common,
        guessable and breached passwords, and say why one is rejected
SHALL   allow password managers and autofill
SHOULD  allow pasting a password
SHALL   store passwords salted and hashed, and limit failed attempts

CISA's advice for organizations is in line, and slightly stricter on length: passwords of at least 16 characters, or a passphrase of five to seven unrelated words, "used for only one account", a company-wide password manager, phishing-resistant MFA, and changing default passwords on devices.

Rotation and complexity rules are out. Forcing changes every 90 days and demanding "one capital, one number, one symbol" lead to predictable passwords such as Summer2026! with the number bumped each time. NIST now forbids both; change a password when there is evidence it has been compromised. Exam questions written to the old rules may still appear, so read the question carefully.

The best protection is not depending on passwords alone. Reused passwords are the main risk, because a breach at one site unlocks every account that shares the password. A password manager makes long, unique passwords practical, and MFA stops a stolen password being enough. NIST allows syncable authenticators (what most people call passkeys) at its middle assurance level, and CISA calls FIDO passkeys an acceptable alternative to hardware security keys. Authentication factors and phishing-resistant MFA are covered in objective 1.1.

The retailer's shared till logins are replaced by individual badge taps plus a short PIN, and head office staff get a company password manager and security keys for administrators.

How AI changes social engineering

Objective 2.3Recognizing how generative AI makes social engineering more convincing and more common, and updating training and procedures to match.

The CC outline expects awareness training to cover how AI escalates social engineering and phishing. An FBI public service announcement from December 2024 describes how criminals use generative AI at each step:

  • Text: fluent, personalized messages in any language, at scale. CISA notes that "in the era of artificial intelligence (AI) some emails will now have perfect grammar and spelling", so spelling mistakes are no longer a reliable sign.
  • Images: fake profile photos and forged ID documents.
  • Voice: cloned voices of relatives, managers or account holders, from a few seconds of public audio. In 2025 the FBI warned of AI-generated voice messages impersonating senior US officials.
  • Video: live video calls with a faked executive or official.

The IC3's 2025 report added an "AI related" label for the first time, covering about 22,000 complaints and roughly $893 million in losses, including BEC, romance, job-interview and voice-clone "relative in trouble" scams.

What changes in awareness training and procedures:

  • Teach the request, not the typos: focus on unusual requests, urgency, secrecy and channel-switching ("let's move to WhatsApp"), not spelling or a strange accent.
  • A familiar voice or face is not proof: verify payments and access requests through a known channel, even on a video call.
  • Agree code words: the FBI suggests a secret word or phrase with family; organizations can use the same idea for urgent requests from executives.
  • Limit what is public: recordings of executives' voices and detailed org charts make cloning and pretexting easier.
  • Make processes resistant: two-person approval for payments, and a rule that no one can override it by phone, protect even when someone is fooled.

The retailer adds a deepfake scenario to its finance team's yearly training: a video call from the "chief financial officer" asking for an urgent supplier payment. The right answer is to end the call and follow the normal approval process.

Further reading

  • NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1, Building a Cybersecurity and Privacy Learning Program (NIST, September 2024): culture, leadership, program phases and non-punitive phishing exercises.
  • Phishing Guidance: Stopping the Attack Cycle at Phase One (CISA, NSA, FBI and MS-ISAC, 2023): phishing techniques and the controls that back up awareness.
  • Criminals Use Generative Artificial Intelligence to Facilitate Financial Fraud (FBI IC3, December 2024): how AI text, images, voice and video are used in fraud, with tips.
  • NIST SP 800-63B-4, Authentication and Authenticator Management (NIST, July 2025): the current password rules in section 3.1.1.

Key takeaways

  • Security culture is how people behave when security is inconvenient. Healthy signs: quick reporting without blame, sensible rules, leaders following them. CSF 2.0 puts culture under Govern (GV.RR-01).
  • Leaders set the tone, fund the program, take the training themselves and reward reporting. Phishing tests must not be punitive (SP 800-50 Rev. 1).
  • An awareness program (SP 800-50 Rev. 1, 2024) is planned, designed, delivered and measured. AT-2 covers all users, AT-3 role-based training before access. CISA: train before access and at least yearly. The old awareness / training / education model is dropped.
  • Measure behaviour: report rates, click rates (in context, with the NIST Phish Scale), incidents reported, MFA adoption.
  • Social engineering manipulates people: pretexting, impersonation, baiting, quid pro quo, thread-jacking, tailgating, social media exploitation. Pressures: authority, urgency, fear, helpfulness, curiosity. Verify through a different channel.
  • Phishing variants: spear phishing, whaling, vishing, smishing, BEC, consent phishing. BEC causes the largest losses. Report quickly, even after clicking.
  • Passwords (SP 800-63B-4): at least 15 characters alone or 8 with MFA; no composition rules, no forced rotation, no hints or security questions; blocklist checks; allow password managers. Use unique passwords plus MFA or passkeys.
  • AI removes typos and adds cloned voices and deepfake video. Train on unusual requests, verify through known channels, use code words and two-person approval.
