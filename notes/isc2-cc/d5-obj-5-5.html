5.5: Understand security testing

Checking that defenses actually work: scanning for known weaknesses, testing applications before and after release, modeling threats at design time, letting authorized attackers try their best against systems, buildings and people, and applying the same methods to AI.

Overview

Objective 5.5

Understand security testing. Sub-topics: security readiness testing (blue, purple and red teaming), application testing (vulnerability scanning, static and dynamic analysis, threat modeling) and physical penetration testing (phishing, tailgating, impersonation).

Controls, monitoring and plans (objectives 1.4 to 5.4) only help if they work as intended. Security testing checks them, from automated weekly scans to months-long simulated attacks. Exercises that test the incident response plan by discussion are covered in objective 5.3; this objective tests the defenses themselves.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools, including a customer chatbot. A small in-house development team builds the online shop; an external firm is hired each year for penetration testing.

Kinds of security testing

Objective 5.5Telling vulnerability assessments, penetration tests and red team exercises apart by their goal, depth and realism.

  • A vulnerability assessment is a "systematic examination of an information system or product to determine the adequacy of security measures, identify security deficiencies" (NIST). It finds and lists weaknesses, mostly with automated scanners, but does not exploit them.
  • Penetration testing is "a test methodology in which assessors, typically working under specific constraints, attempt to circumvent or defeat the security features of a system." NIST SP 800-53 notes it "goes beyond automated vulnerability scanning": testers actually exploit weaknesses to show what an attacker could reach (control CA-8).
  • A red team exercise is "a simulated adversarial attempt to compromise organizational missions or business processes", testing not just systems but people, processes and the defenders' ability to detect and respond (CA-8(2)). It is broader, longer and more realistic than a penetration test.

Tests can be overt, "performed with the knowledge and consent of the organization's IT staff", or covert, "without the knowledge of the organization's IT staff, but with full knowledge and permission of upper management" (NIST SP 800-115). Overt testing "is less expensive, carries less risk ... and is more frequently used", while covert testing "provides a better indication of the everyday security". CISA's Performance Goals 2.0 expect organizations to obtain independent validation of their controls through outside penetration tests, bug bounties and exercises (goal 2.C), and SP 800-53 suggests an independent testing team (CA-8(1)).

No authorization, no test. Every test needs written permission from someone with authority over the systems or premises, and agreed rules of engagement. Without them, "testing" is just an attack. Scans and exploits can also disrupt fragile systems, which is why scope and timing are agreed in advance.

Red, blue, purple and white teams

Objective 5.5Knowing the role of each team colour in security readiness testing and how they work together.

  • Red team: "a group of people authorized and organized to emulate a potential adversary's attack or exploitation capabilities against an enterprise's security posture", to improve security "by demonstrating the impacts of successful attacks and by demonstrating what works for the defenders" (CNSSI 4009).
  • Blue team: "the group responsible for defending an enterprise's use of information systems by maintaining its security posture against a group of mock attackers" — in practice, the SOC and IT staff who detect and respond.
  • White team: the referees and "a small group of people who have prior knowledge of unannounced Red Team activities", who make sure "the scope of testing does not exceed a pre-defined threshold" and help set the rules of engagement. CISA calls them trusted agents.
  • Purple team: not a NIST term, but widely used for red and blue working together openly: the red team runs an attack technique, the blue team checks whether it was detected, and both tune detections on the spot.

CISA has published lessons from its own red team assessments, and they are sobering. At one organization with a "mature cyber posture", defenders "did not detect the red team's activity throughout the assessment" (AA23-059A, 2023). In a long, no-notice exercise against a federal agency, the team got in through an unpatched server and a phishing payload, and CISA found that "a 'known-bad' detection approach hampered detection" of new techniques (AA24-193A, 2024). In a third, spear phishing failed entirely, blocked by endpoint protection, but the team got in through a web shell left on an unpatched internet-facing server (AA24-326A, 2024). The point of red teaming is exactly this: finding the gaps defenders assume are covered.

Running a penetration test

Objective 5.5Following the phases of a penetration test and knowing what the rules of engagement must settle before it starts.

NIST SP 800-115 describes a four-stage methodology:

  1. PlanningRules are agreed, "management approval is finalized and documented, and testing goals are set. ... No actual testing occurs in this phase."
  2. DiscoveryGathering information and scanning, then analysing which vulnerabilities might be exploitable.
  3. Attack"Verifying previously identified potential vulnerabilities by attempting to exploit them"; new access often leads back to more discovery.
  4. ReportingFindings, evidence, impact and recommended fixes. NIST notes reporting "occurs simultaneously with the other three phases".

The rules of engagement (ROE) are "detailed guidelines and constraints regarding the execution of information security testing", agreed before the test and giving the team "authority to conduct defined activities without the need for additional permissions." SP 800-115's template covers scope (which systems, sites and people are in and out), schedule and hours, contacts including the incident response team, how to handle an accidental outage or a real incident found during testing, how test data is handled, reporting, and signatures. The retailer's ROE for its annual test excludes the payment provider's systems (not the retailer's to authorize), bans denial-of-service attacks in peak season, and names the CISO as the 24-hour contact.

Vulnerability scanning

Objective 5.5Running and interpreting vulnerability scans, and using CVE, CVSS, EPSS and KEV to decide what to fix first.

A vulnerability is a "weakness in an information system, system security procedures, internal controls, or implementation that could be exploited or triggered by a threat source." Vulnerability scanning is "a technique used to identify hosts/host attributes and associated vulnerabilities." SP 800-53 RA-5 requires scanning at a set frequency and when new vulnerabilities are announced, analysing the results, and remediating "legitimate vulnerabilities ... in accordance with an organizational assessment of risk." Points to know:

  • Credentialed vs uncredentialed: a scanner with administrator credentials can read installed versions and settings directly and finds much more than one probing from outside (RA-5(5)).
  • Internal vs external: "internal scanning usually uncovers more vulnerabilities than external scanning", but both viewpoints matter.
  • False positives: scanners "can have a high false positive error rate"; NIST says assessors should "not simply accept the risk levels assigned by vulnerability scanners."
  • Vulnerability disclosure: RA-5(11) asks for "a public reporting channel for receiving reports of vulnerabilities", so outside researchers can report what they find.

Naming and scoring

Publicly known vulnerabilities get a CVE identifier. The CVE program had a funding scare in April 2025, after which a separate CVE Foundation was created; CISA described plans for a "Quality Era" for the program in September 2026. NIST's National Vulnerability Database (NVD) adds analysis to CVEs, but after CVE submissions grew 263% from 2020 to 2025, NIST announced in April 2026 that it would prioritize CVEs in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, software used by the federal government and critical software, so many CVEs no longer get NVD analysis.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS, version 4.0 since November 2023, maintained by FIRST) rates severity from 0 to 10: None 0.0, Low 0.1-3.9, Medium 4.0-6.9, High 7.0-8.9, Critical 9.0-10.0. It has Base, Threat (which replaced the old Temporal group), Environmental and Supplemental metric groups; Supplemental metrics "do not modify the final score." Severity is not the same as likelihood, so two more inputs help: FIRST's EPSS "estimates the probability that a published CVE will be exploited in the wild in the next 30 days", and CISA's KEV catalog lists vulnerabilities known to be exploited (objective 5.2).

Reading a CVSS v4.0 base vector 
CVSS:4.0/AV:N/AC:L/AT:N/PR:N/UI:N/VC:H/VI:H/VA:H/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N
# AV:N   attack vector: network (reachable remotely)
# AC:L   attack complexity: low
# AT:N   no special attack requirements
# PR:N   no privileges needed
# UI:N   no user interaction
# VC/VI/VA:H  high impact on the vulnerable system's
#             confidentiality, integrity, availability
# SC/SI/SA:N  no impact on other systems
# base score 9.3: Critical

The retailer patches by combining all three: a Critical CVSS score on an internet-facing server that is also in KEV is fixed the same day; a High score on an internal test server with a low EPSS waits for the monthly cycle.

Static and dynamic application testing

Objective 5.5Comparing testing that reads code with testing that attacks the running application, and knowing the common web application weaknesses to test for.

NIST's microservices guidance (SP 800-204C) describes the main application security testing tools:

  • Static analysis (SAST): tools that "analyze an application's source, bytecode, or binary code for security vulnerabilities" without running it, early in development; they can also find hard-coded passwords and secrets.
  • Dynamic analysis (DAST): tools that "analyze applications in their dynamic, running state" and "simulate attacks against an application", like an outside attacker (black box).
  • Interactive testing (IAST): combines the two, with an agent inside the running application.
  • Software composition analysis (SCA): identifies "open-source and third-party components in use in an application, their known security vulnerabilities", the basis for a software bill of materials.
  • Fuzzing: a fuzzer "performs automatic active testing; fuzzers create huge numbers of inputs during testing", looking for the rare input that crashes or confuses the program.

NIST's minimum standards for developer testing (NISTIR 8397, 2021) list eleven techniques: threat modeling, automated testing, static code scanning, tools that look for hard-coded secrets, built-in checks and protections, black-box tests, code-based structural tests (with at least 80% statement coverage), historical "regression" tests, fuzzing, web application scanners, and checking included libraries and services. NIST's Secure Software Development Framework (SP 800-218) asks teams to "review and/or analyze human-readable code" (PW.7) and "test executable code" (PW.8) before release.

The OWASP Top 10 lists the most critical web application risks, and its 2025 edition is the current one: A01 Broken Access Control, A02 Security Misconfiguration, A03 Software Supply Chain Failures, A04 Cryptographic Failures, A05 Injection, A06 Insecure Design, A07 Authentication Failures, A08 Software or Data Integrity Failures, A09 Security Logging and Alerting Failures, and A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions.

Check which edition a question uses. Much material still teaches the OWASP Top 10:2021, where Injection was third and server-side request forgery had its own category; in 2025 SSRF was folded into Broken Access Control and two categories are new. The well-known free scanner ZAP is no longer an OWASP project: it left OWASP in 2023 and is now "ZAP by Checkmarx".

The retailer's online shop pipeline runs SAST and SCA on every code change, blocks merges with critical findings, runs a DAST scan against the staging site nightly, and is penetration-tested once a year and after major changes.

Threat modeling

Objective 5.5Identifying design-level threats early by modeling the system, its trust boundaries and what could go wrong, using methods such as STRIDE.

NIST defines threat modeling as "a form of risk assessment that models aspects of the attack and defense sides of a logical entity, such as a piece of data, an application, a host, a system, or an environment." NISTIR 8397 recommends "using threat modeling early in order to identify design-level security issues and to focus verification", and repeating it "multiple times during development." The Threat Modeling Manifesto (2020) frames it as four questions:

  1. What are we working on?Draw the system: usually a data flow diagram with processes, data stores, external entities, data flows and trust boundaries.
  2. What can go wrong?Look for threats where data crosses trust boundaries.
  3. What are we going to do about it?Mitigate, eliminate, transfer or accept each threat.
  4. Did we do a good enough job?Review the model and check the mitigations were built and tested.

STRIDE is the most common checklist for the second question, each threat violating a security property:

  • Spoofing (authentication)
  • Tampering (integrity)
  • Repudiation (non-repudiation)
  • Information disclosure (confidentiality)
  • Denial of service (availability)
  • Elevation of privilege (authorization)

Other methods include attack trees, which "start with the attacker's goal and decompose the alternative or required paths to reach it", and PASTA, which links business objectives to technical threats. When the retailer designed its "click and collect" feature, the model showed the store pickup screen crossing a trust boundary from the store network to the order system; STRIDE turned up spoofing (someone collecting another customer's order) and the team added a one-time pickup code.

Physical and social engineering tests

Objective 5.5Testing buildings and people with authorized phishing, tailgating and impersonation attempts, safely and without blaming individuals.

Attackers do not stay on the network, so tests should not either. SP 800-53 CA-8(3) asks for "announced" or "unannounced attempts to bypass or circumvent controls associated with physical access points to the facility," and red team exercises may include "social engineering-based attacks ... via email, telephone, shoulder surfing, or personal conversations" (CA-8(2)). Common tests:

  • Phishing simulations, measured by click and report rates (objective 2.3).
  • Tailgating (piggybacking): following an authorized person through a secure door. NIST describes it as "following authorized individuals into facilities with controlled access", which access control vestibules are designed to prevent (PE-3(8)).
  • Impersonation: posing as a delivery driver, contractor, auditor or IT support. SP 800-115 gives examples such as "posing as a help desk agent and calling to request a user's passwords", or calling the help desk "posing as a user and asking for a password to be reset."

Safety and legality come first. SP 800-115 says security guards should know "how to verify the validity of tester activity, such as via a point of contact", and that physical testers carry a signed form "to show to law enforcement or onsite security personnel in the event that they are questioned" (often called a "get out of jail" letter). The person who signs must actually have authority over the premises: in a widely reported 2019 case in Iowa, two contracted testers were arrested at a county courthouse because the official who authorized the test did not control that building. Results should "improve the security of the organization and not ... single out individuals."

The retailer's annual test included three unannounced store visits by a tester posing as a till engineer. At one store a manager asked for ID and called the help desk number on file, as trained; at another the tester reached the back office by following a delivery. The fix was a sign-in rule for all visitors and a reminder in the next awareness campaign, with no names reported.

Testing AI systems

Objective 5.5Extending red teaming and application testing to AI systems and to defenses that rely on AI.

The CC outline expects red, blue and purple teaming and application testing to validate AI-integrated defenses. NIST defines AI red teaming as "a structured testing effort, often adopting adversarial methods, to find flaws and vulnerabilities in an AI system, including unforeseen or undesirable system behaviors" (AI 100-2). Its Generative AI Profile recommends red teaming against "abuse to facilitate attacks on other systems (e.g., malicious code generation, enhanced phishing content), GAI attacks (e.g., prompt injection), ML attacks (e.g., adversarial examples/prompts, data poisoning, membership inference, model extraction ...)" (MS-2.7-007), checking that "fine-tuning does not compromise safety and security controls", and regularly verifying "that security measures remain effective." CISA describes AI red teaming as "a subset of AI Testing, Evaluation, Verification and Validation" and urges testers to build on existing software testing practice rather than "reinventing the wheel."

  • AI applications: test for prompt injection (direct, and indirect through documents or web pages the AI reads), data leakage, and actions the AI should not be able to take (objective 3.2). OWASP published an AI Testing Guide in November 2025 covering the application, model, infrastructure and data layers; MITRE ATLAS catalogues attack techniques.
  • AI-integrated defenses: purple-team the AI features of SIEM, EDR and email filters, checking that they detect a technique and that an attacker cannot easily evade or poison them.

Before launching the chatbot, the retailer's testers tried to make it reveal other customers' orders, follow instructions hidden in a product review, and offer unauthorized refunds. Two prompt injection paths worked in testing and were fixed by checking permissions in the order system rather than in the prompt. The tests now run again after every model or prompt change (objective 5.4).

Further reading

  • NIST SP 800-115, Technical Guide to Information Security Testing and Assessment (NIST, 2008): test techniques, phases and rules of engagement.
  • AA24-326A: Insights from CISA Red Team Assessment of a US Critical Infrastructure Sector Organization (CISA, November 2024).
  • NISTIR 8397, Guidelines on Minimum Standards for Developer Verification of Software (NIST, 2021): threat modeling, static and dynamic analysis, fuzzing.
  • AI Red Teaming: Applying Software TEVV for AI Evaluations (CISA blog, November 2024).

Key takeaways

  • Vulnerability assessment finds and lists weaknesses; penetration testing exploits them to show impact; red teaming simulates a real adversary against people, processes and defenders. Overt (IT staff know) vs covert (only management knows). Always written authorization and rules of engagement.
  • Red attacks, blue defends, white referees and knows about unannounced tests, purple (industry term) is red and blue working together to improve detections. CISA red teams often go undetected even in mature organizations.
  • Pen test phases (SP 800-115): planning (no testing), discovery, attack, reporting (throughout). ROE set scope, schedule, contacts, incident handling, data handling and signatures.
  • Scanning (RA-5): credentialed finds more; internal and external views; verify false positives. CVE names vulnerabilities; CVSS v4.0 scores severity (Critical 9.0-10.0); EPSS estimates exploitation probability; KEV lists exploited ones. NVD now analyses only priority CVEs.
  • SAST reads code without running it; DAST attacks the running app; IAST combines them; SCA checks third-party components; fuzzing floods inputs. OWASP Top 10:2025 starts with Broken Access Control and Security Misconfiguration.
  • Threat modeling: early and repeated; four questions; data flow diagrams with trust boundaries; STRIDE (spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege); attack trees; PASTA.
  • Physical and social engineering tests (CA-8(3)): phishing, tailgating, impersonation. Signed authorization from someone with real authority, guards able to verify testers, no blaming individuals.
  • AI: red-team AI systems for prompt injection, data leakage, poisoning and misuse; purple-team AI-based defenses; retest after every model or prompt change.
