2.2: Understand redundancy

How an organization keeps going when something breaks: removing single points of failure, planning for business continuity, working out how long each process can be down, recovering systems after a disaster, and keeping backups that still work after an attack.

Overview

Objective 2.2

Understand redundancy. Sub-topics: Business Continuity (BC) and Disaster Recovery (DR).

Availability, the third part of the CIA triad (objective 1.1), depends on planning for failure before it happens. Business continuity keeps the organization's important work going during and after a disruption. Disaster recovery brings the IT systems back after a major failure. Both depend on redundancy: spare capacity, copies and alternatives, so that one failure does not stop everything. In the new outline, BC and DR sit under governance because leadership decides what must keep running and pays for it. Handling the security incident itself is objective 5.3.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments at the tills, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools, including a customer chatbot. Its busiest weeks are before the winter holidays.

Redundancy and single points of failure

Objective 2.2Finding the parts whose failure would stop the business, and knowing the common ways to add a spare.

A single point of failure is any one component (a server, a network link, a power feed, a supplier, even one person who knows the system) whose failure stops a whole service. Redundancy removes it by adding a second one. NIST's contingency planning guide, SP 800-34 Rev. 1, asks the plan coordinator to identify single points of failure, and lists redundancy among the preventive controls: RAID disk arrays, automatic failover, uninterruptible power supplies, server clustering and mirrored systems.

The goal is resilience, which SP 800-34 describes as the ability to adapt quickly to known or unknown changes and recover from them. Common redundancy techniques:

  • Disks: RAID spreads or copies data across several disks so the array survives a failed disk. RAID 1 mirrors two disks; RAID 5 and 6 add parity so one or two disks can fail.
  • Servers: a cluster or a load-balanced group of servers. In active-active, all copies serve traffic at once; in active-passive, a standby takes over when the active one fails. NIST SP 800-53 defines failover as "the capability to switch over automatically (typically without human intervention or warning) to a redundant or standby system upon the failure or abnormal termination of the previously active system."
  • Capacity: N+1 means one more unit than the load needs (five air conditioners where four would do); 2N means a complete duplicate.
  • Power: SP 800-34 notes that dual power supplies protect only against a failed power supply, a UPS gives roughly 30 to 60 minutes, and a generator covers long outages as long as it has fuel.
  • Network: two internet links from different providers. SP 800-53 control CP-8 asks for telecommunications without single points of failure, from separate providers.
  • Locations: copies of systems and data in a second site or cloud region, far enough away not to be hit by the same flood or power cut.

At the retailer, each store has two internet links (fibre plus a mobile backup) so card payments keep working, and the online shop runs on several servers in two cloud locations.

Redundancy is not a backup. A mirror or a replica copies every change straight away, including mistakes, deletions and ransomware encryption. SP 800-34 warns that high availability "cannot be a replacement for a solid backup strategy, as a corruption of data on a system may propagate through an HA system." RAID keeps a disk failure from stopping the system; it does not protect the data.

Business continuity planning

Objective 2.2Telling apart the plans an organization keeps for disruption, and knowing what a business continuity plan covers.

NIST does not define "business continuity" in its glossary. The US emergency agency FEMA describes continuity as the ability to keep providing critical services and essential functions before, during and after a disruption. SP 800-34 defines the business continuity plan (BCP) as a documented, predetermined set of instructions for keeping mission and business processes going during and after a significant disruption.

SP 800-34 draws a line between the two kinds of planning: continuity planning is about the business processes (taking orders, paying staff), and contingency planning is about the information systems behind them. It lists several related plans, which work together:

  • Business continuity plan (BCP): keeps business processes running, for one department or the whole organization.
  • Continuity of operations (COOP) plan: the US government version, restoring essential functions at an alternate site within 12 hours, for up to 30 days. Private companies usually write BCPs instead.
  • Disaster recovery plan (DRP): moves IT operations to an alternate site after a major disruption, such as losing a building (below).
  • Information system contingency plan (ISCP): recovers one system, wherever it is running.
  • Cyber incident response plan: handles attacks; it may be an appendix to the BCP (objective 5.3).
  • Crisis communications plan: who says what to staff, customers, regulators and the media. Only named people speak publicly.
  • Occupant emergency plan (OEP): getting people safely out of, or sheltered in, a building during a fire or other emergency.

People first. In any disaster the safety of people comes before systems and data. The occupant emergency plan is carried out first and separately, before anyone starts recovering servers.

What goes into a BCP

Ready.gov, the US government's preparedness site, gives small businesses a plain-language order: prepare, set objectives, identify and rank the risks and their impacts, choose continuity strategies, assign teams and tasks, and test the plan. A workable BCP usually includes:

  • Scope and priorities: which processes matter most, taken from the business impact analysis (next section).
  • Who decides: who can declare a disruption and start the plan, and an order of succession (who takes over if that person is unavailable). Delegations of authority say in writing what each stand-in may approve, such as emergency spending.
  • Strategies for each critical process: a workaround, a manual fallback, a second supplier or an alternate site. Remote work is now a standard strategy; FEMA's 2024 federal requirements expect telework to be part of continuity plans.
  • Contacts and communications: call lists for staff, suppliers and authorities, and a way to reach them when email is down. CISA advises keeping incident response and communications plans in hard copy and offline.
  • Suppliers: the BCP depends on suppliers too. NIST CSF 2.0 expects suppliers to be included in incident planning, response and recovery (GV.SC-08).

The retailer's BCP says that if the payment network fails, stores switch to the card terminals' offline mode for small amounts and post a sign for cash only above that limit. Each store manager has a printed call tree.

Business impact analysis

Objective 2.2Working out which processes matter most and how long each one can be down or lose data, so recovery targets can be set.

A business impact analysis (BIA) studies each business function and what a disruption would do to it. SP 800-34 gives three steps: identify the processes and how critical their recovery is, identify the resources each one needs (systems, people, suppliers, data), and set recovery priorities. Ready.gov lists the impacts to weigh: lost or delayed sales, extra costs such as overtime or outsourcing, regulatory fines, contract penalties, customers who leave, and delayed new business. Timing matters: an outage in the holiday season costs a retailer far more than one in February.

The BIA produces three numbers for each process, all defined in SP 800-34:

  • Maximum tolerable downtime (MTD): how long the process can be disrupted before the harm becomes serious. This is a business decision made by the owner.
  • Recovery time objective (RTO): how long the systems behind it can take to recover before the business is hurt. It must be shorter than the MTD, because after the systems are back, staff still need time to check them and catch up on work.
  • Recovery point objective (RPO): "the point in time to which data must be recovered after an outage", in other words how much recent data the business can afford to lose. An RPO of 15 minutes means backups or replication at least every 15 minutes.

RTO is about time; RPO is about data. SP 800-34 says RPO is not part of the MTD: a system can be back in an hour (good RTO) with yesterday's data (poor RPO). If the RTO cannot fit inside the MTD, the gap is recorded and a plan made to close it. Many study guides add work recovery time (WRT) and write MTD = RTO + WRT; this is a common industry convention, not a NIST term.

Shorter targets cost more. An RPO of near zero needs continuous replication to a second site; an RPO of a day can be met with a nightly backup. The BIA is how leadership decides where the money goes.

Disaster recovery and alternate sites

Objective 2.2Knowing what a disaster recovery plan does, the phases of recovery, and how the kinds of alternate site trade cost against speed.

SP 800-34 defines a disaster recovery plan (DRP) as "a written plan for recovering one or more information systems at an alternate facility in response to a major hardware or software failure or destruction of facilities." It is tied to a site: it moves operations from a building or data centre that cannot be used. Once operations have moved, the plan for each system (its ISCP) brings that system back.

SP 800-34 describes recovery in three phases:

  1. Activation and notificationAssess the damage, decide to start the plan, and tell the recovery team.
  2. RecoveryRestore systems in priority order at the alternate site, from backups or replicas.
  3. ReconstitutionTest and confirm the recovered systems, return to normal operations (at the original site or a new one), then formally close the plan.

Moving back to the primary site after recovery is often called failback. It is planned and tested as carefully as the failover, because it is a second disruption.

Alternate sites

SP 800-34 compares three types (its Table 3-3):

SiteWhat is already thereCost and setup time
Cold siteSpace, power and cooling, but no computer equipment or telecommunicationsLowest cost; longest setup
Warm sitePartly equipped, with some hardware and some or all telecommunicationsMedium cost; medium setup
Hot site"A fully operational offsite data processing facility" with hardware, software and telecommunications readyMedium to high cost; short setup

SP 800-34 also mentions mobile sites (self-contained, transportable units, often available within 24 hours) and mirrored sites (fully redundant facilities with real-time mirroring, the most expensive, giving close to 100 percent availability). Sites can be owned by the organization, rented from a commercial provider, or shared through a reciprocal agreement: "an agreement that allows two organizations to back up each other." Reciprocal agreements only work if both have similar systems and spare capacity, and a commercial site may be oversubscribed if a regional disaster hits many of its customers at once.

SP 800-34 matches the strategy to how important the system's availability is: a cold site and tape backups for low-impact systems, replication with a cold or warm site for moderate ones, and mirrored systems with a hot site for high-impact ones. Today the "site" is often a second cloud region. NIST SP 800-53 allows failover to a cloud service provider as an alternate processing site (CP-7).

The retailer's online shop and loyalty database replicate to a second cloud region that can take over within the 4-hour RTO (in effect a warm site). The head-office file servers have a cold option: rebuild from backups on rented cloud servers within two days.

Far enough apart. SP 800-53 asks for an alternate site separated from the primary so the same event is unlikely to hit both (CP-6, CP-7). It also notes that distance matters less against a hostile attacker, who can reach both sites over the network. That is why backups also need to be offline (next section).

Backups that survive an attack

Objective 2.2Choosing backup types and storage so that a clean, tested copy is still available after a ransomware attack.

SP 800-34 defines a backup as "a copy of files and programs made to facilitate recovery if necessary." It describes three types:

  • Full: everything, every time. Slowest to make, simplest to restore.
  • Incremental: only what changed since the last backup of any type. Fastest to make; a restore needs the last full backup plus every incremental since.
  • Differential: everything changed since the last full backup. Grows each day; a restore needs only the last full plus the latest differential.

Ransomware changed the rules

Attackers now look for backups and delete or encrypt them before launching ransomware. CISA's #StopRansomware Guide says to "maintain offline, encrypted backups of critical data, and regularly test the availability and integrity of backups in a disaster recovery scenario." Practical points from CISA and NIST:

  • Keep a copy offline or otherwise out of reach of the normal network and admin accounts. Cloud sync tools that overwrite files are not backups.
  • Encrypt backups and protect the keys, since a stolen backup is a data breach.
  • Immutable storage, which cannot be changed or deleted for a set period, helps, though CISA advises using it with caution and not relying on it alone.
  • Test restores: CISA's Cybersecurity Performance Goals ask for backup and recovery to be tested at least once a year.
  • Check before restoring: NIST CSF 2.0 expects "the integrity of backups and other restoration assets is verified before using them" (RC.RP-03), so the malware is not restored with the data.
  • Back up how to rebuild, not just data: CISA suggests "golden images" of standard systems and keeping infrastructure-as-code templates offline.

The 3-2-1 rule. Keep 3 copies of the data, on 2 different types of media, with 1 copy offsite. It comes from a 2012 US-CERT paper and is a rule of thumb, not a standard. Many organizations now add that one copy should be offline or immutable.

Testing and maintaining the plans

Objective 2.2Knowing the ways BC and DR plans are tested, from talking through them to a real switch-over, and keeping them current.

An untested plan is a guess. NIST SP 800-53 (CP-4) lists the main ways to test, from least to most disruptive:

  1. Checklist reviewEach team reads its part of the plan and checks it is complete and current.
  2. Walk-through or tabletop exerciseThe team sits together and talks through a scenario ("ransomware has encrypted the warehouse system: what now?"). No systems are touched.
  3. SimulationA realistic scenario is acted out, with people carrying out their procedures, but production keeps running.
  4. Parallel testSystems are brought up at the alternate site and checked while the primary site keeps serving the business.
  5. Full interruption testThe primary site is actually shut down and the business runs on the alternate site. This is the most realistic test and carries the most risk.

NIST's exercise guide, SP 800-84, ends each exercise with a hotwash (an immediate discussion of what went well and what did not) and an after-action report with improvements. SP 800-34 links the depth of testing to how important the system is: a tabletop for low-impact systems, a functional exercise for moderate ones, and a full-scale exercise including failover to the alternate site for high-impact ones.

Plans go out of date as staff, suppliers and systems change. SP 800-34's templates suggest testing and reviewing plans at least once a year and whenever there is a significant change, with contact lists checked more often. Training matters too: everyone with a role in the plan needs to know it.

The retailer runs a ransomware tabletop each spring with the CISO, store operations and the communications lead, and fails the online shop over to the second region every autumn, before the holiday season.

Watch out for old sources. Many study materials still cite FEMA's Federal Continuity Directives 1 and 2, which were rescinded and replaced in August 2024, and NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2, which was withdrawn in April 2025 and replaced by Rev. 3. SP 800-34 Rev. 1 (2010) itself is still current.

Continuity for AI services

Objective 2.2Including AI tools in continuity planning: what to back up, what can fail, and how the business carries on without them.

The CC outline asks entry-level staff to think about AI in BC and DR, such as backing up what an AI service depends on and treating a model's gradual loss of accuracy as a continuity risk. AI tools bring new single points of failure:

  • A provider outage: most organizations use AI models through one cloud provider. NIST's Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1) calls foundation models potential "single points of failure" and suggests documenting dependence on third parties and identifying fallbacks.
  • Losing what makes it work: the prompts, settings, connections and the documents the chatbot looks up are configuration and data like any other, and need backing up. AI 600-1 also suggests redundancy for data, including model weights where the organization runs its own model.
  • Quiet failure: a model can keep running while its answers get worse over time (model drift), for example because customer behaviour has changed since it was built. Monitoring the quality of answers is part of keeping the service available in a useful form.

The usual answer is a fallback. AI 600-1 suggests testing rollover and fallback processes, which "may include manual processing", and CISA's Performance Goals ask organizations to plan for running essential functions in a degraded state. If the retailer's chatbot is unavailable, the website shows the customer service phone number and the contact form, and the BIA records that the chatbot can be down for days without serious harm. The recommendation engine falls back to showing best-sellers.

Further reading

  • NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1, Contingency Planning Guide for Federal Information Systems (NIST, 2010): the source for the plan types, the BIA, MTD, RTO and RPO, and alternate sites.
  • Business Continuity Plan (Ready.gov, updated March 2026): a plain-language guide for businesses, with a separate page on the business impact analysis.
  • #StopRansomware Guide (CISA, 2023): practical advice on offline backups, recovery plans and exercises.

Key takeaways

  • Redundancy removes single points of failure: RAID, clusters (active-active or active-passive), N+1 capacity, UPS and generators, two network providers, a second site or region. It is not a backup, because it copies corruption and encryption too.
  • Business continuity keeps business processes going; disaster recovery restores IT after a major failure, usually at an alternate site. Related plans: COOP (government), ISCP (one system), incident response, crisis communications, occupant emergency (people first).
  • A BCP sets priorities, who decides and who stands in (succession, delegation), strategies including manual fallbacks and remote work, offline contact lists, and supplier roles.
  • The BIA finds the critical processes and sets MTD (business limit), RTO (system recovery time, shorter than MTD) and RPO (acceptable data loss). Timing such as peak season changes the impact.
  • Recovery phases (SP 800-34): activation and notification, recovery, reconstitution. Sites: cold (cheap, slow), warm, hot (fast, costly), plus mobile, mirrored and reciprocal.
  • Backups: full, incremental (fast backup, longer restore), differential. Keep an offline, encrypted copy, test restores at least yearly, and verify integrity before restoring. 3-2-1 is a rule of thumb.
  • Test from checklist and tabletop up to parallel and full interruption, then hotwash and after-action report. Review plans at least yearly and after significant changes.
  • For AI: back up configurations and data behind AI services, watch for model drift, and keep a tested manual fallback.
