4.2: Understand network security architecture

How networks are designed so that one breach does not become a total loss: dividing them into zones, VLANs and micro-segments, layering several independent defenses, and the zero trust approach of verifying every request instead of trusting anything inside the perimeter.

Overview

Objective 4.2

Understand network security architecture. Sub-topics: network segmentation (firewall zones, VLANs, micro-segmentation), defense in depth and zero trust (ZT).

Objective 4.1 covered the parts: protocols, firewalls, VPNs, wireless and devices. This objective is about arranging them. A flat network, where every device can reach every other, lets an attacker who gets in anywhere move everywhere. Good architecture assumes something will be breached and limits how far the damage spreads.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools. Each store has tills, back-office PCs, staff and guest Wi-Fi, cameras and a refrigeration controller. A small data team is building a demand-forecasting model.

Why networks are segmented

Objective 4.2Understanding segmentation as a way to contain breaches, limit lateral movement and reduce what needs the strongest protection.

Network segmentation divides a network into separate parts, with controlled paths between them. NIST's glossary has no entry for the term, but CISA's Performance Goals 2.0 (goal 3.I) explain the purpose: segmentation can "contain security breaches by restricting traffic to separate segments", limit an attacker's access to sensitive data, and "even shut down segments of the network during an intrusion." Goal 3.S adds that networks should be split "according to trust boundaries and platform types (e.g., IT, IoT, OT, mobile, guests)" and "only permit required communications between segments."

Three benefits follow:

  • Less lateral movement: an attacker who compromises a guest phone or a camera cannot reach the tills.
  • Smaller blast radius: ransomware on one segment does not encrypt everything.
  • Smaller scope: payment-card rules apply in full to the systems that handle card data and anything connected to them. Isolating the card systems shrinks what must meet those rules; the PCI Security Standards Council published updated scoping and segmentation guidance in 2024.

NIST SP 800-53 control SC-7 Boundary Protection asks organizations to monitor and control traffic at external and key internal boundaries, to put publicly reachable systems in "subnetworks" separate from internal networks, and to use separate subnets for different security domains and critical functions.

Firewall zones and the DMZ

Objective 4.2Placing systems in zones by how much they are trusted, and knowing what goes in a DMZ and how the strongest forms of separation work.

A zone is a group of systems with the same trust level and the same rules, separated from other zones by a firewall or router. The classic layout has three:

  • Untrusted: the Internet.
  • DMZ (demilitarized zone), also called a screened subnet: in NIST's words a "'neutral zone' between an organization's private network and the Internet." NIST's firewall guide notes: "It is common to put public-facing servers, such as web and email servers, on the DMZ." Traffic between the DMZ and the inside still passes through the firewall.
  • Internal (trusted): staff systems and internal servers, often split further into zones of their own.

The retailer's zones and the main rule for each:

Network zones: store and head office
Internet
Untrusted; inbound traffic reaches only the DMZ
DMZ
Web front end of the online shop; may call the order API inside, nothing else
Card zone
Tills and payment terminals; may reach only the payment provider
Staff zone
Back-office PCs and staff Wi-Fi; reach head-office apps over the VPN
OT/IoT zone
Cameras and refrigeration controller; no Internet; vendor access through a jump server with MFA
Guest zone
Guest Wi-Fi; Internet only, blocked from every other zone
Management zone
Firewall and switch admin interfaces; reachable only from admin workstations

Stronger separation

  • Bastion host: "a special purpose computer on a network specifically designed and configured to withstand attacks", such as a hardened server in the DMZ. A jump server is a bastion that administrators must log in to before reaching a protected zone, so all admin access passes one monitored point.
  • Data diode (unidirectional gateway): "a network appliance or device that allows data to travel only in one direction." Unlike a firewall, NIST notes the hardware is physically incapable of sending data back, which makes it popular for sending data out of control systems.
  • Air gap: systems that "are not connected physically" and where any transfer is manual. Air gaps are strong but hard to keep: one USB stick or a forgotten maintenance modem breaks them.

VLANs

Objective 4.2Using VLANs to separate traffic on shared switches, and knowing why a VLAN needs a firewall between segments to be a real security boundary.

NIST describes a VLAN (virtual local area network) as "a broadcast domain that is partitioned and isolated within a network at the data link layer." One physical switch can carry several VLANs, and one VLAN can span several switches. The IEEE 802.1Q standard adds a tag with a 12-bit VLAN ID to each Ethernet frame, allowing roughly 4,000 VLANs. Large data centres and clouds use overlays such as VXLAN, with 24-bit IDs and about 16 million segments.

VLANs are cheap logical separation: the retailer's store switch carries tills, staff, cameras and guests on different VLANs over the same cabling. A simplified, generic switch configuration:

Store switch VLANs (generic, simplified) 
vlan 10  name TILLS
vlan 20  name STAFF
vlan 30  name CAMERAS-OT
vlan 40  name GUEST
vlan 99  name MGMT

port 1-8    access vlan 10   # tills
port 9-16   access vlan 20   # back-office PCs
port 17-20  access vlan 30   # cameras, fridge controller
port 24     trunk vlans 10,20,30,40,99  # to the firewall
# traffic between VLANs goes through the firewall rules

The key point is the last line: a VLAN separates traffic, but the rules between VLANs are what make it a security boundary. If the router simply forwards everything between VLANs, the network is still effectively flat.

VLANs have limits. Misconfigured switches can be tricked into letting traffic jump between VLANs (VLAN hopping), so unused ports should be disabled and trunk ports locked down. For control systems, NIST SP 800-82 says VLANs can be a cost-effective option, but organizations "should consider utilizing physically separate switches for segmenting high-criticality devices, such as those that support safety systems."

Micro-segmentation

Objective 4.2Distinguishing broad zones from fine-grained segments around individual workloads, and knowing how micro-segmentation is planned and enforced.

CISA's guide "Microsegmentation in Zero Trust, Part One" (July 2025) defines it as "a networking control that limits connections to a zone or segment", applied at a much finer level than traditional segmentation. It contrasts two scales:

  • Macrosegmentation: broad divisions such as "isolation of development and production environments, demilitarized zones (DMZ) ..., user segmentation and application segmentation." Simpler to manage, less precise.
  • Micro-segmentation: rules around individual workloads, so that servers "can only communicate with other servers needed to perform their business function", instead of every server in the data centre sharing one segment.

CISA lists the benefits as "reducing the attack surface, limiting lateral movement and increasing visibility", and notes that micro-segmentation "does not replace defense-in-depth". It can be enforced in many places: network devices and next-generation firewalls, host firewalls or agents on each endpoint, container platforms, hypervisors and cloud security groups. Software-defined networking makes this practical, because rules follow the workload rather than its physical port.

CISA's planning steps: identify the resources to protect, map their dependencies (who really needs to talk to whom), write the segmentation policies, and deploy them, first in a monitoring or "permissive mode" so that legitimate traffic is not blocked by mistake. At the retailer, the loyalty database accepts connections only from the order service and the backup system, even though other servers sit in the same cloud network.

Defense in depth

Objective 4.2Layering several different, independent controls so that one failure does not lead to a breach.

The US national security glossary (CNSSI 4009, used by NIST) defines defense in depth as an "information security strategy integrating people, technology, and operations capabilities to establish variable barriers across multiple layers and missions of the organization." NIST's engineering guide (SP 800-160 Vol. 1, principle E.9) puts the idea in three lines: "Loss control should not rely on a single defensive element", multiple barriers should be placed along the path an attack would take, and "the successive barriers should be diverse in nature and include technical, operational, and organizational barriers." SP 800-53 control PL-8(1) asks for controls in different layers that work "in a coordinated and mutually reinforcing manner."

For a phishing attack on a store manager at the retailer, the layers might be:

  1. Policy and peopleAwareness training and a reporting button (objective 2.3).
  2. Email filteringSPF, DKIM and DMARC, and link scanning, stop most phishing before delivery.
  3. IdentityPhishing-resistant MFA, so a stolen password is not enough.
  4. EndpointEndpoint protection and no admin rights on the back-office PC.
  5. NetworkSegmentation keeps the PC away from the tills and the card zone.
  6. DataCard data is tokenized and the loyalty database is encrypted.
  7. Monitoring and recoveryAlerts on unusual logins, and tested offline backups (objective 2.2).

The layers should be diverse: two firewalls from the same vendor with the same flaw count as one layer. NIST treats this as its own principle (diversity), and SP 800-53 mentions supplier diversity (PL-8(2)). CISA's older recommended practice for industrial control systems (2016) lays out the same idea for OT, from risk management and physical security through network zones, host security and monitoring to vendor management and "the human element".

Zero trust

Objective 4.2Understanding zero trust as removing trust based on network location, verifying every request, and knowing NIST's tenets and how US policy applies them.

Traditional networks trusted anything inside the perimeter. NIST SP 800-207 explains the problem: "once attackers breach the perimeter, further lateral movement is unhindered." Zero trust "provides a collection of concepts and ideas designed to minimize uncertainty in enforcing accurate, least privilege per-request access decisions in information systems and services in the face of a network viewed as compromised." A zero trust architecture (ZTA) is the enterprise's plan for applying those concepts. Executive Order 14028 (2021) adds that the model "assumes that a breach is inevitable or has likely already occurred."

SP 800-207 sets out seven tenets, summarized:

  1. Everything is a resourceAll data sources and computing services, including personal devices that access company resources.
  2. Secure all communicationRegardless of network location: "network location alone does not imply trust."
  3. Per-session accessGranted for each session, with "the least privileges needed to complete the task."
  4. Dynamic policyDecisions use the user's identity, the application and the device's state, plus behaviour and environment.
  5. Monitor every asset"No asset is inherently trusted"; device health is checked continuously.
  6. Authenticate and authorize every timeDynamically and strictly, before access, using MFA and identity management.
  7. Collect and learnGather data on assets, network and traffic and use it to improve security.

Decisions are made by a policy decision point and enforced by a policy enforcement point in front of each resource (objective 3.2). Everything behind the enforcement point is an implicit trust zone, which NIST says "must be as small as possible." SP 800-207 describes three ways to build it, noting that a full solution uses all three: enhanced identity governance (identity at the centre, see objective 3.1), micro-segmentation (above) and software-defined perimeters (the network is hidden until a user and device are verified). In June 2025 NIST published SP 1800-35, with 19 example zero trust builds from commercial products.

US policy status

  • Executive Order 14028 (May 2021) directed federal agencies to "advance toward Zero Trust Architecture"; it remains in force.
  • OMB M-22-09 (January 2022) set civilian agencies zero trust goals by the end of fiscal year 2024, organized around CISA's five pillars: identity, devices, networks, applications and workloads, and data. It includes phishing-resistant MFA, encrypting DNS and HTTP traffic, and requiring agencies to "break down their perimeters into isolated environments."
  • The Department of Defense strategy (2022) targets its baseline zero trust level by the end of fiscal year 2027, using seven pillars. NSA published Zero Trust Implementation Guidelines for it in January 2026.

Do not mix the numbers or the slogans. NIST SP 800-207 has seven tenets and three approaches; CISA and OMB use five pillars plus three cross-cutting capabilities (visibility and analytics, automation and orchestration, governance); the Defense Department and NSA use seven pillars. "Never trust, always verify" and "assume breach" come from Defense Department and NSA guidance, not from the NIST tenets, and "castle and moat" is an informal description of the old perimeter model. Zero trust is also not a product: SP 800-207 calls it "a set of guiding principles", and moving to it "is a journey".

Isolating AI environments

Objective 4.2Applying segmentation and zero trust to AI systems, so that development work cannot reach sensitive production data and models are protected.

The CC outline expects segmentation such as VLANs and micro-segmentation to keep AI development environments isolated from sensitive production data, alongside defense in depth and zero trust. Joint guidance led by NSA's AI Security Center with CISA, the FBI and international partners, "Deploying AI Systems Securely" (April 2024), recommends:

  • protections for "the boundaries between the IT environment and the AI system";
  • running models in hardened containers or virtual machines, with firewall allow lists;
  • to "aggressively isolate weight storage" (the trained model files) in a highly restricted zone;
  • applying zero trust frameworks and "least privilege and defense-in-depth" to the AI architecture.

Development and production separation is what CISA calls macrosegmentation, and zero trust applies to AI agents too: a NIST blog in 2026 notes that "as agent autonomy grows, Zero Trust principles such as least privilege, continuous verification, and strong identity become even more critical."

At the retailer, the data team's forecasting environment sits in its own cloud network with no route to the production loyalty database. It receives a weekly extract with customer identifiers removed, through a one-way export job. The trained model is stored in a restricted storage account that only the deployment pipeline can write to.

Key takeaways

  • Segmentation splits the network so breaches are contained, lateral movement is limited and compliance scope shrinks. Allow only required traffic between segments (CISA CPG 3.I, SP 800-53 SC-7).
  • Zones: untrusted Internet, DMZ (screened subnet) for public-facing servers, internal zones by trust level (card, staff, OT/IoT, guest, management). Bastion and jump servers concentrate access; data diodes allow one-way flow; air gaps disconnect entirely.
  • VLANs (802.1Q, 12-bit IDs) separate traffic at layer 2 on shared switches; the firewall rules between VLANs make the boundary. Guard against VLAN hopping; use separate switches for safety-critical OT.
  • Micro-segmentation puts rules around individual workloads; macrosegmentation covers broad zones such as dev vs production and the DMZ. Plan by mapping dependencies, then deploy in monitoring mode first.
  • Defense in depth: people, technology and operations in multiple, diverse, coordinated layers, so no single control is the only barrier.
  • Zero trust (SP 800-207): no trust from network location; per-session, least-privilege, dynamically verified access; seven tenets, three approaches (identity governance, micro-segmentation, software-defined perimeter). US policy: EO 14028, OMB M-22-09 (five pillars), DoD target FY2027.
  • For AI: isolate development from production data, run models in hardened containers, keep model weights in a restricted zone, and apply zero trust to AI agents.
