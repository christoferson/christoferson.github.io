1.3: Understand governance concepts

The rules security works within: laws and regulations that come from outside, frameworks that describe good practice, and the organization's own policies, standards and procedures that turn both into daily practice.

Overview

Objective 1.3

Understand governance concepts.

Governance answers "who decides, by what rules, and how do we know the rules are followed?" Some rules are imposed from outside (laws and regulations), some are good practice the organization chooses to follow (frameworks), and some it writes itself (policies, standards, procedures and guidelines). An entry-level practitioner needs to know which is which, because they carry different weight: breaking a law has legal consequences, ignoring a guideline may not.

This page explains the categories with current examples; laws change often, so each example notes its status in late 2026. How governance, risk and compliance are run as a program is covered in objective 2.1. The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer from the earlier objectives: 60 US stores, an online shop that also sells to customers in Europe, card payments, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools. Each rule below is tested against the question "does this apply to the retailer?"

What governance is

Objective 1.3Seeing governance as the leadership layer of security: setting direction, assigning responsibility and making sure the organization meets its obligations.

NIST SP 800-100 defines information security governance as "the process of establishing and maintaining a framework and supporting management structure and processes" that make sure security strategies "are aligned with and support business objectives, are consistent with applicable laws and regulations through adherence to policies and internal controls, and provide assignment of responsibility, all in an effort to manage risk."

That sentence contains the four jobs of governance:

  • Align security with what the business is trying to do. The retailer's security exists to keep stores trading and customers trusting it, not for its own sake.
  • Comply with the laws, regulations and contracts that apply.
  • Direct through policies and internal controls that everyone follows.
  • Assign responsibility, so every risk and every control has an owner who answers for it.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 made this its own function, Govern: "The organization's cybersecurity risk management strategy, expectations, and policy are established, communicated, and monitored." It sits at the center of the framework because it shapes how all the other functions are carried out.

Governance is a leadership job. Senior management and the board are accountable for security governance; the security team advises and carries it out. When a question asks who is ultimately responsible for setting security direction or accepting risk, the answer is senior leadership, not the IT department. This ties to due care and due diligence in objective 1.5.

Laws and regulations

Objective 1.3Recognizing the legal rules that force organizations to protect information, and how they differ from industry rules and contracts.

Where the rules come from

TypeWhat it isExample
Law (statute)Passed by a legislature. In the US, "the new law is called an act or statute" (EPA)The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002
RegulationDetailed rules written by a government agency that a law authorizes. "Regulations set specific requirements about what is legal and what isn't" (EPA)The FTC Safeguards Rule, made under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act
Industry standardRules set by an industry body, enforced through business agreements rather than by governmentPCI DSS, set by the card industry's council
ContractObligations two parties agree to, such as security clauses with a supplierA cloud provider's data-processing agreement

PCI DSS is not a law. The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard is "a baseline of technical and operational requirements designed to protect payment account data", set by the industry's Security Standards Council. Whether a merchant must comply is decided by the card brands and the merchant's bank, through their agreements. The consequences of ignoring it (fines from the bank, losing the ability to take cards) are serious but contractual, not criminal.

EU regulations and directives

The European Union uses two kinds of law. A regulation "is a binding legislative act. It must be applied in its entirety across the EU." A directive "sets out a goal that EU countries must achieve", but "it is up to the individual countries to devise their own laws on how to reach these goals." GDPR, DORA and the AI Act are regulations, so the same text applies everywhere. NIS2 is a directive, so each country passed its own national law.

The exam is international and does not test any one country's law in detail, but these are the names that come up most. The status column is as of November 2026.

Not yet in force. The US CIRCIA law (2022) will require critical infrastructure organizations to report cyber incidents to CISA, but its final rule has not been issued. CISA states that until it takes effect, "organizations are not required to submit covered cyber incident or ransom payment reports under CIRCIA." Older material that describes its 72-hour reporting as current is ahead of the facts.

Which laws apply to the retailer?

  • State breach notification laws: yes, it holds customers' personal information in every state it sells to.
  • GDPR: yes, for its European customers, even though it has no office in the EU, because it offers them goods.
  • CCPA: likely, if it meets California's thresholds for revenue or volume of customer data.
  • PCI DSS: yes, by contract with its bank, because it takes cards.
  • SOX and the SEC rule: only if it is a publicly traded company.
  • HIPAA, DORA, FISMA: no; it is not a health, financial or federal organization.

Notice how the notification clocks differ: GDPR 72 hours to the regulator, NIS2 a 24-hour early warning, the SEC four business days after deciding the incident is material, the FTC 30 days. A good incident response plan lists every clock that applies, which is part of objective 5.3.

Frameworks and guidelines

Objective 1.3Knowing the main published frameworks, what each is for, and which ones are voluntary, certifiable or contractual.

A framework is a published, structured description of good security practice. Organizations adopt one so they do not have to invent their own program from scratch, and so that customers, auditors and regulators can recognize what they are doing. Most frameworks are voluntary unless a law, regulator or contract makes them mandatory.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

Published February 26, 2024, replacing version 1.1. It describes outcomes, not methods: "The CSF does not prescribe how outcomes should be achieved." It is designed for any organization, and "may be adopted voluntarily and through governmental policies and mandates."

  • Six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Govern was added in 2.0.
  • Profiles: a Current Profile records the outcomes the organization achieves now; a Target Profile the outcomes it wants. The gap between them becomes the improvement plan.
  • Tiers: Partial, Risk Informed, Repeatable and Adaptive, describing "the rigor of an organization's cybersecurity risk governance and management practices". An organization uses them to set a target that fits its risk and resources; nothing requires every organization to reach Tier 4.

ISO/IEC 27001 and 27002

The ISO/IEC 27000 family is the main international set of information security standards.

  • ISO/IEC 27001:2022 sets the requirements for an information security management system (ISMS): the policies, risk process and continual improvement cycle an organization runs. An organization can be certified against it by an accredited auditor. Certificates to the old 2013 edition expired or were withdrawn at the end of the transition period on October 31, 2025.
  • ISO/IEC 27002:2022 is guidance: a reference set of security controls to choose from when building the ISMS. Organizations are not certified against 27002.

CIS Critical Security Controls

The Center for Internet Security publishes the CIS Controls, "a prioritized set of CIS Safeguards to defend against the most prevalent cyber attacks". Version 8.1 has 18 controls containing 153 safeguards, starting with knowing what hardware and software you have. They are grouped into three Implementation Groups, so smaller organizations know where to start:

GroupSafeguardsWho it suits
IG156, described by CIS as "essential cyber hygiene"Every organization; the minimum for small ones with little security staff
IG2IG1 plus 74 moreOrganizations with more complex systems or sensitive data
IG3All 153Organizations facing skilled attackers or strict regulation

CIS also publishes the CIS Benchmarks: consensus-based secure configuration guides for specific products, such as an operating system or a cloud service. Configuration baselines like these come back in objective 5.4.

Voluntary, certifiable or required? The NIST CSF and the CIS Controls are voluntary frameworks, and neither NIST nor CIS certifies organizations against them. ISO/IEC 27001 is voluntary but certifiable. SOC 2 is an audit report, not a certificate. PCI DSS is required by contract for anyone taking cards. NIST SP 800-53 is required for US federal systems (through FISMA) and voluntary for everyone else.

Policies, standards and procedures

Objective 1.3Telling apart the organization's own governing documents by how broad, how detailed and how binding each one is.

Laws and frameworks say what must be achieved. The organization's own documents turn that into instructions its staff can follow. NIST SP 800-12 Rev. 1 describes the layers: policy is "written at a broad level", so organizations "also develop standards, guidelines, and procedures" to make it practical.

DocumentWhat it doesBinding?Retailer example
PolicyStates management's intent and rules: the what and why. NIST SP 800-82: policies answer "what" and "why" "without dealing with "how""Yes; approved by senior management"All access to company systems requires multi-factor authentication."
StandardSpecifies "uniform use of specific technologies, parameters, or procedures"Yes: "normally compulsory within an organization""Approved MFA methods are security keys and the company authenticator app; SMS codes are not allowed."
Procedure"Detailed steps to be followed ... to accomplish a particular task": the howYes, for the people doing that taskThe help desk's step-by-step guide to issuing a security key to a new employee
GuidelineAdvice that helps people apply the rules; it "can be implemented, and correctly so, in more than one way"No; recommendedTips for store managers on spotting fake MFA prompts
BaselineThe minimum set of security settings or controls for a type of systemYes, as a minimumThe required configuration for every till, based on a CIS Benchmark

Two meanings of "standard". SP 800-12 warns that an organization's internal standards are "not to be confused with" national or international standards such as ISO or FIPS. An ISO standard is an external, consensus document; the retailer's "MFA standard" is an internal rule. Questions about the document that names specific approved technologies mean the internal kind.

A common way to remember the order, from broad to detailed: policy (why and what) → standard (with what) → procedure (how, step by step). Guidelines sit alongside as optional advice. If the policy changes, everything below it is reviewed.

Three kinds of policy

SP 800-12 Rev. 1 sorts policies into three types:

  • Program policy: creates the security program, sets "the strategic direction for security" and assigns resources. It is issued by a senior official and covers purpose, scope, responsibilities and compliance, including penalties for breaking it.
  • Issue-specific policy: covers one topic of current concern, such as internet use, email, bring-your-own-device or social media. Because technology changes quickly, these "must be reviewed on a regular basis".
  • System-specific policy: states "what actions are permitted on a particular system", for example who may change prices in the retailer's point-of-sale system.

Policies must be communicated and enforced to mean anything. CSF 2.0 expects policy to be "established, communicated, and enforced" (GV.PO), and kept "reviewed, updated, communicated, and enforced" as things change. Staff usually confirm they have read key policies, such as the acceptable use policy, when they join and each year after; awareness training is covered in objective 2.3.

Governing AI

Objective 1.3Applying the same laws, frameworks and policies to AI tools as to any other software, plus the new rules written for AI.

The CC outline makes the point that AI tools are not exempt: the organization's existing policies and legal obligations apply to them just as they do to traditional software. A customer-service chatbot that handles personal data is subject to GDPR and state breach laws exactly as the loyalty database is. On top of that, AI has its own governance documents:

ItemTypeStatus
EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)Law, risk-based: bans some uses, sets duties for "high-risk" systemsBans applied from February 2, 2025; rules for general-purpose AI models from August 2, 2025. An amendment in July 2026 (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) moved the high-risk obligations to December 2, 2027 and August 2, 2028
ISO/IEC 42001:2023Standard: a certifiable AI management system, the AI counterpart of 27001Current
NIST AI RMF 1.0Voluntary framework (see objective 1.2)Being revised
NIST Cyber AI Profile (IR 8596)CSF 2.0 profile for AIPreliminary draft, December 2025

AI Act dates have moved. Material written before mid-2026 says the high-risk rules apply from August 2026 or August 2027. Those dates were replaced by the July 2026 amendment. The bans and the general-purpose AI rules are unchanged.

Inside the organization, the usual tool is an issue-specific policy on AI use. A short one at the retailer might look like this:

Issue-specific policy: use of AI tools (summary)
Purpose
Let staff benefit from AI tools without exposing customer or company data
Scope
All staff and contractors, all AI tools, including free public chatbots
Position
Only tools on the approved list may be used for work. Customer personal data and card data must never be entered into an unapproved tool
Responsibilities
The security team maintains the approved list; managers make sure their teams follow it; each AI tool has a named business owner
Compliance
Breaches are handled under the disciplinary policy
Review
Every six months, because the tools change quickly

The structure follows SP 800-12's parts of an issue-specific policy. The detailed standard (which tools are approved) and procedure (how to request a new one) would sit underneath it.

Key takeaways

  • Governance aligns security with business goals, ensures compliance, directs through policy and assigns responsibility. Senior leadership is accountable; CSF 2.0 calls this the Govern function.
  • A law (statute) is passed by a legislature; a regulation is detailed rules from an agency; an industry standard such as PCI DSS is enforced by contract, not by government. An EU regulation applies directly; a directive (NIS2) needs national laws.
  • Recognize GDPR (EUR 20 million or 4%), NIS2 (24-hour early warning), DORA (January 2025), HIPAA (ePHI), GLBA/FTC Safeguards Rule, SOX, FISMA, the SEC rule (four business days after materiality) and state breach laws (all 50 states). CIRCIA reporting is not yet required.
  • NIST CSF 2.0: six functions, Current and Target Profiles, four Tiers, voluntary and outcome-based. ISO/IEC 27001 is certifiable; 27002 is control guidance. CIS Controls v8.1: 18 controls, 153 safeguards, IG1 = 56 safeguards of essential cyber hygiene.
  • Policy = what and why (binding, approved by management); standard = specific required technologies or settings; procedure = step-by-step how; guideline = optional advice; baseline = minimum configuration.
  • Policy types (SP 800-12): program (sets up the security program), issue-specific (one topic, reviewed often), system-specific (rules for one system).
  • AI tools fall under the same laws and policies as other software. The EU AI Act's high-risk dates moved to December 2027 and August 2028; ISO/IEC 42001 is the certifiable AI management system.
