1.2: Understand risk management concepts

How organizations decide which dangers matter: the words used to describe risk, the cycle every risk program follows, how risks are measured and ranked, the ways to respond to them, and how they are tracked over time.

Overview

Objective 1.2

Understand risk management concepts.

No organization can protect everything equally, and none can remove every danger. Risk management is how it decides where to spend its effort: work out what could go wrong, how likely and how harmful that would be, choose a response, and keep checking. This objective covers the lifecycle (the repeating cycle of steps) and the processes inside it (assessing, responding, recording and monitoring).

The definitions here come from NIST, mainly SP 800-39 (managing security risk), SP 800-30 (risk assessments) and the IR 8286 series (linking cybersecurity risk to business risk). The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The same mid-sized retail company as in objective 1.1: about 800 staff, 60 stores, an online shop, card payments, a cloud loyalty database, and new AI tools for recommendations and customer service.

The language of risk

Objective 1.2Telling apart the things that make up a risk: what is valuable, what could harm it, the weakness that lets harm happen, and how likely and how bad that would be.

Exam questions often hinge on using these words precisely. NIST defines risk as "a measure of the extent to which an entity is threatened by a potential circumstance or event", typically a function of "the adverse impacts that would arise if the circumstance or event occurs" and "the likelihood of occurrence". In plain terms: risk is how likely something bad is, combined with how bad it would be.

TermNIST meaningRetailer example
Asset"An item of value to the achievement of organizational mission/business objectives" (CNSSI 4009)The loyalty database, the online shop, the tills, staff
Threat"Any circumstance or event with the potential to adversely impact" operations, assets or people (SP 800-53)Ransomware hitting the store network
Threat source or actor"A malicious person with harmful intent or an unintended or unavoidable situation (such as a natural disaster, technical failure, or human error)" (IR 8286)A criminal group; a flood at the data center; a careless employee
Vulnerability"Weakness in an information system, system security procedures, internal controls, or implementation that could be exploited or triggered by a threat source" (SP 800-53)An unpatched server; staff who reuse passwords
Likelihood"A weighted factor based on a subjective analysis of the probability" that a threat exploits a vulnerability (SP 800-30)"High": attacks on unpatched servers are common
ImpactThe effect on operations, assets or people "of a loss of confidentiality, integrity, or availability" (SP 800-53)Stores unable to take payments for two days

Put together, a risk reads like a sentence: a threat source uses a vulnerability to harm an asset, with some likelihood and impact. "A criminal group (threat source) exploits the unpatched server (vulnerability) to encrypt the payment systems (asset); likely, and the impact is two days of lost sales."

A threat is not a risk, and a vulnerability is not a risk. NIST IR 8286 Rev. 1 notes that a vulnerability "does not cause harm in and of itself, as there needs to be a threat present to exploit it". A weakness nobody can reach, or a threat with no weakness to use, is low risk. Risk only exists where the two meet and something valuable is at stake.

Inherent and residual risk

  • Inherent risk: the risk "in the absence of any direct or focused actions by management to alter its severity" (IR 8286, from COSO) — the risk before any controls.
  • Residual risk: the "portion of risk remaining after security measures have been applied" (SP 800-30).

If the retailer patches the server and adds offline backups, ransomware becomes less likely and less damaging, but not impossible. What is left is residual risk, and someone must decide whether it is acceptable.

Residual risk is never zero. SP 800-39: whatever the decision, "there still remains a degree of residual risk that must be addressed." The NIST AI Risk Management Framework adds that "attempting to eliminate negative risk entirely can be counterproductive". An answer that promises to remove all risk is wrong.

The risk management lifecycle

Objective 1.2Seeing risk management as a loop that never finishes: set the ground rules, find and weigh the risks, act on them, and watch for change.

Risk management is not a one-off project. Systems, attackers and the business all change, so the steps repeat. NIST SP 800-39 describes four components that every organization's risk process contains:

  1. FrameSet the context: the assumptions, constraints, risk tolerance and priorities that guide decisions. The result is a risk management strategy saying how the organization will assess, respond to and monitor risk.
  2. AssessIdentify threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood and impact. "The end result is a determination of risk (i.e., the degree of harm and likelihood of harm occurring)."
  3. RespondDecide what to do about each risk, giving "a consistent, organization-wide, response".
  4. MonitorCheck that responses were put in place, that they keep working, and spot "risk-impacting changes" — which feeds back into framing and assessing.

The NIST IR 8286 series, which ties cybersecurity risk into an organization's overall (enterprise) risk management, describes a similar cycle in six steps, adapted from ISO 31000 and the US Federal ERM Playbook:

IR 8286 stepWhat happensSP 800-39 match
1. Identify the contextObjectives, risk appetite and tolerance, what is in scopeFrame
2. Identify the risksList what could go wrong (and what opportunities exist)Assess
3. Analyze the risksEstimate likelihood and impactAssess
4. Prioritize the risksRank by exposure, the combination of likelihood and impactAssess
5. Plan and execute risk response strategiesAccept, avoid, mitigate or transfer, then do itRespond
6. Monitor, evaluate, and adjustKeep risk "within the defined risk appetite levels"Monitor

IR 8286 was replaced in December 2025. NIST withdrew IR 8286, 8286A and 8286C on December 18, 2025 and replaced each with a Rev. 1, updated for the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0. Many glossary entries and study guides still cite the original "NISTIR 8286"; the ideas are the same, but Rev. 1 is the current text.

Three levels of risk decisions

SP 800-39 also says risk is managed at three tiers: the organization (Tier 1: strategy, appetite, the risk frame), the mission or business process (Tier 2: for example "online sales" or "payroll"), and the information system (Tier 3: one application or network). Decisions flow down and information flows back up: the board sets the appetite; the people running one system report its risks.

The NIST Risk Management Framework

For a single system, US federal agencies follow the Risk Management Framework (RMF) in NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2 (December 2018, still current). Its seven steps apply the same cycle to one system:

StepPurpose (NIST wording)
Prepare"Essential activities to prepare the organization to manage security and privacy risks"
CategorizeCategorize the system and its information "based on an impact analysis" (the FIPS 199 levels from objective 1.1)
Select"Select the set of NIST SP 800-53 controls to protect the system based on risk assessment(s)"
Implement"Implement the controls and document how controls are deployed"
AssessDetermine "if the controls are in place, operating as intended, and producing the desired results"
Authorize"Senior official makes a risk-based decision to authorize the system (to operate)"
Monitor"Continuously monitor control implementation and risks to the system"

The Authorize step is where risk acceptance becomes formal: a named senior official signs off that the system's residual risk is acceptable. That official, not the IT team, owns the decision.

NIST's Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (February 2024) puts the same ideas under its Govern function. Category GV.RM requires, for example, that "risk appetite and risk tolerance statements are established, communicated, and maintained" (GV.RM-02) and that there is "a standardized method for calculating, documenting, categorizing, and prioritizing cybersecurity risks" (GV.RM-06). Frameworks in general are covered in objective 1.3.

Assessing risk

Objective 1.2Finding risks and putting a size on them, either with descriptive levels or with money and numbers.

A risk assessment is "the process of identifying risks" to operations, assets and people; it "incorporates threat and vulnerability analyses, and considers mitigations provided by security controls planned or in place" (SP 800-39). NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 (still current) lays out four steps:

  1. PrepareAgree the purpose, scope, assumptions, information sources and the method to use.
  2. ConductIdentify threat sources and threat events; identify vulnerabilities and conditions that make harm more likely; determine likelihood; determine impact; combine them into a level of risk.
  3. CommunicateShare the results with the people who make decisions.
  4. MaintainKeep watching the risk factors and update the assessment when they change.

Qualitative, semi-quantitative and quantitative

ApproachHow it measuresStrength and weakness
QualitativeDescriptive levels: "very low, low, moderate, high, very high"Quick and easy to explain to decision makers; different experts "could produce significantly different" results
Semi-quantitativeScores or bins (1–10, or 0–100 where "a score of 95 can be interpreted as very high")Easier to rank and compare; the numbers only mean something inside that assessment
QuantitativeReal numbers, usually money and frequency"Most effectively supports cost-benefit analyses"; needs good data and can cost more than it is worth

In a qualitative assessment, likelihood and impact are each rated, then combined using a table agreed in advance (a risk matrix). At the retailer, ransomware on the store network might be rated likelihood high, impact very high, so overall risk very high; a stolen display laptop with no data on it might be moderate likelihood but very low impact.

NIST says risk is a function or combination of likelihood and impact, not a strict multiplication. "Likelihood × impact" is a useful shorthand. Note that SP 800-30's middle level is called moderate, not "medium".

Putting a price on risk

Quantitative assessment usually estimates money lost. NIST IR 8286A Rev. 1 describes the two figures leaders ask for: "the loss for each occurrence (the single loss expectancy, or SLE)" and "the total loss for that risk over an annual period (the annualized loss expectancy, or ALE)". Security courses commonly calculate them like this (a widely taught convention; NIST itself does not publish the formulas):

Worked example: stolen store laptops
Asset value (AV)
$2,000 per laptop, including setup time
Exposure factor (EF)
100%: a stolen laptop is a total loss
Single loss expectancy (SLE = AV × EF)
$2,000 per theft
Annualized rate of occurrence (ARO)
6 thefts a year across 60 stores
Annualized loss expectancy (ALE = SLE × ARO)
$12,000 a year
Decision
Cable locks for every store cost $3,000 a year and are expected to halve thefts, saving about $6,000. The control is worth it.

The comparison in the last line is the point: a control is worth buying when it reduces expected loss by more than it costs. Note what the numbers miss: if the laptops held unencrypted customer data, the real impact (breach notification, fines, lost trust) would dwarf the hardware cost.

The original NIST source for this idea, FIPS 65 (1979), defined annual loss exposure as "the product of estimated impact in dollars (I) and estimated frequency of occurrence per year (F)". It was withdrawn in 1995 and replaced by SP 800-30.

Responding to risk

Objective 1.2Choosing what to do about each risk, guided by how much risk the organization has decided it can live with.

Risk appetite and risk tolerance

Before choosing a response, the organization needs to know how much risk is acceptable. NIST IR 8286 Rev. 1 separates two terms:

  • Risk appetite: "the types and amount of risk, on a broad level, [an organization] is willing to accept in its pursuit of value". It is set by the most senior leaders.
  • Risk tolerance: the "readiness to bear the remaining risk after risk response in order to achieve its objectives". It is narrower and usually set for a program or system, often as a measurable limit.

NIST's own example: the appetite says "email service shall be available during the large majority of a 24-hour period"; the tolerance says "email services shall not be interrupted for more than five minutes during core hours." For the retailer, the appetite might be "we accept low risk to customer data and moderate risk to internal tools"; a tolerance might be "the online shop may be unavailable for no more than 30 minutes a month."

Older material uses one word for both. IR 8286 Rev. 1 notes that SP 800-39 "uses the term 'risk tolerance' to collectively refer to" both ideas. On the exam, read appetite as the broad, top-level statement and tolerance as the specific acceptable limit.

The response options

SP 800-39 lists five responses: "risk acceptance; risk avoidance; risk mitigation; risk sharing; risk transfer; or a combination".

ResponseWhat it meansRetailer example
AcceptDo nothing more, because the risk is within tolerance. IR 8286 Rev. 1: "No additional risk response action is needed except for monitoring."Accept the small risk of a store's guest Wi-Fi going down
AvoidStop or never start the activity that creates the risk, used when the risk "exceeds the organizational risk tolerance"Stop storing card numbers at all and let the payment processor hold them
Mitigate (reduce)Apply controls that lower likelihood or impact "to an acceptable level"Patch servers, add MFA, keep offline backups
TransferShift the financial consequences to another party, typically through insurance or a contractBuy cyber insurance that covers breach costs
ShareSplit part of the risk with another organization, each accepting its portionA store credit card run with a bank, where the contract splits fraud losses between them

Most real risks get a combination: the retailer mitigates ransomware with patching and backups, transfers part of the cost with insurance, and accepts what is left.

Transferring a risk does not make the harm go away. SP 800-39: "risk transfer reduces neither the likelihood of harmful events occurring nor the consequences in terms of harm". IR 8286 Rev. 1 adds that some consequences "cannot be transferred, like the loss of customer trust." If the insured retailer is breached, its customers are still harmed and it still answers to regulators.

Share or transfer? SP 800-39 treats them separately: transfer "shifts the entire risk responsibility or liability", sharing "a portion". IR 8286 Rev. 1 lists only four responses for negative risk (accept, transfer, mitigate, avoid) and describes transfer as sharing part of the consequences. Either way, insurance is the classic example of transfer.

Risk can also be positive

Not every risk is a threat. The Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 asks organizations to consider "strategic opportunities (i.e., positive risks)" (GV.RM-07), such as moving to a cloud service that improves resilience. IR 8286 Rev. 1 gives four responses to an opportunity: realize (sometimes called exploit), share, enhance and accept.

Responses also have side effects: "risk responses themselves may introduce new risks." NIST's example is MFA slowing staff down. Avoiding a risk can mean losing the opportunity that came with the activity, and that cost should be weighed too.

Recording and monitoring risk

Objective 1.2Keeping one up-to-date list of risks, each with a named owner, and watching for anything that changes them.

A risk register is "a repository of risk information, including the data understood about risks over time" (IR 8286 Rev. 1). It lets leaders see every risk in one place, ranked by priority. IR 8286 Rev. 1 suggests these fields for a cybersecurity risk register; here is one entry from the retailer's register:

The risk owner is "the designated party responsible and accountable for ensuring that the risk is maintained in accordance with enterprise requirements". It is usually a business leader, not the security team: here the person whose stores stop trading if the risk happens. IR 8286 Rev. 1 recommends keeping the register a short summary, with the details in a separate risk detail record for each risk.

Accepted risks stay in the register. Accepting a risk means choosing to live with it, not forgetting it: it still has an owner and is still monitored.

Monitoring and change

NIST SP 800-137 defines information security continuous monitoring (ISCM) as "maintaining ongoing awareness of information security, vulnerabilities, and threats to support organizational risk management decisions". "Continuous" does not mean every second; it means checking "at a frequency sufficient to support risk-based security decisions".

Things that should send a risk back through the cycle include a new type of attack, a newly published vulnerability, a new system or supplier, a merger, a new law, or an incident. CSF 2.0 makes this explicit: changes and exceptions should be "managed, assessed for risk impact, recorded, and tracked" (ID.RA-07). How organizations measure whether risk is under control, with metrics and Key Risk Indicators, is covered in objective 2.4.

Managing AI risk

Objective 1.2Applying the same risk cycle to AI systems, which bring new kinds of harm alongside the usual security ones.

AI tools are assets with their own threats and vulnerabilities, and they fit into the same risk register as everything else. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, NIST AI 100-1, January 2023) organizes the work into four functions:

FunctionNIST wordingRetailer example
Govern"Cultivates and implements a culture of risk management"; cross-cutting, it informs the other threeAn AI use policy and a named owner for each AI tool
Map"Establishes the context to frame risks related to an AI system"List what the customer-service chatbot can see and do, and who could be harmed
MeasureUses "quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method tools" to "analyze, assess, benchmark, and monitor AI risk"Test the chatbot for leaking customer data or giving wrong refund rules
Manage"Allocating risk resources to mapped and measured risks on a regular basis"Limit the chatbot's data access, add human review of refunds, record the residual risk

The AI RMF deliberately "does not prescribe risk tolerance": each organization decides for itself, in line with its risk appetite. The ISC2 outline makes the same link, asking entry-level staff to support AI adoption in a way that fits "the organization's risk appetite".

Status in 2026. NIST's AI RMF page says version 1.0 "is being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan"; no draft of the new version has been published. The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024) remains current. A CSF 2.0 profile for AI, the Cyber AI Profile (NIST IR 8596), was released as a preliminary draft in December 2025 and is not yet final.

Key takeaways

  • Risk combines likelihood and impact. It exists where a threat can use a vulnerability to harm an asset; a vulnerability alone is not a risk.
  • Inherent risk is before controls; residual risk is what remains after them, and it is never zero.
  • The lifecycle repeats: SP 800-39's frame, assess, respond, monitor, or IR 8286 Rev. 1's six steps (context, identify, analyze, prioritize, respond, monitor). Decisions happen at the organization, business process and system tiers.
  • The RMF (SP 800-37 Rev. 2) has seven steps: Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize (a senior official accepts the residual risk), Monitor.
  • SP 800-30 assessments: prepare, conduct, communicate, maintain. Qualitative uses levels (very low to very high, with "moderate" in the middle); quantitative uses money. Common formulas: SLE = AV × EF, ALE = SLE × ARO; buy a control when it saves more than it costs.
  • Appetite is the broad amount of risk leaders accept; tolerance is the specific acceptable limit. Older NIST text uses "tolerance" for both.
  • Responses: accept (within tolerance, still monitored), avoid (stop the activity), mitigate (controls), transfer (insurance, contracts), share, or a combination. Transfer does not remove the harm or the loss of trust.
  • A risk register lists each risk with its rating, response, cost, status and a business risk owner. Continuous monitoring (SP 800-137) means often enough to support decisions.
  • AI risk follows the same cycle. NIST AI RMF functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage; it leaves risk tolerance to each organization and is being revised in 2026.
