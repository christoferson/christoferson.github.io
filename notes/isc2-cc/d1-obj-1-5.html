1.5: Maintain professional and ethical conduct

Overview

Objective 1.5

Maintain professional and ethical conduct.

Security people are trusted with access, secrets and power over systems that others depend on. This objective is about using that trust well. It has three parts: codes of conduct (written expectations from an employer or a professional body), due care and due diligence (the legal standard of acting as a reasonable, careful person would), and the ISC2 Code of Ethics, which every ISC2 member, including CC holders, commits to.

About the ISC2 texts on this page. ISC2's documents are copyrighted, and ISC2 says its content may not be reproduced without permission. So this page explains the Code of Ethics and the Code of Professional Conduct in its own words. Read the exact wording on ISC2's site: the Code of Ethics and the Code of Professional Conduct. The exam outline is on isc2.org.

Codes of conduct

Objective 1.5Knowing the written behaviour expectations a security professional works under, and who can hold them to each one.

A security professional usually answers to more than one code at once:

CodeWho sets itHow it binds you
Employer's code of conductYour organizationThrough your employment; breaking it can lead to discipline or dismissal
Professional body's code of ethicsISC2, ISACA, ACM, IEEE and othersThrough membership or certification; breaking it can cost you the credential
The lawGovernmentsApplies to everyone, whatever they have signed

They overlap rather than compete. ISC2's own guidance asks practitioners to follow their organization's code of conduct as part of respecting laws and regulations.

ISC2's Code of Professional Conduct (2026)

On February 24, 2026, ISC2 launched a Code of Professional Conduct, developed with input from about 1,400 professionals. It is a practical companion to the Code of Ethics, written for all cybersecurity practitioners, certified or not. Two facts matter for the exam:

  • It is guidance, not a requirement. Certification still depends on the Code of Ethics, which ISC2 says remains unchanged.
  • It turns the high-level canons into everyday expectations.

In summary, its themes are:

ThemeWhat it expects, in brief
IntegrityBe honest and open, declare any actual or possible conflict of interest, refuse bribes, and do not misuse privileged access
ConfidentialityProtect the data and privacy of the people and organizations you work for
Respect for the lawComply with laws, regulations and your organization's own code
Public safetyReduce risk to society, and secure new technologies such as AI responsibly, with governance in place before they are deployed
ResponsibilityBalance security with business goals; protecting human life comes first
Teamwork and competenceTreat colleagues with respect, lead ethically, and keep your skills current
Raising concernsReport vulnerabilities, compliance breaches and unethical behaviour through proper channels, such as an ethics hotline, HR or a compliance officer

In April 2026 ISC2 added an ethical decision-making guide: understand the situation (the issue, the facts, who is affected), evaluate the options against the codes and the risks, then act and reflect on the result. It warns specifically against excusing a choice on the grounds that it is not illegal.

Other professional codes. ISACA's Code of Professional Ethics asks members to work "with objectivity, due diligence and professional care" and to keep information confidential unless the law requires disclosure. Unlike ISC2, ISACA lets anyone report a violation, even anonymously. ACM and IEEE publish their own codes for computing and engineering professionals.

Due care and due diligence

Objective 1.5Doing what a reasonable, careful organization would do: finding out about its risks, then acting on what it finds.

Both terms come from law, not from NIST (the NIST glossary defines neither). Cornell Law School's legal dictionary explains them:

  • Due care is "ordinary" or "reasonable" care: the care a reasonable person would use in similar circumstances. Courts use it to decide whether someone was negligent.
  • Due diligence is the reasonable care and attention expected of someone to avoid liability, for example reviewing a company's records before buying it.

Security courses split the two like this. The split is a widely taught convention rather than a legal definition; Cornell notes the terms overlap.

TermIdeaRetailer example
Due diligenceFind out: investigate, assess and keep checking. "Know your risks."Before choosing a new payment provider, review its SOC 2 report and security questionnaire; reassess every year
Due careAct: do what a reasonable organization would do about what you found. "Do something about them."Patch the critical flaw the assessment found, train staff, and enforce the contract's security terms

NIST uses "due diligence" in this investigative sense for suppliers: SP 800-161 describes due diligence questionnaires and assessments when choosing and monitoring suppliers. The US Federal Trade Commission, which takes action against companies with poor security, asks businesses to take "reasonable security measures".

When care is missing: negligence

Negligence is failing to use the care a reasonable person would, whether by doing something or by failing to do something one had a duty to do. Cornell lists its usual elements: a duty, a breach of that duty, harm, and a causal link between the breach and the harm. If the retailer knows its customer database has a serious flaw (due diligence was done) but never fixes it (due care was not), and customers' data is then stolen through that flaw, it is exposed to a negligence claim.

Knowing is not enough. An assessment that finds problems, with no action afterwards, can make an organization's position worse: it shows the risk was known. Due diligence without due care is a classic exam scenario. The fix is to act, or to formally accept the risk through the process in objective 1.2.

The CC outline ties these ideas to leadership: entry-level staff help senior management exercise due care and due diligence, including when adopting AI. Accountability stays with leadership, as in objective 1.3.

The ISC2 Code of Ethics

Objective 1.5Knowing the four duties every ISC2 member accepts, in their order, and what happens when someone breaks them.

Every ISC2 member, including CC holders, must commit to fully supporting the Code of Ethics; following it strictly is a condition of certification. ISC2's candidate policies also require exam candidates to abide by it. The Code has a short preamble and four canons (binding principles).

The preamble, in summary: because society's safety and welfare, the common good, and duties to employers and to each other depend on it, members must hold, and be seen to hold, the highest ethical standards.

The four canons, in order and in our own words (the exact text is on isc2.org/ethics):

  1. Canon I: societyProtect the public, the common good, the trust people place in systems, and the infrastructure everyone relies on.
  2. Canon II: integrityBehave with honour and honesty, fairly and responsibly, and within the law.
  3. Canon III: principalsGive the people you work for, your employers and clients ("principals"), careful and competent service.
  4. Canon IV: the professionHelp the cybersecurity profession grow and protect its reputation.

Remember the order as widening circles of duty, from everyone to your own field: society → integrity → principals → profession. Older study material says an earlier canon always outranks a later one; ISC2's current page does not state that rule. But when a scenario sets people's safety against an employer's wishes, safety wins: canon I comes first, and the Code of Professional Conduct calls protecting human life the primary responsibility. Note the spelling: canon III is about principals (the people you serve), not "principles".

Complaints and enforcement

ISC2 accepts ethics complaints only from someone who claims to have been harmed, and which canon they can complain about depends on who they are:

CanonWho may complain
I and IIAny member of the public
IIIOnly principals: an employer or client of the person complained about
IVOnly other professionals who are certified or licensed and themselves follow a code of ethics
  1. Written, sworn complaintThe complainant submits a sworn affidavit naming the person, the behaviour, the canon broken, their standing and any evidence. No other form is considered.
  2. ReviewISC2's Professional Conduct (Ethics) Committee reviews it. It does not investigate on its own; without a convincing case on the evidence submitted, the complaint is closed.
  3. ResponseThe person complained about sees the evidence and can reply, also under oath. Where facts remain disputed, the benefit of the doubt goes to them.
  4. DecisionThe committee recommends action and the ISC2 Board decides; its decision is final. The sanction ISC2 names is revocation of certification.

Members must report breaches they see. An ISC2 member who sees another member break the Code is expected to use the complaint procedure, and failing to do so can itself be treated as a breach of canon IV. Also, do not confuse ethics sanctions with suspension: ISC2 suspends certifications for unpaid annual fees or missing continuing education, not as an ethics penalty.

Keeping CC. CC holders pay an annual maintenance fee of US$50 and earn 45 Group A continuing professional education (CPE) credits every three years, about 15 a year. After a 90-day grace period, a lapsed certification is suspended. Unlike ISC2's other certifications, CC needs no endorsement from an existing member.

Ethics in everyday security work

Objective 1.5Applying the codes to real decisions: staying within your authority, handling what you find responsibly, and speaking up.

Most ethics questions are scenarios. These are the situations that come up most:

SituationThe ethical responseWhy
You want to scan a system to check its securityGet written permission from its owner first, with a clear scopeTesting without authorization can be a crime, whatever your intentions
You find a vulnerability in another company's productTell the vendor privately and give them time to fix it (coordinated disclosure); do not publish or exploit itPublishing first puts users at risk (canon I)
Your access lets you see a colleague's salary or a celebrity customer's ordersDo not look; use access only for your assigned workPrivileged access is held in trust (canons II and III)
A supplier you are assessing offers you an expensive giftDecline or declare it under your employer's rulesEven the appearance of a conflict of interest damages trust
Your manager tells you to hide a breach from customers to avoid bad pressRefuse, and raise it through compliance, legal or an ethics hotlineBreach notification is often a legal duty, and the public comes first (canon I)
You are asked to do work beyond your skillsSay so, and get help or trainingCompetent service is a duty (canon III)

Two public frameworks support these answers. CISA runs a coordinated vulnerability disclosure program: it works with the reporter and the vendor, and if a vendor does not respond, CISA "may disclose ... as early as 45 days after" its first attempt to contact it. The international standard for this is ISO/IEC 29147 (vulnerability disclosure). And the US Department of Justice's 2022 policy tells prosecutors not to charge good-faith security research under the federal anti-hacking law, but it is guidance only and gives no guaranteed protection; the safe course is always to get permission.

Legal is not the same as ethical. Many questions offer an answer that is legal but harmful. The Code of Ethics asks for more than staying within the law, and ISC2's decision guide treats "it's not illegal" as a warning sign, not a justification.

Someone who reports wrongdoing by their employer, inside the organization or to an outside authority, is a whistleblower; many laws protect whistleblowers from retaliation.

Ethics and AI

Objective 1.5Bringing the same care and honesty to AI, where the new risks include unfair bias and decisions nobody can explain.

The CC outline links AI adoption to due care, due diligence and the Code of Ethics, and to a culture that expects transparency and avoids bias. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework lists the characteristics of trustworthy AI:

CharacteristicWhat it means at the retailer
Valid and reliableThe chatbot gives correct refund rules consistently. NIST calls this the base the others rest on
SafeIt cannot be led into giving harmful advice
Secure and resilientIt resists attacks such as prompt injection and keeps working
Accountable and transparentCustomers are told they are talking to an AI, and a named owner answers for it. NIST: "accountability presupposes transparency"
Explainable and interpretableStaff can see why it refused a refund
Privacy-enhancedIt uses only the customer data it needs
Fair, with harmful bias managedIts fraud scoring does not treat customers differently because of where they live

NIST describes three sources of bias, any of which can occur with no intention to discriminate: systemic (in society and institutions, reflected in the data), computational and statistical (in data and algorithms, such as unrepresentative training data), and human-cognitive (in how people design and use the system). NIST SP 1270 (2022) covers this in depth.

Due diligence for an AI tool means finding out how it was trained and tested, what data it uses and what it can get wrong, before relying on it. Due care means acting on that: limiting what it can do, keeping a human in the loop for important decisions, and monitoring it. Adopting an AI tool because it is convenient, without either, falls short of both.

Key takeaways

  • Professionals answer to an employer's code (through employment), a professional body's code (through certification) and the law (always).
  • ISC2's Code of Professional Conduct (February 2026) is voluntary guidance covering integrity, conflicts of interest, confidentiality, respect for the law, public safety, competence and raising concerns. The binding document is the Code of Ethics, which is unchanged.
  • Due diligence = investigate and keep checking; due care = act as a reasonable person would. (A teaching convention; the legal terms overlap.) Knowing about a risk and doing nothing invites a negligence claim: duty, breach, harm, causation.
  • Code of Ethics canons, in order: society, integrity and legality, principals, the profession. Following the Code is a condition of certification.
  • Complaints: anyone for canons I and II; only principals for III; only other certified or licensed professionals for IV. A sworn affidavit is required; the Board decides; the named sanction is revocation. Members must report breaches they see.
  • Get written permission before testing; disclose vulnerabilities to the vendor privately (coordinated disclosure); avoid conflicts of interest; use access only for your job; legal does not automatically mean ethical.
  • Trustworthy AI (NIST AI RMF): valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, fair with harmful bias managed.
  • Keeping CC: US$50 a year and 45 CPE credits every three years.
