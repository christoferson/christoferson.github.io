5.4: Understand asset protection

Keeping track of every device, application and AI tool from purchase to disposal: knowing what you have, replacing what vendors no longer support, setting secure configurations, and controlling every change so nothing drifts out of a known, safe state.

Overview

Objective 5.4

Understand asset protection. Sub-topics: asset lifecycle management (including end-of-life software and devices) and configuration and change management.

Many breaches begin with something nobody was managing: a forgotten server, an unsupported router, a setting changed and never changed back. This objective covers the routine disciplines that prevent that. Protecting the data on these assets, including wiping media at disposal, is objective 5.1; identity accounts, which have their own life cycle, are objective 3.1.

The official exam outline is on isc2.org.

Running example. The mid-sized retailer: 800 staff, 60 US stores, an online shop with European customers, card payments, a cloud loyalty database and new AI tools. Each store has tills, back-office PCs, a router, cameras and a refrigeration controller; head office runs servers in two cloud accounts.

Assets and the life cycle

Objective 5.4Understanding what counts as an asset and the stages it passes through, with security built into each.

An asset is "an item of value to the achievement of organizational mission/business objectives", tangible or not. NIST CSF 2.0 lists assets as "data, hardware, software, systems, facilities, services, people". A system's life cycle is the "period that begins when a system is conceived and ends when the system is destroyed." NIST SP 800-37 names five phases:

  1. InitiationThe need is defined; security requirements and the risk level are set.
  2. Development and acquisitionThe system is built or bought; supported products and secure defaults are chosen.
  3. ImplementationIt is configured to the baseline, tested and recorded in the inventory before going live.
  4. Operation and maintenanceIt is patched, monitored and changed only through change control.
  5. DisposalIt is retired: data migrated or sanitized, accounts and licences removed, inventory updated.

CSF 2.0 expects that "systems, hardware, software, services, and data are managed throughout their life cycles" (ID.AM-08). Its examples include identifying redundant systems that "unnecessarily increase the organization's attack surface", updating inventories when assets move, and securely sanitizing storage when hardware is retired or sent for repair. Another example asks organizations to "identify unofficial uses of technology to meet mission objectives (i.e., shadow IT)": tools staff adopt without approval, which no one patches or reviews.

Inventory

Objective 5.4Keeping accurate inventories of hardware, software and services, and detecting anything that should not be there.

You cannot protect, patch or retire what you do not know about. CSF 2.0 starts with inventories of hardware (ID.AM-01), of "software, services, and systems" (ID.AM-02) and of services provided by suppliers (ID.AM-04), and suggests that organizations "constantly monitor networks to detect new hardware and automatically update inventories." NIST SP 800-53 control CM-8 System Component Inventory requires an inventory that "accurately reflects the system", includes all components (hardware, software and firmware), is updated whenever components are installed or removed (CM-8(1)), and is backed by automated detection of unauthorized components, which are then disabled, isolated or reported (CM-8(3)).

A useful inventory entry records more than a serial number. One of the retailer's records:

Inventory record: STORE-0417-RTR
Type
Branch router and firewall, store 0417
Model and version
Vendor model X200, firmware 7.4.2
Owner
Network team (custodian: managed service provider)
Exposure
Internet-facing
Support status
Vendor end of support: March 2027; replacement budgeted for Q4 2026
Baseline
Store router baseline v3; last compliance check passed

US federal agencies are required by CISA's BOD 23-01 to "perform automated asset discovery every 7 days" and scan discovered assets for vulnerabilities every 14 days. The same idea suits any organization: compare what a network scan finds with what the inventory says, and investigate the differences.

End-of-life software and devices

Objective 5.4Recognizing when products stop receiving security updates, planning their replacement, and protecting the ones that must stay a little longer.

Every product eventually reaches end of life (EOL) or end of support (EOS): the vendor stops fixing it. One major vendor's lifecycle policy spells out what that means: "there will be no new security updates, non-security updates, free or paid assisted support options or online technical content updates." New vulnerabilities in it will never be patched, and attackers know this. For example, Windows 10 reached end of support on October 14, 2025.

NIST SP 800-53 control SA-22 Unsupported System Components says to "replace system components when support for the components is no longer available from the developer, vendor, or manufacturer", or else arrange alternative support, in-house or from a third party. CSF 2.0 adds "replace end-of-life software and service versions with supported, maintained versions" and plan for hardware end of life (PR.PS-02 and PR.PS-03). CISA asks manufacturers to "clearly communicate the period of support for their products at the time of sale", which lets buyers plan; and in February 2026 it ordered federal agencies to inventory and replace Internet-facing edge devices that are past end of support (BOD 26-02).

When something cannot be replaced in time, such as specialist store equipment or industrial controllers, SA-22 suggests reducing the risk "by prohibiting the connection of such components to public or uncontrolled networks, or implementing other forms of isolation": segmentation, strict firewall rules, removing Internet access, extra monitoring, and a dated plan to replace it, with the risk formally accepted by its owner.

Extended support is a bridge, not a fix. Paid extended security updates (offered for Windows 10, for example) provide only critical and important security fixes, for a limited time (at most three years for organizations): no new features and no general technical support. Use the time to migrate. And retiring an asset is not finished until its data is sanitized (objective 5.1) and its accounts, licences and inventory entry are removed.

Configuration management

Objective 5.4Setting a secure baseline for each type of system from recognized hardening guides, and keeping systems matched to it.

NIST SP 800-128 defines configuration management as "a collection of activities focused on establishing and maintaining the integrity of products and systems, through control of the processes for initializing, changing, and monitoring the configurations of those products and systems throughout the system development life cycle." Key terms:

  • Baseline configuration: "a documented set of specifications for a system ... that has been formally reviewed and agreed on at a given point in time, and which can be changed only through change control procedures." It is the known good state every new build starts from.
  • Configuration item: "an identifiable part of a system (e.g., hardware, software, firmware, documentation, or a combination thereof)" that is placed under configuration control.
  • Hardening: "a process intended to eliminate a means of attack by patching vulnerabilities and turning off nonessential services."
  • Least functionality (CM-7): "configure the system to provide only ... mission essential capabilities", disabling unused ports, protocols, services and software. CM-7 also allows allowlisting (deny all, permit by exception) instead of blocklisting.

SP 800-128 organizes the work in four phases: planning; identifying and implementing configurations; controlling configuration changes; and monitoring, which looks for "undiscovered/undocumented system components, misconfigurations, vulnerabilities, and unauthorized changes." SP 800-53 adds keeping previous baselines "to support rollback" (CM-2(3)) and setting each system to "the most restrictive mode consistent with operational requirements" (CM-6), with documented, approved exceptions.

Hardening guides

Organizations rarely write baselines from scratch. NIST's National Checklist Program (SP 800-70 Rev. 5, May 2026) collects security configuration checklists, also called hardening guides, lockdown guides, STIGs or benchmarks, and says "a checklist should be considered a starting point" to be tailored. The main sources are NIST, the US Department of Defense's Security Technical Implementation Guides (STIGs), NSA and CISA guidance, vendors, and the Center for Internet Security's CIS Benchmarks, whose Level 1 is a base set that keeps machines usable and Level 2 adds defense in depth where security is paramount. Tools check compliance automatically using SCAP, now version 1.4 (SP 800-126 Rev. 4, June 2026). Default settings are a top risk: "default configurations of software and applications" head CISA and NSA's 2023 list of common misconfigurations.

The retailer keeps its till baseline as a file in version control, so every till is built and checked the same way:

Store till baseline (excerpt, YAML) 
baseline: store-till
version: 3.2               # changed only via change control
source: CIS Benchmark Level 1 (tailored)
settings:
  disk_encryption: required
  local_admin_accounts: none
  usb_storage: blocked
  remote_desktop: disabled
  services_allowed: [payment-client, edr-agent, ntp]
  auto_screen_lock_minutes: 5
exceptions:
  - id: EX-14
    setting: legacy_printer_driver
    approved_by: head-of-store-ops
    expires: 2027-03-31

Old references are still everywhere. NIST withdrew SP 800-70 Rev. 4 in May 2026 and the newer edition describes the US Government Configuration Baseline (USGCB) as retired. SCAP is now version 1.4, not 1.3. CIS Benchmarks no longer have a Level 3; a STIG profile replaced it.

Change management

Objective 5.4Running every change through request, impact analysis, testing, approval and verification, with a way back, and catching changes that skip the process.

Unmanaged changes break systems and weaken security. NIST SP 800-53 control CM-3 Configuration Change Control requires organizations to decide which changes are controlled, review and approve them "with explicit consideration for security and privacy impact analyses", document decisions, keep records, and "test, validate, and document changes to the system before finalizing the implementation" (CM-3(2)). Decisions usually sit with a configuration control board (often called a change advisory board), "a group of qualified people with responsibility for the process of regulating and approving changes." SP 800-128 describes the steps:

  1. Request and recordDescribe the change, the reason and its urgency.
  2. Analyse impact"Analyze changes to the system to determine potential security and privacy impacts prior to change implementation" (CM-4), including the impact of not making it.
  3. TestIn a separate test environment, with a back-out plan ready.
  4. ApproveThe board approves or rejects, with reasons.
  5. Implement and notifyIn the agreed window; documentation and baseline updated.
  6. Verify and closePost-implementation review confirms it was done as approved and works.

Not every change needs a meeting. SP 800-128 recognizes preapproved changes (routine ones such as vendor security patches and antivirus signature updates, similar to ITIL's "standard" changes), exempt activity that is outside configuration control altogether (such as creating user accounts or updating database content), and unscheduled or emergency changes, which "are still managed and controlled" but are reviewed by the board "as soon as is practical after" with retroactive analysis and testing.

Separation of duties applies (objective 3.2): SP 800-53 limits who can change production systems (CM-5(5)) and gives configuration management to people "not directly involved in system development" (CM-9(1)). Unauthorized changes are caught by integrity verification tools that "detect unauthorized changes to software, firmware, and information" (SI-7), such as file integrity monitoring with cryptographic hashes, and by regular baseline compliance scans that spot drift: systems gradually moving away from their baseline.

Patch management

Objective 5.4Treating patching as planned preventive maintenance, with routine and emergency plans and a response for assets that cannot be patched.

NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4 (2022) frames patching "as a critical component of preventive maintenance for computing technologies – a cost of doing business", like servicing vehicles. It points out that "by default, an organization accepts the risk posed by using its software", and lists four responses to a vulnerability: accept it, mitigate it (patch, disable the feature, or add controls), transfer it (for example by moving to a SaaS service where the provider patches), or avoid it (uninstall or retire the software). Patching is the only response that removes the vulnerability without removing functionality.

Assets are grouped, and each group has maintenance plans for four situations: routine patching, emergency patching (the same steps on a compressed timeline), emergency mitigation when no patch exists yet (to be replaced by a permanent fix later), and unpatchable assets (isolation and other controls, as for end-of-life kit). How quickly to fix depends on risk: CISA's 2026 directive for federal agencies sets the most urgent deadline at three days for exploited flaws on Internet-facing systems (objective 2.4). At the retailer, store tills get patches in a weekly overnight window after a pilot in three stores; an actively exploited flaw on the online shop is patched the same day as an emergency change.

AI tools from inventory to retirement

Objective 5.4Applying the asset life cycle and change control to AI tools and models: inventory, version control, validated changes and safe retirement.

The CC outline expects configuration and change management and the asset life cycle to cover AI tools up to end of life. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework asks for "mechanisms ... to inventory AI systems" (GOVERN 1.6) and "processes and procedures ... for decommissioning and phasing out AI systems safely and in a manner that does not increase risks or decrease the organization's trustworthiness" (GOVERN 1.7). Its Generative AI Profile suggests inventory entries include the "underlying foundation models, versions of underlying models, and access modes."

For changes, joint guidance led by NSA's AI Security Center ("Deploying AI Systems Securely", 2024) recommends:

  • storing code and artifacts, including "models, parameters, configurations, data, tests", in a version control system so "any changes are tracked";
  • using "cryptographic methods, digital signatures, and checksums to confirm each artifact's origin and integrity";
  • running a full evaluation of accuracy, performance and security before redeploying a new model version;
  • preparing "for automated rollbacks" and monitoring the model's configuration "for any unauthorized changes."

AI tools also reach end of life on someone else's schedule. Model providers publish deprecation timelines; one provider's documentation, for example, says requests to retired models fail and promises "at least 60 days' notice before model retirement." When retiring an AI tool, NIST AI 600-1 lists what to consider: data retention, "data leakage after decommissioning", and dependencies on other systems.

At the retailer, the chatbot's model version, system instructions and connected data sources are configuration items in version control. Any change goes through the change board with a test run against a fixed set of customer questions, and the previous version is kept for rollback. When the provider announced retirement of the model in use, the move to its replacement was scheduled as a normal change well before the date.

Further reading

  • NIST SP 800-128, Guide for Security-Focused Configuration Management of Information Systems (NIST, 2011, updated 2019): baselines, change control and monitoring.
  • NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4, Guide to Enterprise Patch Management Planning (NIST, 2022): patching as preventive maintenance.
  • NIST SP 800-70 Rev. 5, National Checklist Program for IT Products (NIST, May 2026): choosing and tailoring hardening checklists.
  • Product Security Bad Practices (CISA, 2025): including publishing product support periods.

Key takeaways

  • Life cycle (SP 800-37): initiation, development and acquisition, implementation, operation and maintenance, disposal. Build security into every phase; find and manage shadow IT.
  • Inventory hardware, software, services and suppliers' services (CSF ID.AM-01/02/04, SP 800-53 CM-8); update on every install and removal; detect unauthorized components automatically; record owner, version, exposure and support status.
  • End of life: no more patches. Replace unsupported components (SA-22) or arrange support; if they must stay, isolate them, monitor, set a replacement date and record the accepted risk. Extended support gives security fixes only, for a limited time.
  • Configuration management (SP 800-128): baselines changed only through change control; configuration items; hardening and least functionality; hardening guides from NIST's checklist program, STIGs and CIS Benchmarks; automated checks with SCAP; keep previous baselines for rollback.
  • Change management: request, impact analysis, test, approve, implement, verify (CM-3, CM-4). Preapproved, exempt and emergency changes; emergency changes are reviewed afterwards. Separation of duties; integrity monitoring (SI-7) and compliance scans catch unauthorized changes and drift.
  • Patching is preventive maintenance (SP 800-40 Rev. 4): accept, mitigate, transfer or avoid; plans for routine, emergency, mitigation-only and unpatchable cases; speed set by risk.
  • AI tools: inventory models and versions, keep models, prompts and settings under version control, verify artifacts with signatures and hashes, evaluate before redeploying, plan rollbacks, and retire safely before provider deadlines.
