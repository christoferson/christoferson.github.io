Task 2.2: Design highly available and/or fault-tolerant architectures

Keeping a workload running when parts of it fail, from one instance to a whole Region: redundancy across Availability Zones, failover between Regions, automation that rebuilds infrastructure the same way every time, metrics that show the business what availability it's getting, and a disaster recovery plan sized to the business's tolerance for loss.

Overview

Task 2.2

Design highly available and/or fault-tolerant architectures.

Two words in the title mean different things. High availability means the service stays usable when something fails, perhaps after a short failover. Fault tolerance means users don't notice the failure at all, because a redundant copy was already doing the work. Both cost money, so the exam expects you to match the design to the business's stated requirement: how long it may be down (RTO), how much data it may lose (RPO), and what failure it must survive (an instance, an AZ, or a Region). Task 2.1 made the system scale; this task makes it survive.

Running example. The company's order system runs in us-east-1 across three AZs: an ALB and Auto Scaling group for the web tier, ECS services for the APIs, Aurora PostgreSQL for orders and DynamoDB for carts. The business wants it to survive the loss of an AZ with no manual action, and the loss of the Region within an hour, losing at most a few seconds of orders. A warm standby runs in us-west-2.

Automation for infrastructure integrity

Skill 2.2.1Determining automation strategies to ensure infrastructure integrity

Infrastructure as code

Why it's a reliability control

An environment built by hand can't be rebuilt exactly, and one changed by hand drifts from what anyone knows about it. Defining infrastructure as code makes every environment reproducible: the DR Region gets the same stack as production, a broken environment is rebuilt rather than repaired, and every change is reviewed. AWS's DR whitepaper builds every strategy on it.

AWS CloudFormation

A CloudFormation stack creates and updates resources from a template; the AWS CDK lets you write the template in a programming language and synthesizes CloudFormation. Features that protect integrity:

  • Change sets show exactly what an update will add, modify or replace before it runs.
  • Rollback: a failed update returns the stack to its last working state. Rollback triggers also roll back if up to five CloudWatch alarms (metric or composite) go into ALARM during the update or a monitoring period of up to 180 minutes after it.
  • Drift detection reports resources changed outside CloudFormation (DRIFTED or IN_SYNC). It only detects; you fix the drift yourself.
  • StackSets deploy one template to many accounts and Regions in one operation, which keeps a DR Region identical to the primary.
  • Git sync updates a stack whenever its template changes in a Git repository.
Update the web stack and roll back automatically if the 5xx alarm fires within 10 minutes (AWS CLI) 
aws cloudformation update-stack --stack-name web-prod \
    --template-body file://web.yaml \
    --rollback-configuration "RollbackTriggers=[{Arn=arn:aws:cloudwatch:us-east-1:111111111111:alarm:web-5xx-high,Type=AWS::CloudWatch::Alarm}],MonitoringTimeInMinutes=10"

End of support. AWS Proton, a managed service for infrastructure templates, reached end of support on 7 October 2026 even though the exam guide still lists it. AWS suggests CloudFormation Git sync, CodePipeline or other pipelines instead.

Immutable infrastructure

Replace instead of patching in place

In an immutable model, servers are never changed after launch. To patch or update, you build a new image and replace the old servers with new ones. Every server then matches a known, tested image; a bad change is undone by going back to the previous image; and nothing drifts. The pieces on AWS:

  1. Build a golden imageEC2 Image Builder pipelines bake and test AMIs (or container images) and can copy them to every Region you use, including the DR Region. Amazon ECR replicates container images across Regions.
  2. Version the launch templateThe new AMI goes into a new launch template version.
  3. Roll it outAuto Scaling instance refresh replaces instances in batches while keeping a minimum healthy percentage, with checkpoints and rollback.
  4. Or switch trafficA blue/green deployment starts a complete new fleet and moves traffic to it, keeping the old one ready for an instant rollback.

Blue/green and canary deployments

AWS CodeDeploy runs in-place, blue/green and canary or linear deployments for EC2, Lambda and ECS. Since July 2025 Amazon ECS also has built-in blue/green deployments: both revisions run during a bake time behind an ALB, NLB or Service Connect, CloudWatch alarms and the deployment circuit breaker detect problems, and ECS rolls back automatically. Lambda aliases can shift a percentage of traffic to a new version. Route 53 weighted records or ALB weighted target groups can also shift traffic gradually.

Keeping running systems compliant

AWS Config rules flag resources that drift from the required configuration and can remediate automatically; Systems Manager State Manager keeps instances in a defined state, Patch Manager patches fleets on a schedule, and Automation runbooks perform repeatable operations. These cover what immutable images can't, such as long-lived stateful servers.

"Recreate the environment identically in another Region" points to CloudFormation StackSets or templates. "Detect manual changes to stack resources" points to drift detection. "Roll out a new AMI with no downtime" points to instance refresh or blue/green.

High availability across AZs and Regions

Skill 2.2.2Determining the AWS services required to provide a highly available and/or fault-tolerant architecture across AWS Regions or Availability Zones

Availability Zones and Regions

The building blocks of fault isolation

Each Region has at least three Availability Zones. An AZ has its own power, cooling and network connections and is physically separated from the others, far enough to avoid shared disasters such as floods, yet close enough (single-digit milliseconds) to replicate data synchronously. AWS's Reliability Pillar says to run every production workload in at least two AZs, and treats going multi-Region when multi-AZ would meet the requirement as an anti-pattern: multi-Region is for surviving a Region-wide event or meeting a regulation, and it costs much more.

Some services are multi-AZ automatically (S3, EFS Regional file systems, Aurora storage, DynamoDB, SQS, Kinesis); for RDS, ElastiCache and Redshift you must turn on Multi-AZ; for EBS and instance store you must handle it yourself. AZ names such as us-east-1a used to map to different physical AZs in each account; AZ IDs such as use1-az1 are the same in every account, and accounts created from November 2025 get the same name mapping too.

Static stability and the data plane

Two principles shape AWS's own answers. Static stability: provision enough capacity in each AZ in advance to carry the load if another AZ is lost, rather than planning to launch replacement capacity during the failure, when everyone else is trying to as well. Prefer the data plane: during recovery, rely on operations that keep working when management APIs are impaired (health-check-driven DNS failover, routing controls) rather than on control plane actions such as creating resources, scaling out or editing DNS records.

Each tier across AZs

Compute and networking

The web and API tiers run in an Auto Scaling group or ECS service spread over three AZs behind an ALB, which sends traffic only to healthy targets in every AZ. Basic networking matters here too: each AZ's private subnets need their own route table pointing at a NAT gateway in the same AZ (or a Regional NAT gateway), so losing one AZ doesn't cut off the others' outbound traffic. Application Recovery Controller zonal shift moves an ALB's, NLB's, Auto Scaling group's or EKS cluster's traffic away from an impaired AZ for up to three days at a time, and zonal autoshift lets AWS do it automatically when its own telemetry detects an AZ problem.

Aurora's storage survives an AZ loss even with one instance, but without a replica it recreates the primary, which typically takes under 10 minutes. Aurora promotes replicas in priority-tier order (0 first).

"Survive an AZ failure with automatic failover" points to Multi-AZ; "offload reads" points to read replicas (Task 2.1), or to a Multi-AZ DB cluster or Aurora when one design must do both.

Across Regions

  • Aurora Global Database: one primary Region and up to 10 secondary Regions, replicated at the storage layer with lag typically under a second, so RPO is measured in seconds. A planned switchover (formerly "managed planned failover") moves the primary with no data loss; an unplanned failover promotes a secondary and accepts the lag as data loss. A global writer endpoint follows the primary.
  • DynamoDB global tables: every Region's replica accepts reads and writes (multi-active), with asynchronous replication usually within a second and "last writer wins" for conflicts, and a 99.999% availability SLA. A newer multi-Region strong consistency mode replicates synchronously for an RPO of zero, using exactly three Regions (or two plus a witness).
  • ElastiCache: Multi-AZ with automatic failover inside a Region, and Global Datastore for read-only secondary clusters in other Regions.
  • S3 Cross-Region Replication and RDS cross-Region read replicas (Task 1.3) copy data for DR.
Planned move of the Aurora primary to us-west-2 with no data loss (AWS CLI) 
aws rds switchover-global-cluster \
    --global-cluster-identifier orders-global \
    --target-db-cluster-identifier arn:aws:rds:us-west-2:111111111111:cluster:orders-west

Routing users to a healthy Region

Route 53 routing policies

  • Failover: active-passive; answers with the primary while it's healthy, otherwise the secondary.
  • Latency: the Region with the lowest latency for the user, skipping unhealthy ones.
  • Weighted: split traffic by weight (0 to 255), for gradual moves or active-active.
  • Geolocation (by the user's location) and geoproximity (by the resources' location, with a bias to shift traffic).
  • Multivalue answer: up to eight healthy records chosen at random, a simple form of client-side load balancing.
  • IP-based (by the user's network) and simple (one resource, no health checks).

Alias records point a name, including the zone apex such as example.com, at AWS resources (load balancers, CloudFront, API Gateway, S3 websites, Global Accelerator); queries to them are free, and EvaluateTargetHealth makes the record follow the target's health.

Health checks

Route 53 health checks test an endpoint over HTTP, HTTPS or TCP from checkers in at least three Regions every 30 seconds (or 10, for a fee); an endpoint is healthy if more than 18% of checkers say so. Calculated health checks combine others (all, any, or at least n), and health checks can follow a CloudWatch alarm's data, which is how you check resources in private subnets that the checkers can't reach. Keep the TTL of failover records short (60 seconds or less); alias records manage TTL for you.

Global Accelerator and Application Recovery Controller

Global Accelerator (Task 2.1) fails over between Regional endpoints without DNS caching, because clients keep using the same two static IPs. Amazon Application Recovery Controller (ARC), formerly Route 53 ARC, adds routing controls (highly reliable on/off switches for manual failover that work through the data plane), and since August 2025 Region switch, which runs a whole multi-Region recovery plan (database promotion, scaling, DNS) across accounts, from a data plane in each Region. ARC readiness checks closed to new customers on 30 April 2026.

"Active-passive across Regions" points to Route 53 failover routing with health checks. "Lowest latency for global users, with failover" points to latency routing. "Static IPs and failover without DNS caching" points to Global Accelerator. "Multi-Region relational database with RPO in seconds" points to Aurora Global Database. "Multi-Region active-active writes" points to DynamoDB global tables.

Metrics for availability

Skill 2.2.3Identifying metrics based on business requirements to deliver a highly available solution

From business targets to numbers

Availability, RTO and RPO

Business requirements arrive as an availability target or as recovery objectives. Availability is the share of time the service works; each extra nine cuts the allowed downtime tenfold:

  • 99% allows about 3.65 days of downtime a year.
  • 99.9% allows about 8.76 hours a year (about 43.8 minutes a month).
  • 99.99% allows about 52.6 minutes a year (about 4.4 minutes a month).
  • 99.999% allows about 5.3 minutes a year.

Components in series multiply their availabilities (two 99.9% components in a chain give about 99.8%), while redundant copies in parallel raise it, which is why redundancy across AZs is the way to reach high targets. RTO (recovery time objective) is the longest acceptable time between an interruption and restored service; RPO (recovery point objective) is the longest acceptable time since the last recovery point, in other words how much data may be lost. AWS's service SLAs are a floor to design above, not a design: DynamoDB global tables, for example, carry a 99.999% SLA against 99.99% for single-Region tables.

Measuring it

Metrics that show availability

For a web tier behind an ALB, the useful CloudWatch metrics are HealthyHostCount and UnHealthyHostCount per target group (AWS suggests alarming when the minimum unhealthy count stays above zero), HTTPCode_ELB_5XX_Count (errors the load balancer itself returned) and HTTPCode_Target_5XX_Count (errors from the application), and TargetResponseTime at a percentile such as p99. Route 53 health checks publish their status too. CloudWatch Synthetics canaries run scripted user journeys on a schedule, so you see an outage before customers report it, and CloudWatch Application Signals collects request volume, latency, faults and errors per service and tracks service level objectives (SLOs), the measurable form of an availability target.

Alarms and composite alarms

A metric alarm watches one metric (for example, alarm when 3 of 5 one-minute periods breach) and can notify through SNS, invoke Lambda, scale an Auto Scaling group, or stop, reboot or recover an EC2 instance. A composite alarm combines other alarms with AND, OR and NOT, which cuts noise: page the on-call engineer only when 5xx errors and unhealthy hosts are both high, and not during a deployment. Composite alarms can notify and invoke Lambda but can't take EC2 or Auto Scaling actions.

Composite alarm: page only when both web alarms fire and no deployment is running (JSON input for aws cloudwatch put-composite-alarm) 
{
  "AlarmName": "web-tier-unhealthy",
  "AlarmRule": "ALARM(\"alb-5xx-high\") AND ALARM(\"alb-unhealthy-hosts\") AND NOT ALARM(\"deploy-in-progress\")",
  "AlarmActions": ["arn:aws:sns:us-east-1:111111111111:oncall"]
}

Workload visibility with tracing

When a request crosses many services, metrics show that something is slow but not where. AWS X-Ray collects traces of each request through API Gateway, Lambda, ECS and downstream calls and draws a service map showing which component adds latency or errors. Since 25 February 2026 the X-Ray SDKs and daemon are in maintenance mode, and AWS recommends instrumenting with OpenTelemetry (the AWS Distro for OpenTelemetry) and sending traces through the CloudWatch agent; the X-Ray service and its trace map continue.

Checking the design against the target

AWS Resilience Hub takes a resiliency policy (RTO and RPO targets for application, infrastructure, AZ and Region disruptions), assesses the application's architecture, reports whether each target is met, and recommends alarms, procedures and fault injection experiments. AWS Fault Injection Service runs those experiments, such as stopping instances or failing an AZ, to prove the design actually recovers.

"Detect failures before users report them" points to Synthetics canaries. "Find which microservice causes latency" points to X-Ray or Application Signals. "Reduce false alarms" points to composite alarms. "Verify the application meets its RTO and RPO" points to Resilience Hub.

Removing single points of failure

Skill 2.2.4Implementing designs to mitigate single points of failure

Find the one of anything

Redundancy layer by layer

A single point of failure is any component whose loss stops the workload: one instance, one NAT gateway, one AZ, a database without a standby, a single VPN tunnel, a self-managed DNS or file server. Go through the architecture tier by tier and give each at least two copies in different AZs, with automatic failover:

  • Entry: Route 53 and CloudFront are global services; the ALB spans several AZs.
  • Compute: an Auto Scaling group or ECS service across AZs, sized for static stability.
  • Outbound: a NAT gateway per AZ (or a Regional NAT gateway), not one shared NAT gateway.
  • Data: Multi-AZ RDS or Aurora, ElastiCache Multi-AZ, EFS rather than one server's disk.
  • Connections: two VPN tunnels, or Direct Connect with a VPN backup (Task 1.2).
  • Coupling: queues between components, so one failing consumer doesn't stop producers (Task 2.1).

Placement and recovery of instances

When a few critical instances must not share hardware, a spread placement group puts each on separate racks, up to seven running instances per AZ (and it can span AZs). A partition placement group groups instances into up to seven partitions per AZ with separate racks, for distributed systems such as HDFS, HBase and Cassandra. A cluster placement group does the opposite (one AZ, for low latency) and is never the high-availability choice. EC2 simplified automatic recovery, on by default for supported instances, restarts an instance on new hardware after a system status check failure, keeping its ID, IP addresses and EBS volumes (not RAM or instance store).

Patterns that keep failures small

Retries, timeouts and idempotency

Most failures in distributed systems are brief, so callers should retry with exponential backoff and jitter (random waits that grow with each attempt) so clients don't retry in lockstep. AWS SDKs do this by default in standard retry mode: three attempts in total, with full jitter and a 20-second cap. Retry at one layer only, because retries at every layer multiply (five layers of three attempts can create 243 times the load). Set timeouts so a slow dependency doesn't hold threads forever, and make operations idempotent (safe to repeat, often with a client token) so a retried request doesn't charge a card twice.

Isolation and graceful degradation

A circuit breaker stops calling a failing dependency for a while instead of piling up requests. Bulkheads, or cell-based architecture, split a workload into independent cells that each serve a subset of customers, so a failure affects only that cell; AWS's example is that with 10 cells, a failure in one leaves 90% of requests unaffected. Graceful degradation keeps the core working when an optional part fails, such as showing products without personalized recommendations. Health checks should be shallow for load balancers (is this host working?), because a deep check that tests a shared database fails on every host at once; ALBs, NLBs, Route 53 and Global Accelerator fail open and send traffic to all targets when all of them look unhealthy.

Service quotas and throttling

A limit is also a single point of failure

Quotas apply per account and per Region. A recovery plan that needs 200 instances in us-west-2 fails if that Region's quota is 50, so AWS's DR guidance is to raise quotas in the recovery Region in advance to match production. Service Quotas shows current values and usage, can alarm when usage nears a quota, and in AWS Organizations a quota request template requests increases (up to 10 quotas) automatically for every new account. Throttling (HTTP 429 or ThrottlingException) is a quota working as designed: API Gateway's account limit is 10,000 requests per second with a 5,000 burst per Region by default (2,500 and 1,250 in many newer Regions), and Lambda's is 1,000 concurrent executions per Region. Handle throttling with backoff, queues and caching, and ask for increases before launches.

"The DR Region couldn't launch enough instances during the failover test" is a service quota problem, not a capacity or AMI problem. Raise quotas in the standby Region ahead of time.

Durability and availability of data

Skill 2.2.5Implementing strategies to ensure the durability and availability of data (for example, backups)

Storage characteristics

Durability is not availability

Durability is the chance that stored data isn't lost; availability is the chance you can reach it right now. Every S3 storage class is designed for 99.999999999% (eleven nines) durability, but availability varies: S3 Standard is designed for 99.99% across at least three AZs, while S3 One Zone-IA (99.5%) and S3 Express One Zone (99.95%) keep data in a single AZ, so losing that AZ can lose the data. EBS gp2, gp3, io1, st1 and sc1 volumes are designed for 99.8% to 99.9% durability, and io2 Block Express for 99.999%; but every EBS volume lives in one AZ, so snapshots (stored Regionally) are what let you recover in another AZ. Instance store data is lost when the instance stops or its hardware fails.

S3 One Zone-IA is fine for data you can recreate, such as thumbnails or a second copy of backups, but not for the only copy of important data, and AWS doesn't consider it statically stable.

Replication and faster recovery

Keeping data available through failures

  • Amazon EFS: Regional file systems store data across multiple AZs; EFS replication copies a file system to another Region or account with an RPO of 15 minutes for most file systems.
  • Amazon FSx: Multi-AZ FSx for Windows File Server fails over in under 30 seconds (a Single-AZ file system can be down for about 30 minutes while it recovers); Multi-AZ FSx for NetApp ONTAP fails over in under 60 seconds.
  • Databases: Multi-AZ inside a Region, Aurora Global Database, DynamoDB global tables or cross-Region replicas across Regions (Skill 2.2.2).
  • Objects: S3 replication, versioning and Object Lock (Task 1.3).

Backups and restore time

Replication copies corruption and deletions as faithfully as good data, so backups are still needed: AWS Backup plans with cross-Region and cross-account copies, point-in-time recovery for RDS, Aurora and DynamoDB, and locked vaults, all covered in Task 1.3. What matters here is how fast a backup becomes usable, because restore time is part of RTO. A volume restored from an EBS snapshot loads its data lazily and is slow on first access; EBS fast snapshot restore makes volumes fully initialized at creation, enabled per snapshot and AZ (up to five snapshots per Region, charged per hour). Large database restores can take hours, which is why tight RTOs need a standby rather than a backup.

"Restore EBS volumes from snapshots with full performance immediately" points to fast snapshot restore. "Shared files must survive an AZ failure" points to EFS (Regional) or FSx Multi-AZ, not an EBS volume on one instance.

Choosing a DR strategy

Skill 2.2.6Selecting an appropriate DR strategy to meet business requirements

The four strategies

From cheap and slow to costly and instant

AWS's whitepaper Disaster Recovery of Workloads on AWS describes four strategies for recovering in another Region, in order of rising cost and complexity and falling RPO and RTO. A workload that's already well architected across AZs may need only backup and restore for the loss of an AZ; the other three are for losing a Region, or for regulations that require it.

What each needs on AWS

Backup and restore needs backups copied to the DR Region (AWS Backup copy actions, S3 replication), golden AMIs there, and infrastructure as code to rebuild everything; restores should be automated and tested, because restoring is a control plane operation. Pilot light adds continuous replication (Aurora Global Database, DynamoDB global tables, RDS cross-Region read replicas, S3 replication) so the data is always current. Warm standby adds a small running copy of every tier, so recovery is scaling out and moving traffic; a hot standby runs at full size so even scaling isn't needed. Multi-site active/active serves users from several Regions at once, with a write strategy such as one write Region (Aurora Global Database with write forwarding), writes in every Region (DynamoDB global tables), or data partitioned by customer.

Making failover work

  • Prefer data plane failover: Route 53 health-check failover or ARC routing controls, rather than editing records or launching resources under pressure.
  • Raise service quotas in the DR Region to production levels beforehand (Skill 2.2.4).
  • Automate the steps, but AWS notes that many teams start failover manually, because automatic failover on a false alarm can cause an outage of its own.
  • Use a separate account per Region for the strongest isolation.
  • Test regularly with game days and Fault Injection Service; an untested plan is a guess.

AWS Elastic Disaster Recovery

AWS Elastic Disaster Recovery (AWS DRS) continuously replicates servers, from on premises (physical, VMware, Hyper-V), other clouds, or EC2 in another AZ or Region, at the block level into a low-cost staging area in AWS. On recovery it launches full-size instances from the replicated data, for RPOs of seconds and RTOs of minutes, can recover to an earlier point in time (for ransomware), supports non-disruptive drills, and fails back afterwards. It's a pilot light for whole servers, and needs no application changes. It replaced CloudEndure Disaster Recovery.

Read the RPO and RTO in the question and pick the cheapest strategy that meets them. "Hours are acceptable, lowest cost" points to backup and restore. "Core data must be current, servers can start on demand" points to pilot light. "Minutes, and must take some traffic immediately" points to warm standby. "Near-zero downtime, users in several Regions" points to multi-site active/active. "Replicate on-premises servers to AWS for DR" points to Elastic Disaster Recovery.

Reliability for legacy applications

Skill 2.2.7Using AWS services that improve the reliability of legacy applications and applications not built for the cloud (for example, when application changes are not possible)

Wrap the application instead of changing it

When the code can't change

Some applications keep state on the server, use hard-coded IP addresses, open a new database connection per request, or simply can't be modified (a vendor product, lost source code). The exam then asks for services that add reliability around the application:

  • Self-healing single instance: an Auto Scaling group with minimum, maximum and desired capacity of 1 across several AZ subnets, ideally behind an ALB with ELB health checks, replaces a failed instance in a healthy AZ automatically. EC2 automatic recovery restarts it on new hardware, keeping its IP addresses.
  • Clients with hard-coded IPs: an Elastic IP can be moved to a replacement instance, and Global Accelerator gives fixed anycast IPs in front of load balancers in one or several Regions.
  • DNS-level failover: Route 53 failover records with health checks need nothing from the application.
  • Shared state on disk: move files to EFS or FSx Multi-AZ so a replacement server sees the same data; move sessions to ElastiCache.
  • Messaging with standard protocols: Amazon MQ active/standby brokers across two AZs, with clients using the broker's failover connection string.
  • Whole-server DR: Elastic Disaster Recovery, with no code change.
  • AZ impairment: ARC zonal shift on the load balancer or Auto Scaling group.

Proxies: Amazon RDS Proxy

Connection pooling and faster failover

A proxy sits between clients and a server and manages connections on their behalf. Amazon RDS Proxy keeps a pool of database connections and shares them among many clients, which protects the database from connection storms (typical with Lambda, which opens connections from thousands of short-lived environments) and queues requests it can't serve immediately. During a failover it keeps the application's connections open and reconnects to the new primary itself, bypassing DNS caching, which AWS says cuts failover time by up to 66% for Aurora Multi-AZ. Most applications need no code change beyond pointing at the proxy endpoint. It can require IAM authentication from clients and read database credentials from Secrets Manager.

Limits to remember: RDS Proxy supports RDS for MySQL, MariaDB, PostgreSQL and SQL Server and Aurora MySQL and PostgreSQL, but not Oracle or Db2; it is never publicly accessible (it lives in the database's VPC); and it targets the writer, with optional read-only endpoints for Aurora Replicas.

Create a proxy for the orders database, using credentials from Secrets Manager and requiring IAM authentication and TLS (AWS CLI) 
aws rds create-db-proxy \

"Lambda functions exhaust database connections" or "reduce database failover time without changing the application" points to RDS Proxy. "A legacy app on one EC2 instance must recover automatically from an AZ failure" points to an Auto Scaling group of one across AZs.

Purpose-built services

Skill 2.2.8Using purpose-built AWS services for workloads

Managed AI services instead of your own models

Reliability you don't have to build

A model you train and host yourself is one more system to keep available. AWS's managed AI services are Regional, multi-AZ and scaled by AWS, called through an API with no machine learning expertise needed. The guide names two:

  • Amazon Comprehend: natural language processing on text, detecting sentiment, entities, key phrases, language and personally identifiable information (PII), with custom classifiers and entity recognizers. Comprehend Medical extracts medical entities and protected health information from clinical text.
  • Amazon Polly: text-to-speech, with standard, neural, long-form and generative voices.

Their siblings: Amazon Transcribe (speech to text), Amazon Translate (translation), Amazon Rekognition (image and video analysis, faces, unsafe content, face liveness), Amazon Textract (text, forms and tables from documents), Amazon Lex (chatbots, now with multi-Region replication of bots), Amazon Personalize (recommendations) and Amazon Bedrock (foundation models).

"Analyze customer reviews for sentiment" or "detect PII in text" points to Comprehend. "Read articles aloud" points to Polly. "Call-center recordings to text" points to Transcribe. "Extract fields from scanned forms" points to Textract.

Closed or ended. Several AI services the exam may mention are no longer for new designs: Amazon Kendra entered maintenance mode on 30 June 2026 and closed to new customers on 30 July 2026 (AWS points to Amazon Bedrock knowledge bases); Amazon Fraud Detector closed to new customers on 7 November 2025; Amazon Forecast is no longer available to new customers (SageMaker Canvas is the suggested path); and Amazon Lookout for Equipment reached end of support on 7 October 2026.

Key takeaways

  • Automation: CloudFormation (change sets, rollback triggers, drift detection, StackSets), golden AMIs from Image Builder copied to every Region, instance refresh and blue/green (CodeDeploy, built-in ECS blue/green since July 2025). Replace, don't patch. Proton has ended.
  • Run production in at least two AZs; design for static stability and prefer data plane failover. Multi-AZ RDS instance: one non-readable standby, 60 to 120 s failover. Multi-AZ DB cluster: two readable standbys, under 35 s. Aurora: six storage copies in three AZs, up to 15 replicas, under 60 s.
  • Across Regions: Aurora Global Database (up to 10 secondaries, switchover with no loss, RPO in seconds), DynamoDB global tables (multi-active, 99.999% SLA), Route 53 failover, latency and weighted routing with health checks, Global Accelerator, ARC routing controls and Region switch.
  • Metrics: 99.9% is about 8.8 hours a year, 99.99% about 53 minutes. Watch HealthyHostCount, UnHealthyHostCount, 5xx counts and p99 latency; use Synthetics canaries, Application Signals SLOs and composite alarms; trace with X-Ray (SDKs now in maintenance; use OpenTelemetry).
  • Single points of failure: one NAT gateway, one instance, one AZ, one tunnel. Use spread placement groups (7 per AZ), retries with backoff and jitter, timeouts, idempotency, cells and shallow health checks. Raise quotas in the DR Region in advance.
  • Data: S3 is eleven nines durable, but One Zone classes can be lost with their AZ; EBS is single-AZ, so snapshots and fast snapshot restore; EFS replication RPO 15 minutes; FSx Multi-AZ fails over in under 30 to 60 s. Replication isn't backup.
  • DR: backup and restore (cheapest, hours), pilot light (data live, servers off), warm standby (small running copy, minutes), multi-site active/active (near zero, most expensive). Elastic Disaster Recovery gives seconds of RPO and minutes of RTO for whole servers.
  • Legacy apps: an Auto Scaling group of one across AZs, Elastic IPs or Global Accelerator for fixed IPs, Route 53 failover, EFS or ElastiCache for state, and RDS Proxy for pooling and up to 66% faster Aurora failover (not Oracle or Db2, never public). Use managed AI services such as Comprehend and Polly; avoid closed ones such as Kendra and Forecast.
