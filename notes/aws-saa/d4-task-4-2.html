Task 4.2: Design cost-optimized compute solutions

Paying the least for the compute a workload needs: buying capacity the right way (commitments, Spot or on demand), switching it off or hibernating it when idle, choosing between EC2, containers and Lambda by price, giving each environment only the availability it needs, and picking the instance family and size that fit.

Overview

Task 4.2

Design cost-optimized compute solutions.

Compute is usually the largest line on an AWS bill, and the biggest savings come from three decisions: how you pay (a commitment can cut the price by up to 72%, Spot by up to 90%), how much runs when nobody is using it (development environments idle every night and weekend), and whether the service and instance fit the workload (an idle server costs the same as a busy one). Task 3.2 chose compute for performance; this task chooses it for price. Cost tools such as Cost Explorer and Budgets were covered in Task 4.1.

Prices are us-east-1 on-demand rates for Linux, checked in November 2026; learn the relative order and rules rather than the cents.

Running example. The company's production web tier and APIs run around the clock with a predictable base load and evening peaks; nightly risk simulations and image processing can be interrupted; development and test environments are used only on weekdays; and a recommendation service has a slow start-up that loads a large model into memory.

Savings Plans

A Savings Plan commits to a dollar amount per hour (for example US$10 per hour) for 1 or 3 years, paid all, partially or not upfront; usage up to that amount gets the plan's rate and anything above is on demand. An hour's unused commitment is lost. Compute Savings Plans apply to EC2 in any family, size, Region and OS, and to Fargate and Lambda; EC2 Instance Savings Plans give a deeper discount for one family in one Region; SageMaker AI Savings Plans cover SageMaker; and Database Savings Plans (December 2025, up to 35%, 1-year No Upfront only) cover RDS, Aurora, DynamoDB and other database services (Task 4.3). Plans can't be cancelled, but a small one (US$100 per hour or less) can be returned within 7 days in the same month. AWS's EC2 guidance is to prefer Savings Plans over Reserved Instances, and Cost Explorer's Savings Plans recommendations size the commitment from your past usage.

Reserved Instances

A Reserved Instance is a billing discount for a matching instance type, Region, tenancy and platform, not a physical instance. Regional RIs apply in any AZ and, for Linux with default tenancy, to any size in the family (size flexibility); zonal RIs also reserve capacity in one AZ but apply only to the exact size. Only Standard RIs can be sold on the RI Marketplace; Convertible RIs can instead be exchanged for other configurations. In a consolidated bill, RIs apply before Savings Plans.

Spot Instances

Spot uses spare capacity at prices set by long-term supply and demand (an m7i.large was about 62% below on-demand in a November 2026 snapshot). EC2 can interrupt with a two-minute notice; if it does so in the first hour you pay nothing. There's no bidding: AWS recommends setting no maximum price. Design for interruption as in Task 3.2, with many instance types and AZs and the price-capacity-optimized strategy. Spot isn't covered by Savings Plans or RIs.

Dedicated capacity and reservations

  • Dedicated Hosts: a whole physical server billed per host, with visible sockets and cores for licences that require them (full bring-your-own-licence).
  • Dedicated Instances: instances on hardware dedicated to your account, billed per instance plus a US$2 per hour per-Region fee; limited licence portability.
  • On-Demand Capacity Reservations: guaranteed capacity in an AZ, billed at the on-demand rate whether used or not, with no discount of their own; Savings Plans and Regional RIs reduce the price.
  • Capacity Blocks for ML: GPU or Trainium capacity reserved for a future period, paid upfront.

Combining them

AWS's Well-Architected guidance is to combine options: cover the steady baseline with Savings Plans, run fault-tolerant extra capacity on Spot, and leave the unpredictable remainder on demand. In AWS Organizations, Savings Plans and RI discounts are shared across accounts by default (the management account can turn sharing off per account), and AWS suggests buying commitments in an account with no workloads, such as the management account. It advises against targeting a coverage percentage; track the recommended savings instead.

"Steady 24/7 workload, flexibility to change instance family or Region, or move to Fargate or Lambda" points to a Compute Savings Plan. "Steady workload staying on one family in one Region, maximum discount" points to an EC2 Instance Savings Plan (or Standard RI). "Interruptible batch processing, lowest cost" points to Spot. "Software licensed per physical core" points to Dedicated Hosts. "Guarantee capacity in an AZ for an event" points to an On-Demand Capacity Reservation.

A Capacity Reservation is not a discount, and a Regional Reserved Instance or a Savings Plan is not a capacity guarantee. When a question needs both, combine a Capacity Reservation with a Savings Plan (or use a zonal RI).

Load balancing for cost

Skill 4.2.1Determining an appropriate load balancing strategy (for example, Application Load Balancer [Layer 7] compared with Network Load Balancer [Layer 4] compared with Gateway Load Balancer)

What load balancers cost

Hourly plus capacity units

  • Application Load Balancer: US$0.0225 per hour plus US$0.008 per LCU-hour. An LCU is the highest of: 25 new connections per second, 3,000 active connections per minute, 1 GB processed per hour (0.4 GB for Lambda targets), or 1,000 rule evaluations per second (the first 10 rules are free).
  • Network Load Balancer: US$0.0225 per hour plus US$0.006 per NLCU-hour (for TCP, 800 new flows per second, 100,000 active flows or 1 GB per hour).
  • Gateway Load Balancer: US$0.0125 per hour per AZ plus US$0.004 per GLCU-hour, with its endpoints billed separately.
  • Classic Load Balancer: US$0.025 per hour plus US$0.008 per GB processed; previous generation.

Two extra costs are easy to miss: every public IPv4 address an internet-facing load balancer uses costs US$0.005 per hour (an ALB can run dual-stack without public IPv4; NLBs can't), and turning on cross-zone load balancing for an NLB adds inter-AZ data transfer charges. LCU reservations (Task 3.4) pre-warm capacity at the normal LCU price and bill even when unused; they're not a discount.

Choosing for cost

Share one ALB, pick the layer you need

  • Consolidate: one ALB with path- or host-based rules can front many microservices, paying one hourly charge instead of one per service (ECS Express Mode puts up to 25 services behind one ALB).
  • Match the layer: use an NLB for plain TCP, UDP or static IPs, where its capacity units are cheaper per flow; use an ALB when layer-7 routing, authentication or WAF are needed; use a GWLB only for appliance inspection.
  • Skip it when possible: a single Lambda function can use a function URL, and small internal tools can use Route 53 or service discovery rather than a dedicated load balancer.
  • Delete idle ones: Trusted Advisor's idle load balancer check covers only Classic Load Balancers, so review CloudWatch request counts for ALBs and NLBs.

"Many microservices, each with its own load balancer, reduce cost" points to one ALB with path-based routing. "Static IP, TCP traffic, lowest cost" points to an NLB.

Scaling methods for cost

Skill 4.2.2Determining appropriate scaling methods and strategies for elastic workloads (for example, horizontal compared with vertical, EC2 hibernation)

Pay only while there's demand

Horizontal versus vertical, for cost

Horizontal scaling lets capacity follow demand in small steps, so you pay for the peak only while it lasts; vertical scaling means paying for the largest size all day, and changing size needs a stop and start. Instance prices double with each size step (an m7i.large is US$0.1008 per hour, an xlarge US$0.2016), so two small instances cost the same as one twice their size, and scaling out gives the same capacity with the ability to remove it. AWS's Well-Architected guidance distinguishes demand-based supply (target tracking, step scaling) from time-based supply (scheduled and predictive scaling), and recommends both.

Turning things off

A development environment used 50 hours a week (weekdays, 10 hours a day) instead of 168 costs about 70% less. Ways to do it:

  • Scheduled scaling on Auto Scaling groups: minimum and desired capacity of 0 outside working hours.
  • Instance Scheduler on AWS, an AWS solution that starts and stops tagged EC2 instances, Auto Scaling groups and RDS databases on schedules, across accounts.
  • Systems Manager Resource Scheduler (Quick Setup) for simple start-and-stop schedules.
  • Infrastructure as code to create whole environments when needed and delete them afterwards.
  • Services that scale to zero by themselves: Lambda, Fargate tasks scaled to zero, Aurora Serverless with auto-pause.

EC2 hibernation

Stop without losing what's in memory

Hibernation saves the instance's RAM to its EBS root volume and stops it; on start, the instance resumes with its memory, processes and caches as they were, and keeps its instance ID, private IP addresses and Elastic IPs. While hibernated you pay only for EBS storage (including the memory image) and Elastic IPs, not for the instance. Unlike a normal stop, you are billed while the instance is in the stopping state saving its memory. It's the cost answer for workloads with long start-up or warm-up, such as the recommendation service loading its model: launch, warm up, hibernate, and resume in place of a full boot.

  • Hibernation must be enabled at launch and can't be turned on later.
  • The root volume must be an encrypted EBS volume large enough for the RAM.
  • RAM must be under 150 GiB for Linux, or at most 16 GiB for Windows; bare metal instances aren't supported.
  • An instance can't stay hibernated for more than 60 days.
  • It works for On-Demand and Spot Instances (Spot with a persistent request).
  • Instances in an Auto Scaling group can't be hibernated directly.
Launch the recommendation service with hibernation enabled, then hibernate it after warm-up (AWS CLI; mapping.json sets an encrypted gp3 root volume) 
aws ec2 run-instances --image-id ami-0recsvc1111111111 --instance-type r7g.xlarge \
    --block-device-mappings file://mapping.json \
    --hibernation-options Configured=true

aws ec2 stop-instances --instance-ids i-0recsvc1111111111 --hibernate

Warm pools: hibernation for Auto Scaling groups

For an Auto Scaling group, a warm pool keeps pre-initialized instances in a Stopped or Hibernated state, paying only for their EBS volumes and Elastic IPs, so scale-out takes seconds instead of minutes. A Running warm pool is possible but AWS discourages it for cost. Warm pools work with mixed-instance groups for On-Demand capacity, but not with Spot.

Keep hibernated, pre-warmed instances ready for the recommendation service's Auto Scaling group (AWS CLI) 
aws autoscaling put-warm-pool --auto-scaling-group-name recsvc-asg \
    --pool-state Hibernated --min-size 2 \
    --instance-reuse-policy '{"ReuseOnScaleIn": true}'

"Long application start-up, keep in-memory state, lowest cost while idle" points to EC2 hibernation (or a hibernated warm pool for an Auto Scaling group). "Development instances idle nights and weekends" points to scheduled scaling or Instance Scheduler.

Hibernation can't be enabled on an existing instance; it must be chosen at launch with an encrypted root volume. And stopping or hibernating an instance doesn't stop EBS or Elastic IP charges.

Cost-effective compute services

Skill 4.2.3Determining cost-effective AWS compute services with appropriate use cases (for example, AWS Lambda, Amazon EC2, AWS Fargate)

How each service charges

Lambda

US$0.20 per million requests plus US$0.0000166667 per GB-second of duration on x86 (20% less on Arm, about US$0.0000133 per GB-second), billed per millisecond. The free tier gives 1 million requests and 400,000 GB-seconds a month. Idle costs nothing. Provisioned concurrency adds a charge for keeping environments warm. Lambda Managed Instances (November 2025) run functions on EC2 in your account for the EC2 price plus a 15% management fee, with no per-duration charge, which suits steady, high-volume traffic but doesn't scale to zero.

Fargate, ECS, EKS and EC2

Fargate charges per second (1-minute minimum) for the vCPU and memory a task requests: US$0.04048 per vCPU-hour and US$0.004445 per GB-hour on Linux x86, 20% less on Arm, and up to 70% less with Fargate Spot (ECS only). ECS itself is free, so ECS on EC2 costs only the instances; EKS adds US$0.10 per cluster-hour (US$0.60 once a Kubernetes version is in extended support). ECS Managed Instances and EKS Auto Mode add a management fee of roughly 12% on top of EC2. EC2 is billed per second while running. AWS Batch has no extra charge, and Lightsail offers fixed monthly bundles (from US$5) for simple servers. AWS App Runner is closed to new customers; ECS Express Mode, which has no extra charge, is the suggested path.

Compute Savings Plans discount EC2, Fargate and Lambda, but not the EKS cluster fee or management fees.

An illustrative comparison

An API handles 20 requests per second around the clock, each taking 200 ms with 512 MB. On Lambda that's about 52 million requests and 5.2 million GB-seconds a month: roughly US$97 (US$10 for requests and US$86 for duration), before the free tier. Two always-on Fargate tasks of 0.5 vCPU and 1 GB cost about US$36 a month. If the same API averaged 1 request per second with bursts, Lambda would cost about US$5 a month, while the Fargate tasks would still cost US$36. The rule that follows: steady, high utilization favours provisioned compute (EC2 or Fargate, with Savings Plans); spiky, low or unpredictable traffic favours Lambda.

"Infrequent or unpredictable requests, pay nothing when idle" points to Lambda. "Containers without managing servers" points to Fargate (with Fargate Spot for interruptible tasks). "Steady 24/7 load, lowest cost" points to EC2 or Fargate with a Savings Plan. "Batch jobs at the lowest cost" points to AWS Batch on Spot.

Where compute runs affects the price

Regions, the edge and hybrid

Prices differ by Region: an m7i.large is US$0.1008 per hour in us-east-1, Ohio and Oregon, about 17% more in N. California and about 59% more in São Paulo, and Local Zones cost roughly 20% to 25% more than their parent Region. AWS's Well-Architected guidance lists Region choice as a cost lever where latency and data residency allow. Moving data also costs money: US$0.01 per GB in each direction between AZs, and more between Regions (Task 4.4).

At the edge, CloudFront Functions cost US$0.10 per million invocations (2 million free a month) against Lambda@Edge at US$0.60 per million plus duration with no free tier, so simple header and URL logic belongs in CloudFront Functions. Hybrid AWS Outposts racks and servers are bought for a 3-year term (all, partial or no upfront) and need Enterprise Support, so they're for workloads that must run on premises, not a way to save money.

Availability by workload class

Skill 4.2.4Determining the required availability for different classes of workloads (for example, production workloads, non-production workloads)

Availability costs money

Identify the real need first

AWS's Reliability Pillar notes that designing for higher availability typically costs more, so the true need should be identified first, and its Cost Optimization Pillar describes a fully cost-optimized workload as the one most aligned to the organization's requirements, not necessarily the cheapest. Each extra nine of availability (Task 2.2) typically means more redundancy, more AZs or Regions, more data transfer and more operations.

Production versus non-production

  • Production: at least two AZs, capacity to survive the loss of one AZ, Multi-AZ databases, health checks and automatic recovery. Pay for the baseline with Savings Plans, add Spot for stateless extra capacity, and keep on demand for the rest.
  • Staging and pre-production: often the same design at a smaller size, perhaps in one AZ, running only during test cycles; on demand if it runs too briefly for a commitment.
  • Development and test: a single AZ, smaller or burstable instances, Spot, single-AZ databases, and schedules that switch everything off outside working hours. AWS's guidance names test and development as natural Spot workloads, and suggests shared resources for less critical workloads.
  • Batch and analytics: interruptions are acceptable when work can be retried, so Spot and AWS Batch fit; availability matters less than completing within a window.

The hidden cost of Multi-AZ

Traffic between AZs costs US$0.01 per GB in each direction, so a chatty application spread across AZs pays for every request between tiers; traffic within one AZ is free, but traffic sent to a public or Elastic IP address costs US$0.01 per GB even inside a Region. Keep high-volume component pairs in the same AZ where availability allows, and use private IPs.

"Reduce costs of development and test environments" points to single-AZ, smaller instances, Spot and schedules. "Production must survive an AZ failure" keeps Multi-AZ, with savings coming from Savings Plans and right-sizing instead.

Applying production's Multi-AZ, always-on design to every environment is the most common way to overspend. Equally, dropping Multi-AZ from production to save money fails the availability requirement; the exam expects you to keep the requirement and save elsewhere.

Choosing an instance family

Skill 4.2.5Selecting the appropriate instance family for a workload

Pay for the resource you actually use

Match the shape

Each family has a different ratio of vCPU to memory (Task 3.2): compute optimized (C) gives more CPU per dollar, memory optimized (R, X) more memory per dollar, general purpose (M) a balance. A memory-bound cache on a C instance pays for CPU it doesn't use, and has to be sized up to get enough memory. In November 2026 a large size cost US$0.08925 per hour for c7i, US$0.1008 for m7i and US$0.1323 for r7i, each with 2 vCPU but 4, 8 and 16 GiB of memory respectively.

Processor and generation

  • Graviton (Arm) instances cost up to 20% less than comparable x86 instances (m7g.large US$0.0816 against m7i.large US$0.1008), for Linux workloads that run on Arm.
  • AMD: the 6th-generation AMD families (M6a, C6a, R6a) and T3a are 10% cheaper than their Intel equivalents, but that isn't universal (m7a.large costs more than m7i.large), so compare actual prices.
  • Burstable T instances suit low-to-moderate average CPU and can cost less than an M instance; T4g is the lowest-cost instance type. In unlimited mode, sustained high CPU adds surplus-credit charges.
  • Flex instances (M7i-flex, C7i-flex) cost about 5% less than the standard versions for workloads that don't use full CPU all the time.
  • Newer generations usually give better price performance; previous generations stay available (and can be good Spot candidates because they're less in demand). Current instances use HVM virtualization on the Nitro System; paravirtual (PV) is limited to old families.

"Reduce cost of a Linux fleet without changing instance size" points to Graviton. "Low average CPU with occasional spikes" points to T instances. "Memory-heavy workload on an oversized compute instance" points to an R family instance of a smaller size.

Choosing an instance size

Skill 4.2.6Selecting the appropriate instance size for a workload

Right-sizing

Size from measurements

Within a family, each size step doubles vCPU, memory and price, so one step too large doubles the cost. AWS's guidance is to select type, size and number from data, and to automate it where the savings justify the effort. AWS Compute Optimizer analyses 14 days of CloudWatch metrics (up to 93 days with paid enhanced infrastructure metrics) and recommends instance types and sizes for EC2 and Auto Scaling groups, memory for Lambda and task sizes for Fargate; memory-based EC2 recommendations need the CloudWatch agent. Cost Optimization Hub collects these with idle-resource and Savings Plans recommendations, and Trusted Advisor's low-utilization checks (full checks need Business Support+ or Enterprise Support) flag underused instances.

Sizes and commitments

Regional Linux RIs and EC2 Instance Savings Plans apply across sizes in a family. RI size flexibility uses normalization factors (small 1, medium 2, large 4, xlarge 8, 2xlarge 16, doubling onwards), so an RI for one xlarge covers two larges or half of a 2xlarge. That means right-sizing within a family doesn't waste an existing commitment, while moving to a different family needs a Compute Savings Plan or a Convertible RI exchange.

Small and many, or large and few

For horizontally scaled tiers, more small instances follow demand more closely and lose less capacity per failure; larger instances suit workloads that can't be split, or software licensed per instance. Size Fargate tasks and Lambda memory the same way: from measured use, not from the peak.

"Instances average 10% CPU" points to right-sizing to a smaller size (or burstable instances), guided by Compute Optimizer. "Right-size without losing the Reserved Instance discount" points to staying within the family (size flexibility) or using a Compute Savings Plan.

Key takeaways

  • Purchasing: On-Demand (per second, 60-second minimum), Compute Savings Plans (up to 66%, any EC2, Fargate, Lambda), EC2 Instance Savings Plans and Standard RIs (up to 72%, one family and Region), Convertible RIs (66%, exchangeable), Spot (up to 90%, 2-minute notice, no max price needed). Combine: commitments for the baseline, Spot for flexible extra, on demand for the rest.
  • Capacity Reservations guarantee capacity at the on-demand price (no discount); zonal RIs reserve capacity too; Savings Plans and Regional RIs don't. Dedicated Hosts for per-core licences; Dedicated Instances add US$2 per hour per Region. Database Savings Plans (up to 35%) are new.
  • Load balancers: ALB and NLB about US$0.0225 per hour plus capacity units; share one ALB across services with path rules; public IPv4 addresses and NLB cross-zone traffic add cost; LCU reservations aren't a discount.
  • Scaling for cost: scale out and in, schedule non-production off (about 70% saving for a 50-hour week), use services that scale to zero. Hibernation keeps RAM on encrypted EBS, is enabled at launch, bills only EBS and Elastic IPs while stopped (but bills during stopping), and works in Auto Scaling through hibernated warm pools.
  • Services: Lambda costs nothing idle and wins for spiky or low traffic; Fargate and EC2 with Savings Plans win for steady load; ECS and Batch are free, EKS adds US$0.10 per cluster-hour; App Runner is closed to new customers. CloudFront Functions are far cheaper than Lambda@Edge.
  • Availability by class: Multi-AZ and always-on for production; single AZ, small, Spot and scheduled for development and test. Cross-AZ traffic costs US$0.01 per GB each way.
  • Family and size: match the CPU-to-memory shape; Graviton up to 20% cheaper; AMD 6a and T3a 10% cheaper (not every generation); T for low average CPU; each size doubles the price; right-size with Compute Optimizer; normalization factors keep RI discounts within a family.
