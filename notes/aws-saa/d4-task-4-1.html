Overview

Task 4.1

Design cost-optimized storage solutions.

Storage bills grow quietly: data is never deleted, volumes are sized for a peak that never came, backups pile up, and small objects generate request charges that dwarf their storage cost. This task asks for the design that meets the requirement at the lowest cost, which usually means matching each piece of data to how often it's read, how fast it must come back, and how long it must be kept. Domains 1 to 3 covered protection and performance; here the deciding factor is price.

Prices on this page are AWS's published us-east-1 (N. Virginia) on-demand rates, checked in November 2026. They change and vary by Region, so learn the relative order and the rules (minimum durations, minimum sizes, retrieval fees) rather than the exact cents.

Running example. The company stores customer uploads and product images in S3, application logs that must be kept seven years, millions of small thumbnail files, EBS volumes for a reporting server, an EFS file system shared by the web fleet, nightly backups, and a 400 TB archive of old video on premises that must move to AWS.

Seeing and controlling storage cost

The knowledge list for this task includes the cost management tools and billing features. They apply to all of Domain 4.

Finding where the money goes

Cost Explorer, Data Exports and Storage Lens

  • AWS Cost Explorer: free charts of cost and usage by service, account, tag or Region, with 13 months of history (up to 38 months if you opt in to multi-year data) and an 18-month forecast. Hourly and resource-level data for the past 14 days is an opt-in, paid option.
  • AWS Data Exports: delivers detailed billing data to S3 for analysis in Athena or Quick Sight. CUR 2.0 (Cost and Usage Report) is the recommended export; the legacy Cost and Usage Report still exists, and FOCUS-format and cost optimization recommendation exports are also available.
  • AWS Cost Anomaly Detection: machine learning that alerts when spend for a service, account or tag jumps unexpectedly.
  • S3 Storage Lens: organization-wide views of S3 usage and activity with cost recommendations (for example, incomplete multipart uploads older than 7 days); free metrics are queryable for 14 days, paid advanced metrics for 15 months.
  • Cost Optimization Hub and Compute Optimizer: recommendations such as deleting idle EBS volumes or downsizing over-provisioned ones, with savings that include your discounts.

Cost allocation tags and consolidated billing

Cost allocation tags let reports group cost by project, team or environment. There are AWS-generated tags (prefix aws:, such as aws:createdBy) and user-defined tags; both must be activated in the billing console of the management account (or a standalone account) before they appear, which can take up to 24 hours. They apply from activation onward, unless you request a backfill of up to 12 months. Consolidated billing in AWS Organizations gives one bill for all accounts and pools their usage, so volume pricing tiers (S3's cheaper rates above 50 TB, for example), Reserved Instance and Savings Plans discounts apply across the organization at no extra fee. AWS Billing Conductor produces adjusted, pro forma bills for showback and chargeback without changing the real invoice.

Activate the CostCenter tag for cost reports (AWS CLI, run in the management account) 
aws ce update-cost-allocation-tags-status \
    --cost-allocation-tags-status TagKey=CostCenter,Status=Active

Stopping surprises

AWS Budgets

AWS Budgets tracks cost, usage, Reserved Instance or Savings Plans utilization and coverage against a limit, daily, monthly, quarterly, yearly or for a custom period, and alerts by email or SNS on actual or forecasted spend. Budget actions can respond automatically or after approval: apply an IAM policy, apply an SCP to an account, or stop EC2 or RDS instances in the same account. Alert-only budgets are free; the first two action-enabled budgets are free and each further one costs US$0.10 a day.

Monthly cost budget for the data platform (budget.json for aws budgets create-budget; the alert at 80% of forecast goes in a separate notifications file) 
{
  "BudgetName": "data-platform-monthly",
  "BudgetLimit": { "Amount": "5000", "Unit": "USD" },
  "BudgetType": "COST",
  "TimeUnit": "MONTHLY"
}

"Visualize and forecast spend" points to Cost Explorer. "Alert when spend will exceed a limit", or "automatically restrict an account when it does", points to Budgets (with actions). "Most detailed line-item billing data for analysis" points to CUR 2.0 in Data Exports. "Charge costs back to departments" points to cost allocation tags (and Billing Conductor). "Unexpected spike" points to Cost Anomaly Detection.

A new cost allocation tag shows nothing in Cost Explorer until it's activated in the management account, and it doesn't cover earlier months unless you request a backfill. Tagging resources alone isn't enough.

Storage strategies

Skill 4.1.1Designing appropriate storage strategies (for example, batch uploads to Amazon S3 compared with individual uploads)

Requests cost money, per object

Why batching small objects pays

S3 charges per request as well as per GB: in S3 Standard, PUT, COPY, POST and LIST cost US$0.005 per 1,000 and GET US$0.0004 per 1,000; colder classes charge more per request, and every lifecycle transition is one request (US$0.01 per 1,000 into Standard-IA, up to US$0.05 per 1,000 into Glacier Deep Archive). Colder classes also have per-object minimums: Standard-IA, One Zone-IA and Glacier Instant Retrieval bill each object as at least 128 KB, and each object in Glacier Flexible Retrieval or Deep Archive carries 40 KB of extra metadata (8 KB of it billed at Standard rates). For small objects, these per-object costs can exceed the storage cost itself, which is why AWS advises aggregating small objects into larger ones before archiving.

Worked example: moving one million 10 KB thumbnails to Deep Archive costs US$50 in transition requests alone and adds about 38 GB of metadata overhead to roughly 10 GB of data. Bundling them into a single 10 GB archive file first costs a fraction of a cent to transition. The same logic applies to uploads: batching thousands of tiny writes into larger objects (or letting Firehose buffer them, Task 3.5) cuts PUT charges.

"After moving millions of small files to Glacier, the bill went up" is the per-object transition charge plus the 40 KB metadata overhead. Aggregate small files first, or leave them in S3 Standard or Intelligent-Tiering.

Who pays for access

Requester Pays buckets

When you share a large dataset with other organizations, Requester Pays makes the requester's account pay for requests and data downloaded, while the bucket owner keeps paying for storage. Every request must be authenticated (no anonymous access) and must include the x-amz-request-payer: requester header (--request-payer requester in the CLI). It's set per bucket by the owner.

Make the shared research dataset bucket Requester Pays (AWS CLI) 
aws s3api put-bucket-request-payment --bucket example-shared-dataset \
    --request-payment-configuration '{"Payer":"Requester"}'

"Share a large dataset, but the consumers should pay for their downloads" points to Requester Pays. "Public website assets downloaded worldwide" points to CloudFront in front of S3 (Skill 4.1.3).

Sizing storage

Skill 4.1.2Determining the correct storage size for a workload

Provisioned versus pay-per-use

EBS charges for what you provision

An EBS volume is billed for its full provisioned size whether it's full or empty, plus provisioned performance: gp3 costs US$0.08 per GB-month including 3,000 IOPS and 125 MiB/s, with extra IOPS at US$0.005 per IOPS-month and extra throughput at US$0.04 per MiB/s-month; gp2 costs US$0.10 per GB-month (gp3 is 20% cheaper per GB). Because gp3 performance is independent of size, you size gp3 for the data and buy only the performance you need, whereas on gp2 you often over-provision capacity to get IOPS. A volume can grow online but never shrink, so start close to the real need and grow with Elastic Volumes (Task 3.1). Compute Optimizer recommends volume type, size, IOPS and throughput from the last 14 days of use.

Worked example: the reporting server needs 500 GB and 6,000 IOPS. On gp2, 6,000 IOPS needs 2,000 GB (3 IOPS per GB): about US$200 a month. On gp3: 500 GB is US$40, plus 3,000 extra IOPS at US$15, so about US$55 a month.

Elastic storage needs no sizing

S3 and EFS bill only for data actually stored, so there's nothing to size and no wasted headroom. FSx file systems are provisioned: FSx for Windows storage can only grow (AWS suggests keeping about 20% free), and data deduplication typically saves 50% to 60% on general-purpose file shares; FSx for NetApp ONTAP keeps a small SSD tier and tiers cold data to an elastic capacity pool at a fraction of the price.

"gp2 volumes over-provisioned only to get IOPS" points to migrating to gp3 (online, with Elastic Volumes) and buying IOPS separately. "Idle or unattached EBS volumes" points to Cost Optimization Hub or Compute Optimizer findings, then snapshot and delete.

Lowest-cost data transfer

Skill 4.1.3Determining the lowest cost method of transferring data for a workload to AWS storage

What data transfer costs

The prices that drive the design

  • Into AWS from the internet or over Direct Connect: free.
  • Out to the internet: US$0.09 per GB for the first 10 TB a month, after a free 100 GB a month across all services. Over Direct Connect: US$0.02 per GB to a US location.
  • Between Regions from us-east-1: US$0.01 to US$0.02 per GB. Between AZs: US$0.01 per GB in each direction.
  • EC2 to S3 in the same Region: free. But through a NAT gateway, every GB costs US$0.045 in processing; an S3 gateway endpoint is free.
  • S3 to CloudFront: free, and CloudFront's always-free tier includes 1 TB out per month, with lower per-GB rates than S3 direct (US$0.085 per GB in North America).

Choosing the cheapest path in

For the 400 TB video archive: data in is free, so the costs are the service and the link. DataSync charges US$0.0125 per GB copied in Basic mode (about US$5,100 for 400 TB) plus S3 request charges, running over the existing internet link or a temporary Direct Connect. AWS Data Transfer Terminal charges per port-hour (US$300 in North America) with no per-GB fee, but you bring the devices to an AWS site and it's currently for Enterprise Support customers. Snowball is no longer available to new customers. Storage Gateway charges US$0.01 per GB written (capped at US$125 per gateway per month) for ongoing hybrid use. Transfer Family costs US$0.30 per hour per protocol for as long as the server exists, even when stopped, plus US$0.04 per GB.

Private instances uploading to S3 through a NAT gateway pay US$0.045 per GB for nothing. Add an S3 gateway endpoint. And a stopped Transfer Family server still bills by the hour; delete servers you don't use.

"Lowest cost to serve S3 content to users worldwide" points to CloudFront (free origin fetch, 1 TB free tier). "Large ongoing outbound transfers to the data center" points to Direct Connect (US$0.02 versus US$0.09 per GB).

When storage needs auto scaling

Skill 4.1.4Determining when storage auto scaling is required

Elastic services need none

Where capacity takes care of itself

S3, EFS, Aurora storage, DynamoDB, S3 Express One Zone, the FSx for NetApp ONTAP capacity pool and the FSx for Lustre and OpenZFS Intelligent-Tiering classes grow (and shrink) with the data, billed for what's stored. Choosing one of these is often the answer to "unpredictable growth, pay only for what's used".

Provisioned storage that can run out

RDS storage autoscaling

An RDS database has an allocated size; if it fills, the database stops accepting writes. RDS storage autoscaling, turned on by setting a maximum (--max-allocated-storage), adds storage automatically when free space is at or below 10% for at least 5 minutes, the previous change has finished and fewer than four storage changes happened in the last 24 hours; each increase is the largest of 10 GiB, 10% of the size, or the predicted growth over 7 hours. It never shrinks storage. Use it when growth is unpredictable and you don't want to over-allocate.

EBS and FSx

EBS volumes have no native auto scaling: increases are manual with Elastic Volumes, or scripted with a CloudWatch alarm and Lambda, and they can't shrink. FSx for Windows File Server storage can be increased by an AWS-provided automation triggered by low free capacity. For these, the cheapest choice is usually right-sizing now and growing when needed, not provisioning far ahead.

"The RDS database runs out of space unpredictably; least operational effort" points to RDS storage autoscaling. "Unknown future size, pay only for what's stored" points to S3 or EFS.

Managing S3 object lifecycles

Skill 4.1.5Managing S3 object lifecycles

Transition rules

Which way objects can move

S3 Lifecycle transition actions move objects to colder classes after a number of days; expiration actions delete them (Task 1.3 covered expiration, noncurrent versions and incomplete uploads). Objects only move downward:

  • S3 Standard can move to any colder class.
  • Standard-IA can move to Intelligent-Tiering, One Zone-IA or any Glacier class.
  • One Zone-IA can move only to Glacier Flexible Retrieval or Deep Archive (not Glacier Instant Retrieval).
  • Glacier Instant Retrieval can move to Flexible Retrieval or Deep Archive; Flexible Retrieval only to Deep Archive.
  • Deep Archive is the end of the line. Lifecycle never moves objects back to a warmer class; you restore and copy them yourself.

Costs and limits that shape the rule

  • Small objects: since September 2024, objects under 128 KB aren't transitioned to any class by default, because the transition request would cost more than it saves. Override with an ObjectSizeGreaterThan filter or the bucket's default minimum object size setting.
  • Minimum storage durations: 30 days for Standard-IA and One Zone-IA, 90 days for Glacier Instant Retrieval and Flexible Retrieval, 180 days for Deep Archive. Deleting or moving an object earlier still bills the remaining days, so a rule that moves to Glacier Instant Retrieval and then to Deep Archive four days later pays the Instant Retrieval minimum anyway.
  • Transition charges per 1,000 objects, from US$0.01 (to Standard-IA) to US$0.05 (to Deep Archive).

Older material says objects must stay 30 days in S3 Standard before a lifecycle rule can move them to Standard-IA or One Zone-IA. The current S3 documentation doesn't state that rule; it documents the 30-day minimum storage charge in those classes, which makes earlier moves pointless in any case.

Application logs: Standard-IA after 30 days, Glacier Flexible Retrieval after 90, Deep Archive after 180, delete after 7 years; skip objects under 128 KB (lifecycle JSON for aws s3api put-bucket-lifecycle-configuration) 
{
  "Rules": [
    {
      "ID": "tier-logs",
      "Status": "Enabled",
      "Filter": { "And": { "Prefix": "logs/", "ObjectSizeGreaterThan": 131072 } },
      "Transitions": [
        { "Days": 30, "StorageClass": "STANDARD_IA" },
        { "Days": 90, "StorageClass": "GLACIER" },
        { "Days": 180, "StorageClass": "DEEP_ARCHIVE" }
      ],
      "NoncurrentVersionTransitions": [
        { "NoncurrentDays": 30, "StorageClass": "GLACIER_IR" }
      ],
      "Expiration": { "Days": 2555 }
    }
  ]
}

Storage class names in the API are STANDARD_IA, ONEZONE_IA, INTELLIGENT_TIERING, GLACIER_IR, GLACIER (Flexible Retrieval) and DEEP_ARCHIVE.

Deciding what the rule should be

Storage Class Analysis and Intelligent-Tiering

Storage Class Analysis watches access patterns on a bucket, prefix or tag for 30 days or more and reports when data becomes infrequently accessed, which tells you when to transition to Standard-IA (it only analyses Standard to Standard-IA). When access patterns are unknown or change, S3 Intelligent-Tiering replaces the rule altogether: it moves each object between tiers by its own access history (Infrequent Access after 30 days without access, Archive Instant Access after 90), with no retrieval fees and no minimum duration, for a monitoring fee of US$0.0025 per 1,000 objects a month. Objects under 128 KB aren't monitored and stay at the Frequent Access price. Optional Archive Access (after at least 90 days) and Deep Archive Access (after at least 180) tiers are turned on per bucket and need a restore before reading.

Let rarely used images in Intelligent-Tiering also move to the archive tiers (configuration JSON for aws s3api put-bucket-intelligent-tiering-configuration) 
{
  "Id": "archive-old-images",
  "Status": "Enabled",
  "Filter": { "Prefix": "images/" },
  "Tierings": [
    { "Days": 90, "AccessTier": "ARCHIVE_ACCESS" },
    { "Days": 180, "AccessTier": "DEEP_ARCHIVE_ACCESS" }
  ]
}

"Known pattern: rarely read after a month, kept for years" points to lifecycle transitions (and expiration). "Unknown or changing access patterns, no retrieval fees" points to Intelligent-Tiering. "Find out when objects become infrequently accessed" points to Storage Class Analysis.

"The lifecycle rule doesn't move our small files" is the 128 KB default, not a broken rule. And Intelligent-Tiering saves nothing on a bucket of tiny objects: under 128 KB they're never tiered.

Backup and archival

Skill 4.1.6Selecting the appropriate backup and/or archival solution

Backup strategies at the right price

Retention drives cost

Backup cost is mostly how much you keep, for how long, and in which tier. Keep frequent short-term backups for operational recovery, fewer long-term ones for compliance, and copy only what DR needs to another Region (cross-Region copies add transfer and storage). Task 1.3 covered the mechanics of AWS Backup plans and locks; the cost levers are:

  • AWS Backup cold storage: lifecycle rules move backups to cold storage (for example EFS backups from US$0.05 to US$0.01 per GB-month). Backups must then stay at least 90 days in cold, so the retention period must be at least 90 days longer than the time before moving.
  • S3 backups can't go to cold storage, but a newer low-cost warm tier (US$0.035 instead of US$0.05 per GB-month) applies after at least 60 days.
  • EBS snapshots are incremental, at US$0.05 per GB-month. EBS Snapshots Archive costs US$0.0125 per GB-month (75% less) for snapshots kept 90 days or more, but each archived snapshot becomes a full copy and restoring takes up to 72 hours, so archive monthly or yearly snapshots, not daily incrementals.
  • Recycle Bin protects against accidental deletion, but resources in it keep billing until the retention period ends.

Archives

The cheapest places to keep data for years

  • S3 Glacier Deep Archive: US$0.00099 per GB-month (about US$1 per TB-month), 180-day minimum, restores within 12 hours (Standard) or 48 hours (Bulk). The cheapest storage on AWS.
  • S3 Glacier Flexible Retrieval: US$0.0036 per GB-month, 90-day minimum, restores in 1 to 5 minutes (Expedited, US$0.03 per GB), 3 to 5 hours (Standard) or 5 to 12 hours (Bulk, which is free).
  • S3 Glacier Instant Retrieval: US$0.004 per GB-month with millisecond access, but US$0.03 per GB to read, for archives read about once a quarter or less.
  • Tape Gateway: existing tape backup software writes virtual tapes that archive to Glacier Flexible Retrieval or Deep Archive (Task 3.1).

Closed to new customers. The original vault-based Amazon Glacier service no longer accepts new customers; new designs use the S3 Glacier storage classes above.

"Keep records for 7 to 10 years, retrieval within 48 hours is fine, lowest cost" points to Glacier Deep Archive. "Archive that must be readable in milliseconds a few times a year" points to Glacier Instant Retrieval. "Retrieval within minutes occasionally" points to Flexible Retrieval with Expedited. "Replace tape library, keep the backup software" points to Tape Gateway.

S3 Express One Zone (US$0.11 per GB-month, with much lower request prices since April 2025) is a performance class, not a cost class (Task 3.1).

Tiers in block and file storage

EBS SSD versus HDD

Per GB-month: io2 US$0.125 plus IOPS, gp3 US$0.08, gp2 US$0.10, st1 (throughput HDD) US$0.045 and sc1 (cold HDD) US$0.015. HDD volumes are much cheaper per GB for large, sequential, throughput-bound data (logs, data warehouses, rarely scanned data), but can't be boot volumes and handle small random I/O poorly. For anything transactional, gp3 is the cost-effective default.

EFS and FSx tiers

EFS Standard costs US$0.30 per GB-month, Infrequent Access US$0.016 with Elastic throughput (US$0.025 with other modes) and Archive US$0.008 (Elastic throughput and 90-day minimum), with per-GB charges to read colder tiers; One Zone file systems cost about half (US$0.16). FSx for Windows offers HDD storage at about a tenth of SSD's price, FSx for NetApp ONTAP tiers cold data to a capacity pool, and FSx for Lustre and OpenZFS offer an Intelligent-Tiering class priced like S3's.

"Infrequently accessed, but needed immediately when requested" points to Standard-IA (or Glacier Instant Retrieval if reads are rarer still). "Easily re-created data, lowest cost with millisecond access" points to One Zone-IA. "Large sequential log processing on EBS, lowest cost" points to st1.

Standard-IA isn't cheaper for data read often: at US$0.01 per GB retrieved, an object read every month costs about as much as keeping it in Standard. Glacier Instant Retrieval's US$0.03 per GB makes the break-even even sooner. Infrequent-access classes pay off only for data that really is infrequently read.

Choosing a data lifecycle

Skill 4.1.9Selecting the correct data lifecycle for storage

Start from the access pattern

Hot, warm, cold, gone

Most data is read heavily when new, occasionally for a while, then rarely or never, and eventually must be deleted. A good lifecycle follows that curve and the retention rules:

  1. HotS3 Standard, EFS Standard or gp3, while data is read often.
  2. WarmStandard-IA or EFS Infrequent Access once reads drop, after at least the class's minimum duration.
  3. ColdGlacier Instant Retrieval, Flexible Retrieval or Deep Archive (EFS Archive, EBS Snapshots Archive) for data kept for compliance or rare investigations.
  4. DeleteAn expiration rule when the retention period ends, so nothing is kept forever by accident.

For the running example: product images stay hot (Standard, served through CloudFront); customer uploads use Intelligent-Tiering because some are re-read for years and others never; application logs follow the lifecycle rule in Skill 4.1.5 to Deep Archive and expire after seven years; thumbnails, too small to tier, stay in Standard; the web fleet's EFS file system moves files to Infrequent Access 30 days after last access and to Archive after 90, the defaults for EFS lifecycle management.

Lifecycles outside S3

  • EFS lifecycle management: transitions after a chosen time since last access, and optionally back to Standard on first access.
  • Amazon Data Lifecycle Manager or AWS Backup plans: create EBS snapshots on a schedule and delete old ones, optionally archiving monthly or yearly snapshots.
  • CloudWatch Logs retention: log groups keep everything forever unless retention is set (Task 1.3).

"Data is accessed frequently for 30 days, rarely for a year, then must be kept for 7 years" points to a lifecycle rule: Standard, then Standard-IA at 30 days, then Glacier Flexible Retrieval or Deep Archive, then expiration. "Unpredictable access" points to Intelligent-Tiering.

The most cost-effective service

Skill 4.1.10Selecting the most cost-effective storage service for a workload

Choosing the service by type and price

Object, file or block, priced

The storage type (Task 2.1) narrows the choice; price decides between the candidates:

  • Amazon S3 is the cheapest place for almost anything that can be accessed as objects: US$0.023 per GB-month in Standard and as little as US$0.00099 in Deep Archive, paying only for what's stored.
  • Amazon EBS (gp3 US$0.08 per GB-month of provisioned size) for block storage attached to one instance.
  • Amazon EFS (US$0.30 per GB-month Standard, but US$0.016 or US$0.008 for cold files) for shared Linux file access, paying only for data stored; with lifecycle management, a mostly cold file system can cost less per GB than EBS.
  • Amazon FSx for Windows SMB (HDD from about US$0.013 per GB-month, plus throughput capacity), NetApp ONTAP or Lustre when those protocols or features are required.

Common cost-driven choices

  • Store files in S3 and serve them through CloudFront, rather than on EBS or EFS behind web servers.
  • Use gp3 rather than gp2 or io1 unless the workload needs io2's performance.
  • Use st1 or sc1 for big sequential data on EC2, and S3 when the application can read objects.
  • Use One Zone classes (S3 One Zone-IA, EFS One Zone) only for data that can be re-created.
  • Use FSx for Windows on HDD with deduplication for large general-purpose shares.

"Most cost-effective storage for static website assets" points to S3 (with CloudFront). "Shared storage for many Linux instances at the lowest cost" points to EFS with lifecycle to Infrequent Access (or One Zone if AZ resilience isn't needed). "Block storage for a database at the lowest cost" points to gp3.

Key takeaways

  • Tools: Cost Explorer (13 months history, 18-month forecast), Budgets (alerts on actual or forecast spend, actions: IAM policy, SCP, stop EC2 or RDS), CUR 2.0 in Data Exports for line items, Cost Anomaly Detection, cost allocation tags (activate in the management account; not retroactive without backfill), consolidated billing pools volume tiers and discounts.
  • Strategy: requests and transitions are charged per object, and cold classes add per-object minimums (128 KB; 40 KB metadata in Glacier Flexible Retrieval and Deep Archive), so batch small objects. Requester Pays makes consumers pay for requests and downloads.
  • Sizing: EBS bills provisioned size and performance (gp3 US$0.08 per GB-month with 3,000 IOPS included; 20% cheaper than gp2; never shrinks); S3 and EFS bill what's stored; FSx for Windows only grows, deduplication saves 50 to 60%.
  • Transfer: inbound is free; internet out US$0.09 per GB, Direct Connect US$0.02; S3 to CloudFront free; NAT gateway US$0.045 per GB (use a gateway endpoint); DataSync US$0.0125 per GB; Transfer Family bills hourly until deleted; Snowball is gone for new customers.
  • Auto scaling: S3, EFS, Aurora and DynamoDB are elastic; RDS storage autoscaling grows at 10% free (never shrinks); EBS and FSx need manual or scripted growth.
  • Lifecycle: transitions go only downward; objects under 128 KB don't transition by default; minimum durations 30, 90 and 180 days; transition charges US$0.01 to US$0.05 per 1,000; Storage Class Analysis guides the rule; Intelligent-Tiering for unknown patterns (no retrieval fees, monitoring fee, small objects untiered).
  • Backup and archive: AWS Backup cold storage (90-day minimum; not for S3, which has a 60-day low-cost warm tier); EBS Snapshots Archive for long-kept snapshots only; Deep Archive about US$1 per TB-month; Flexible Retrieval Bulk restores are free; Tape Gateway for tape replacement.
  • Tiers and services: Standard US$0.023, Standard-IA US$0.0125, One Zone-IA US$0.01, Glacier Instant Retrieval US$0.004, Flexible Retrieval US$0.0036, Deep Archive US$0.00099 per GB-month; st1 and sc1 for cheap sequential block storage; EFS Infrequent Access and Archive; S3 plus CloudFront for anything served to users.
